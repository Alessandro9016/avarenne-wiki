Chapter 41 — Three Exits

Sybille Kest counted three exits and ruled out two.

The window overlooked the courtyard, but a recently fitted grille narrowed its sill. The door beside the stove opened into a windowless storeroom. The main stairs behind her remained the only convenient way to take out a man who did not wish to cooperate.

For herself, perhaps, she could use the stretch of shadow beneath the stairs. That was not enough to make her like the room.

Mael Vaur indicated the chair.

“It isn’t nailed down.”

“The chair doesn’t worry me.”

He went on clearing the desk. He had moved three restored objects to make room for a package no one would gladly have restored: an ordinary component, badly wrapped in cloth too good for it.

Sybille sat where she could see the door. The room above the depot smelled of dry paper and wood soap. From downstairs came the thumps of the last delivery being unloaded. Every so often Mael’s glass trembled against its saucer.

“It came back?” she asked.

“Exactly what we sent.”

“Wasn’t that what you wanted?”

Mael handed her the piece.

The central curve resembled a hinge only if someone already knew what to look for. The inner surfaces had irregularities a hand recognized more easily than the eyes. Sybille turned it. One point pressed against her fingertip.

“It opens nothing,” she said.

“No.”

“Then they haven’t taken anything from us.”

Mael smiled as though he appreciated a good joke. He took the component back and laid it on the cloth. Beside his elbow he had left an unpaid bill.

“They realized the text wasn’t the inspector’s. They had the versions compared.”

“I told you a signature wouldn’t be enough.”

“I never said it would.”

Sybille leaned forward far enough to read the beginning of the commercial response. She could see the refusals clearly. The requests remained elsewhere, in sheets Mael kept on his side of the desk.

They had had a simple opportunity: bring the customer into the work, pay for a demonstration, leave with an object and a name to contact again. Ruy had refused. The draftsman had taken the documents to someone capable of recognizing them.

“Ilian?”

“He has yet to finish collecting his payment.”

“He finished the drawing.”

Mael rested a hand on the bill.

“We need to establish what the client considers delivered.”

Sybille looked at him. It was the sentence meant to discourage her from discussing an outstanding payment in front of him. He used it even when he was the client himself.

“A man can come back for his money and bring you an officer instead of a technician.”

“He can.”

No irritation. Mael considered objections of that kind only if she offered them as problems with the work. Sybille still could not tell how much of his calm was habit and how much pleasure at making her choose her tone.

She got up and went to the window. The courtyard was already almost dark. A porter had stopped to rub his neck before going back into the depot. Neither of the two men waiting for him told him to hurry; one simply took the load from his hands.

Sybille recognized the moment when she could have vanished from both men’s sight without creating a great darkness. She would merely have had to stop offering their gaze the signal it sought. She did not. The dirty glass reflected a little of the room behind her too, and she wanted to keep seeing it.

“You didn’t call me here about the bill.”

Mael pushed a sheet into the cleared space.

A plan. Not of a house. A stretch of street, the entrance to a depot and the room where the new work would be proposed.

“This time we’re only asking to have a checking collar measured.”

“Only.”

“A measurement. The object stays ordinary. It needn’t open a passage.”

Sybille read the note. It did not say to bring a working hinge. A technician could accept the visit without feeling he was being asked to sell everything he knew.

“And when he arrives?”

“We see what he knows how to measure.”

She raised her eyes.

Mael had not answered the question he had heard. For an instant the unloading stopped and the depot seemed empty. Sybille heard a wheel drag over stone; then a man called out, filling all the space the silence had left.

She remembered another gate, a departure passed off as a late delivery. She had kept the noise low on the hinge while two people crossed the yard. One was walking with the other’s help. She had not asked why his wrists were so tightly covered.

She had been paid for the stretch between the gate and the street.

Some of that money was still part of the money for the house she wanted to buy.

“I’m not good at arguing with whoever gets brought inside,” she said.

“You’re good at keeping the alley from knowing.”

Sybille sat again. The chair leg scraped, giving her a brief, childish pleasure. Mael merely looked up from the sheet.

“This can be fixed,” she said.

She pressed two fingers against the wood, reached the vibration through the contact and pushed the chair a handspan. It moved without the earlier complaint. From below came the porter’s noise again: she had not silenced the depot.

When she took her hand away and shifted her foot, the wood scraped once more.

Mael looked at the place on the floor.

“Will that be enough for the door?”

“For the noise of that door, if I can work on it. Not for everything that might happen on the other side.”

“I’m not asking you to silence the neighborhood.”

“Asking for less doesn’t make it easier to hold the right point.”

He picked up a pen. Sybille recognized the beginning of a cost item. Her skill became more expensive when they let her describe what it did not include; less safe when the client seemed too pleased to receive a short answer.

“I want to see the place empty first,” she added.

Mael looked at her as though the condition were already written down.

“You can see it now.”

The test room was downstairs, beyond a wooden partition that retained the color of an old warehouse. It was not a prison. Sybille recognized precisely what would let them use it without making it look like one: a bench, tools, two chairs. Someone could enter expecting work.

A lamp lit the middle of the bench. Mael moved it to show her a corner.

“We keep the light here.”

“If you want me working on shadows, you need to stop moving it first.”

He left the lamp where she indicated. Sybille stood at the edge of the darker area and looked at the partition, the floor, the stretch beneath the bench. Darkness did not offer her a destination merely because it would be useful. She needed continuity, something she could actually reach.

Mael waited.

“Can you see the bench from the door?” she asked.

He went to check. As he turned, he gave her a moment when no one was looking at her directly. Sybille damped the sound of her steps and moved against the partition. The edge of the bench brushed her skirt; she went around it without losing sight of the door. When Mael looked for her again, she was already where the light ended.

“From there?”

“From here I can reach the door. I want the bench far enough away that it doesn’t cut off my route.”

He considered the space. Then he pushed one of the chairs beneath the table.

The gesture made her see a man seated there. A technician busy looking at a part, his open folder beside him. Sybille did not know his face and realized she wanted to give him an unpleasant one.

“We don’t have anyone who has accepted yet,” she said.

“That’s why we prepare.”

Mael returned to the door. Sybille stayed in the room a moment longer, long enough to feel how easily she could cover part of a noise and how little that ability changed what someone decided to make happen there.

She followed him upstairs without asking who would question the technician. Asking that too would make it harder to call her own work a separate service.

In the upper room Mael had left a second folder open. He closed it before picking up the visit request. Sybille saw only the lines of a table and a space for a signature.

He had her check her assignment. A prepared support, access to the site, attendance required only on a date still to be agreed. No indefinite availability.

“Payment first,” she said.

Mael pointed to a line.

“Some first. The rest when the service is complete.”

“Ilian is still on the second half.”

He lifted his finger off the paper.

It was the first silence that evening Sybille felt belonged entirely to her. She could have taken the sentence back with a joke. Instead she left the sheet on the bench and waited.

Mael asked her to specify when she wanted the initial payment. He did not raise his voice or promise money he was already counting. Sybille spelled out the conditions one by one, until the assignment no longer obliged her to rely solely on her memory of the conversation.

By the time they finished, the glass had stopped trembling. The depot had finished unloading.

A couple’s footsteps sounded in the street. The windows opposite were lit; in one, a woman was moving a flowerpot to close the shutters. Sybille looked at the gap between the pot and the curtain. In her own house there would be room for both, and no one would ask her to keep a room available for a future assignment.

Sybille folded her copy and put it in her inner pocket. The edge met another, thinner sheet: the particulars of a house she had visited twice. On the second visit, the owner had mostly talked about the first offer someone else had made.

She kept the two papers apart with a finger.

“If the date changes, tell me before you make arrangements with the others.”

“Do you have another job?”

“I might.”

Mael’s eyes dropped to the pocket. He did not try to find out what it was. Sybille would have preferred him to ask outright instead of leaving her uncertain whether she had answered a question about her price.

“Confirmation through the usual contact,” he said.

She checked the address in the margin. It was where she could receive a request without having someone come to the place she slept. After months of working with Mael, none of his promises seemed more important than that distance.

A noise from below made them both turn. The last porter closed the depot door from inside and headed for the courtyard exit. Sybille followed his footsteps until she heard his voice in the street. The test room had stayed empty as she had asked. For that evening, at least, she could leave without checking whether Mael had already changed the job.

Mael handed her the coat she had left on the arm of the chair.

“When the draftsman comes back, we’ll have a question he can answer.”

Sybille put on a sleeve. The cloth caught a finger; she had to free it before reaching the door.

“Don’t let me find a person where you promised me an empty bench.”

“I’ll tell you first.”

She went down the stairs, counting the steps. In the courtyard she turned to look at the window. From outside, the grille looked like good protection against thieves.
