Chapter 74 — Service Water

The first man Elias met at the Mutual did not want to see a pump. He wanted to get through with a basket of laundry and considered the surveyor, standing in the worst part of the doorway, to have chosen a rather useless trade.

“Excuse me.”

Elias moved aside. The man passed without recognizing him and asked someone whether the shelf near the water was finally clear. A voice farther in answered that he could leave the basket, but not there. It was an argument about where things belonged, identical to a hundred others. The pump worked behind the wall, and nobody had interrupted a sentence to listen to it.

Elias had imagined arriving with the finished instructions and finding a little anticipation. Not a ceremony; enough, though, to show that this morning completed a job. He had to wait for a second basket to pass before he could enter.

Gaston greeted him by raising a hand from the log. “Have you brought the new page?”

“And the replacement reference.”

“Then come this way. We're not taking the old one down until we've put up the other.”

The board bearing the first instructions still hung beside the entrance. The paper had a bent corner, a finger stain, and a correction not in Elias's handwriting. He followed the mark. Someone had written where the drain container actually stood instead of where they had planned to keep it.

It was not an elegant correction. It was accurate.

The new page retained the directions they had been using since June seventh and added the results of the test with the local replacement. The part had not been tested by dismantling the machine in service. Elias showed Gaston the number two recorded on the sheet; the man checked the number one on the casing, as though determined to prevent the two tests from becoming confused on paper too.

“Does the part fit here the same way?”

“The seat is the same. This is the drawing of the seat, and this is the material reference. The test was done on the workshop bench.”

Gaston followed the line with a finger, then asked for the wrench specified in the equipment list. It had arrived in an ordinary case along with the sheets. He took it out, compared it with the profile, and returned it to the case beside the machine's tools. Nobody needed to open the cover to prove they knew how.

Water rose through the service section. At the tank, a worker filled the next container and slid it toward a woman, who lifted it from a comfortable height. She had not climbed the stairs carrying that weight. Elias looked at the floor, expecting to find a trace where the jet still returned to his memory. The floor was dirty with work, not flooded.

Noemi arrived with a folder and a handkerchief tucked into her cuff. “Have you found something to reopen already?”

“Not yet.”

“At least wait until the handover is finished.”

She stopped beside Gaston and read the page's heading. She had trimmed the blank edge of the old board because a passing sleeve kept catching on it. Elias noticed only when he laid the larger sheet beside it.

“The new one is too wide,” she said.

“I can fold it.”

“Not over the name of the replacement.”

Gaston found a wider support among two boards saved for another job. He leaned it against the wall and showed where it would need shortening. The worker at the tank finished filling and came to fetch it. He did not look to Elias for confirmation. He had a saw, a bench, and enough annoyance with the old board to want the problem fixed at once.

Meanwhile Elias went over the final scheduled check with Gaston. They were not looking for an accident. The level was the one required for service, the return followed the repaired route, the supports remained stable. The point where the old pipe had failed was visible. The repair had not erased the other joints, but neither had every sound in the room become a warning.

A man pushed a crate into the corridor and left it against the access guard. Before Elias could open his mouth, the tank worker called him back.

“I can't get to the tools there.”

“It's only for a moment.”

“The moment fits on the other side too.”

He indicated a free space. The man moved the crate with a grumble, without asking who had given him that authority. Elias followed the movement and felt an intervention he was already preparing taken away. Then Gaston put the log before him and asked him to check a line. He did not have to invent something else to do.

The line recorded two level readings taken before and after filling the containers the previous day. The times were noted, the worker's signature readable. Elias asked whether they had noticed differences in the sound and received a concrete description of the two moments. There had been no miracle of a machine that prevented every doubt: someone had listened while it worked and written down what they saw, without having to interrupt service.

Noemi then pointed to two columns on the handover sheet. “This one is for what we're receiving today. Is this one about subsequent service?”

“The contact and work to be agreed. It doesn't mean every future intervention is already included.”

“I'm asking so nobody writes repair instead of request. We lost a week on a prosthesis order over a word like that.”

She corrected the heading in larger handwriting than his. She did not ask for a promise that would commit Yselle without her being there. She wanted to know which door to knock on if the work returned.

The new board arrived smelling of fresh-cut wood. The worker had rounded the corner that projected into the passage. Gaston held it steady, Noemi chose the height, and Elias attached the page. The material sample remained protected in a separate envelope, its reference readable, rather than hung above the tank to get wet.

They stood before the work a moment. Elias searched for a sentence resembling the right conclusion and found none that Noemi would not have been able to tease him about. She saved him by asking him to move because the woman with the container was coming back.

The woman set it down, looked at the water, and said they had begun washing earlier than usual. She knew nothing about dimensions or securities. She was angry because the laundry table was not ready yet, and glad she could be angry with a bucket already full.

Elias asked how long it had taken them before. She told him to try making two trips with an empty container, then imagine it full. He did not need to turn the answer into a number to believe her.

The woman lifted a corner from the basket. It had caught in the handle beneath the rest of the laundry; if she pulled, she would overturn the container just filled. She asked Elias to hold it steady. He slipped two fingers beneath the wood and left both her hands free. The knot of fabric came out slowly, wetting his cuff.

“This wasn't in the handover,” she said.

“I can add it to the sheet.”

“Don't. Then tomorrow they'll think you're coming.”

She showed him the space beneath the bench where they would put the baskets once it was cleared of the containers previously used to carry water upstairs. She had already moved two. Two paler rectangles remained on the floor, along with a button nobody had bent to collect. The woman picked it up and put it in her apron pocket.

Elias released the handle once she had arranged the cloth. He had thought finished work was recognizable by a motionless machine and a sheet without corrections. That morning nothing was motionless: laundry, baskets, people, even the objects that had lost their usefulness were finding a place. The woman asked him not to leave his folder on the shelf she had just cleared. He picked it up without explaining that he would not have kept it there long.

In a dry corner, on a different shelf, the drinking jug remained. Nobody filled it from the service tank. The distinction belonged to the room's habits; the new machine had not changed the water into something it was not.

Noemi took him to the office table for the signatures. The room had a window open to the courtyard, and a draft immediately tried to carry off the first sheet. Elias set the weight too far from the corner; Noemi moved it before handing him the pen.

“You know they'll have to find you another job now?”

“Marcel is probably thinking about it already.”

“I meant the pump. We can't keep having you check the same screw to keep you here.”

He laughed, then looked at the form. He had found the service already running. The need for which they had measured, argued, and transported machines had truly been met. It might seem small beside what the duke had made appear. On the sheet, though, that small thing was enough to be handed over.

He signed for the check performed. Noemi signed for receipt of the sheets and equipment, and Gaston added his confirmation. Nobody turned those signatures into certification of drinking water or the entire city. The room, the service, and the machine had boundaries Elias knew.

While the ink dried, a man appeared in the doorway. He did not enter. He asked Noemi whether she had news about the visits they had discussed.

“Not a date yet,” she answered. “If you want, when I have one I'll have you called on your own. You don't have to come with the others.”

The man nodded, looked at Elias, and seemed about to add a question. Noemi waited. In the end he merely said to leave a message at the address she knew, then went away.

“He didn't want to ask in front of me,” Elias said.

“Or he didn't want to ask today.”

Noemi picked up the sheet again and showed him where to put his provisional copy. On her right forearm, where the old fastening had cut her, there was no mark where Elias still expected one. The healing had remained true even when nobody discussed it.

“Have you written to the duke already?”

“I gave him the questions before he left. Now I'm waiting for him to tell me how to proceed. Meanwhile I can ask people whether they want to be there.”

She did not say it like someone awaiting permission to return to life. She still had a correspondence box to open, two people to receive, and a new board to stop someone covering with an apron. Elias let her return to those things without offering to bring an answer he did not have.

Passing the machine again, he heard the worker explain to his colleague where to find the offset wrench. The man opened the case and showed him. Their voices continued over the pump's noise: a brief argument about who had put it back the wrong way around. It had become theirs enough to be disordered by hands that did not belong to the designer.

At the workshop Yselle received the original handover record and read the signatures before asking about the level. Elias told her about the moved crate, the board, and the service he had found already running. She checked Noemi's additions and placed the sheet in the project folder.

“I'll keep your final copy here,” she said. “Come and collect it when it's back from comparison.”

Elias nodded. That copy would belong to him without his having to remain attached to the machine to deserve it. He set the now-lighter folder on the bench and realized he had brought nothing away to repair. For once, that was exactly what he was supposed to deliver.
