Chapter 56 — The People Who Are Missing

Noemi searched for the wound with her thumb while she counted plates.

At seventeen she realized she had made a mistake. She started again, keeping both hands on the table this time. The cut was gone; searching for it would not make it easier to understand. Eighteen plates, two with chipped rims that must not go to anyone who had trouble keeping a spoon steady. Four bowls were still in the other room. The girl helping in the kitchen brought three and said the last had fallen.

“Did it break?”

“No. It was empty.”

“Empty bowls break too.”

The girl went back for it. Noemi lined up the plates and found herself smiling, irritated with herself. There was nothing to laugh about. They had flooded a room, frightened people, and delayed everything; the salt for the soup had ended up on the wrong shelf, and a man had been waiting since before the test to pick up a new strap.

And she kept touching a healed arm.

She took the strap into the waiting room. The man had placed his prosthesis beside his chair, wrapping its end in his handkerchief so the wood would not touch the wet floor. He looked straight at Noemi's shoes.

“Did you find the river under the house?”

“Part of it. We're leaving the rest where it is.”

She gave him the strap. He folded it, tried the hole, checked its edge with his nail. That was what he had come for. Noemi had known for years how he took his time before saying whether something was right: first a grimace, then another inspection, finally a hand into his pocket for the receipt. Today he only reached the second inspection.

“They say the duke healed you.”

The sentence traveled across the room and reached the woman by the window too. Noemi did not need to turn to know that the woman had stopped running a cord between her fingers.

“I had a cut on my arm. Now it's gone.”

“Let me see.”

Noemi raised her sleeve far enough. The man leaned forward, squinted, and then rested against the chair back.

“There should be a scar.”

“I know.”

“How deep was it?”

She showed him with two fingers, without turning her arm into a medical report. He looked at the strap in his hand. By the window, the cord began to move again, quickly.

“And this?” he asked, touching the handkerchief around his prosthesis.

Noemi had dreaded the question through the whole plate count. Hearing it spoken made it less immense and more difficult: it was not about the people of the Mutual, in the plural, but this man who had to cross two streets to get home.

“I asked whether we could discuss old injuries too. He said yes. I'll bring him some questions.”

“If I bring him my leg?”

“Not today. First I'll ask how to arrange a visit.”

The woman by the window let the cord fall into her lap. “And you'll line us all up? With people watching?”

Noemi turned. The woman held one rigid hand against her stomach; with the other she gathered up the cord as soon as she realized she had drawn attention. She had once argued with a visitor who called her brave, and that visitor had had nothing more to say when she asked him for a cup with a wide handle.

“I haven't lined anyone up,” Noemi replied.

“It always starts with a list.”

The man snorted. “If he gives me my leg back, he can put me in the square.”

“Not me.”

Noemi took a chair and turned it so the woman would not have to lift her chin to answer her. She wanted to explain at once that this was not what she intended. Instead she left the chair empty and went to close the door on two people listening in the corridor.

“We don't need the square,” she said when she returned. “And anyone who wants to watch will have to settle for the pump.”

The woman lowered her eyes. “I didn't ask you to have me examined.”

“I know.”

The man clicked his tongue, but this time he did not speak. He placed the strap against his prosthesis and began threading it himself. The task occupied both his hands and gave the two women room to be quiet.

Noemi returned to the table without securing general agreement. She had not needed it to deliver his strap; she would not obtain it for so vast a possibility before she even knew how to discuss it. In the day's ledger she recorded only that the replacement had been collected. On a separate sheet, beneath the date, she wrote: individual visits, location.

The words were meager beside what she wanted to ask. She crossed them out and rewrote the whole sentence. Where can you meet people without having to gather them all here?

Someone knocked on the already open door. It was the cook, holding a ladle that should not have left the kitchen.

“Time to eat. Or shall we find out whether he can work miracles on the soup too?”

“That has been beyond recovery for years.”

“It's good today.”

“Then you've been replaced.”

He passed her the ladle. “Taste it and apologize.”

Noemi tasted it. It really was good, though too thick for some of the men. She found herself giving instructions to add broth, looking for salt, and sending a portion into the room where two workers were finishing the drying. The relief those tasks gave her almost made her angry: she should not have needed soup to know that the afternoon still existed.

She sat with the others once everyone had a bowl. Her usual place near the door was occupied by a woman who had arrived late; she took the one opposite the window. The change forced anyone who wanted to ask her something to wait until she looked their way.

A young man held his spoon with a band adapted to his wrist. Every time he raised it, the edge tilted a little too far, spilling soup onto his napkin. He had learned the movement and still hated it. He said he would return the next day to watch the pump test.

“For the water or the duke?” the cook asked.

“So I don't have to carry buckets anymore.”

“Then you can come even when the duke isn't here.”

“That's what I'm hoping.”

Noemi watched him set the spoon back on the rim. The wish for a whole hand and the wish for less exhausting work could inhabit the same person without either making the other false. She wanted to give him both. The thought did not make her capable of doing it alone.

After the meal, the woman from the waiting room stopped her beside the storeroom. She held her cup by its wide handle.

“I wasn't angry with you.”

“A little.”

“A little.” The woman looked at Noemi's arm. “What was it like?”

Noemi could have said warmth, light, tingling. There had been no light; and the little warmth she remembered would not explain the moment when she had searched for pain and failed to find it.

“He held my wrist. Then I moved my hand, and it didn't hurt anymore.”

“That fast?”

“That fast.”

The woman ran her thumb along the cup handle. “Ask him whether we can talk without starting right away.”

“I'll write that down.”

“Not my name.”

“The question.”

The woman nodded and returned to the main room. Noemi waited to hear her voice, busy arguing with the cook about the window letting in cold air, before taking up the sheet again. She did not write the name. She wrote the whole question, and this time the words did not seem too small.

Her prosthesis was bothering her. Water had reached a spot that usually stayed dry; she would need to check it before going home. The thought that she might perhaps no longer have to do that struck her as she closed the inkwell. She sat down again, slowly.

She had never loved that weight simply because it allowed her to walk. She had been grateful to it, cursed it, learned sounds another person would not have heard. She could know it intimately and want it to end. For years that wish had seemed like a locked room against which it was not worth spending her fingernails. Now someone had said a single word, and the door was no longer in the same place.

She would have to ask for herself too.

The pen hovered. Outside, someone was looking for a clean cloth; Noemi knew where they were, but waited for someone else to find one. She wrote a third question. It was not about an organization, an address, or a time. It concerned her closely enough that she folded the sheet before finishing.

She opened it again. Alexander was not there to watch what she wrote. Nobody was asking her to decide by evening. She completed the sentence, dried the ink, and put down the pen.

When the cook came in, she was checking the list of people who had not come that day. Two had sent word that they would stop by tomorrow. One needed someone to deliver materials for a routine adjustment at home. Another had not answered the last message.

“Shall we go and see her?” the cook asked, indicating that line.

“Tomorrow. Today we'll send someone to ask whether she'd like company.”

He nodded, took the delivery sheet, and left the other one alone, folded beside the inkwell. Noemi was grateful. If all those people were healed, she would still want to know which had failed to turn up for a meal, which had found work, who did not want visitors. But she would not keep anyone bound to a prosthesis so she could continue asking.

She stood and went to the water room. The repaired pipe ran above the shield, still in place. The new metal reflected cold light; the old joint remained wrapped on the bench with its fracture exposed. She would have to have that bench moved before morning to leave more room for the operators.

The young man with the spoon caught up with her carrying two bowls. He had fitted one inside the other and held them against his chest.

“Where do these go?”

“The kitchen. You don't need to bring them to me here.”

“I was looking for you.”

Noemi took the top bowl; the other came free without forcing him to change his grip. She waited for him to continue.

“If the duke doesn't come tomorrow, will you still do the test?”

“The machine works even when he leaves the room.”

“I want to see it then.”

“You've already said that.”

“Not quite.”

She understood that he was not asking about water. He had seen something extraordinary entrusted to a person who could leave, and wanted to know whether there would still be room for him in front of the result. Noemi told him to arrive before the test began and leave her a chair beside the dry wall. He asked whether it was for her or for him. She said they would argue about it when the time came. The young man headed back toward the kitchen with his own bowl and left the other on the bench.

Noemi bent to pick up the pink and gray cloth she had left there. It was already drying. She thought of throwing it in with the laundry, then stopped, irritated by her own need to keep it as evidence.

The evidence was the arm moving without pain.

She put the cloth in the basket. She went back for the sheet of questions and slipped it into an envelope. Before sealing it, she added that she would prefer to meet him when they did not have a flooded room around them.

That request finally made her laugh without fear.
