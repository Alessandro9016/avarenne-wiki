Chapter 71 — The Person to Be Measured

Ilian had prepared a sentence and forgot it when Mael offered him a seat. On that side of the desk lay his old receipt, folded along the line showing the outstanding balance. Seeing it there, ready, robbed him of the pleasure of laying down his own copy as a surprise.

“First we discuss this,” he said, and the prepared sentence returned uselessly to his head.

Mael moved a brass weight beside the paper. “The account is still open.”

“The work is finished.”

He added no threat. He was forty-nine and carried an apprentice draftsman's folder; he knew the sound of someone who could not afford to walk away as much as he wanted. He placed his own copy beside the other. The same numbers, the same work delivered. No dimension had become wrong while waiting.

Mael ran a finger along the margin, not the figure. “That isn't in dispute.”

“Then tell me when it's being paid.”

The small room received the sounds of the warehouse without revealing all of it. Someone slid a crate past the inner door. Ilian waited for the noise to end, and Mael let him wait. It was a civil gesture he would have liked in a different meeting.

“Not today,” Mael said at last.

Ilian felt something retreat from his face. It was not surprise. He wished it were, because then he could have believed he had not foreseen the answer. He closed the folder clasp too firmly and had to reopen it to free his trapped sleeve.

“The new work doesn't include the old.”

“Nobody is asking you to give it away.”

“You're asking me to continue before I'm paid.”

Mael looked at the two receipts. “I'm asking you to take a question. The answer will determine whether there is a job.”

Ilian drew his own copy toward him. That, at least, he could do. He returned it to the folder and left out only the sheet about the collar. He would not give Mael the satisfaction of watching him leave with all the papers still needing to be distinguished.

The drawing occupied half a page. An outer circle, an inner one, a fastening recess, and a section nobody had dimensioned for him. Nothing about the part required a portable hinge. The smaller request he had taken to Livia remained just that: comparison of the collar, not delivery of a device. But the lines beneath the drawing grew every time someone looked at it.

“You've added available with operator here,” he said.

“It's an option useful to the recipient.”

“It isn't a measurement.”

Mael did not answer at once. Ilian did not talk over his silence; he waited for a real answer, and for a few moments the room seemed to belong to the person who had not yet explained.

“The recipient wants to know whether the comparison can be performed on site,” Mael said.

Ilian drew a light line beside the phrase. It could mean that. It could mean that the person taking measurements must then accompany a specimen elsewhere. A craftsperson had the right to ask where he would work, not merely how wide the part he would measure was.

“Whose site?”

“The owner's.”

“Who must authorize access.”

“Naturally.”

The ease with which Mael agreed did not supply a name. Ilian straightened the sheet. He had learned drawing late enough to retain a memory of what it meant not to know how to read a section. That was ignorance one could remedy. Not knowing who ought to open a door was a different thing.

“For an estimate I'll need the location. For a visit, the name of whoever can authorize it. And I want to know whether they want a check of the collar or an opinion on the whole device.”

Mael took a second sheet. He did not hand it over. He read it on his own side of the desk, then copied two lines onto a slip: dimensional check and compatibility of the ordinary part; no test of the complete device.

It was a better answer than the first. Ilian read it and did not feel any better himself.

“Can you sign that?”

Mael looked at him. “It's a preliminary question.”

“Exactly. It can't commit you as much as an order.”

The signature was brief. Mael set down the pen so it would not roll. Ilian let the slip dry and clipped it to the drawing. He would take those words, not the sheet Mael had read without showing him.

Ilian returned to the incomplete section. The inner line ended before reaching the fastener; it might have been a choice by whoever copied the part, or the outline of a recess. He marked it with the point without widening it.

“Is this supposed to remain empty?”

Mael looked at the spot. “I don't have the sample here.”

“I'm not asking you to guess. I'm telling you why someone will have to see it.”

Working from the drawing cost something different from taking dimensions from an actual example. Ilian had discussed that the first time, yet the small gap survived all the new sheets. It happened often: people who could not draw considered an interrupted line a space into which the technician ought to put his intelligence. Ilian put a question mark there. The difference had cost him at least one order and saved him from paying for another out of his own pocket.

He turned the page and sketched two possible sections. In one the collar rested against a continuous shoulder; in the other the fastening reached a recessed part. Mael indicated the first, then withdrew his finger.

“I think it's this.”

Ilian wrote to be checked beneath both. “Resemblance isn't enough for me.”

Mael's pen remained still. He did not seem offended by the correction, and Ilian was grateful for those few seconds. It was easier to talk about metal. Metal did not imply that asking for what one was owed was a failure of trust.

Outside the window a carter shouted for someone to move an obstruction. Mael stood, looked into the yard, and closed the window. The noise diminished enough for the pen cap to be heard against the desk. Ilian realized he had not yet named the technician.

“I was thinking of Veyne,” he said.

“Is he available?”

“I haven't asked.”

“He works with the workshop.”

“That doesn't mean he works for me.”

Mael sat again. Ilian remembered his question to Livia: was Veyne working again? He had not asked it to prepare a chain of compulsory favors. He had wanted to know whether that young man had kept his trade. Now it was useful to know, and the coincidence bothered him.

“If he can't, ask someone else.”

“If he can't, I'll report that he can't. I don't have a list of people at your disposal.”

The sentence risked sounding braver than necessary. Ilian left it without a friendly addition. He had one person to whom he could take a sufficiently precise question; he did not want to start distributing it all over Lethorne like a request for technical charity.

Mael pushed the now-dry slip toward him. “Then take it to him.”

“I'm not promising a visit.”

“Nor am I arranging one for you.”

Ilian looked at the drawing. The collar still had its simple, almost offensive shape. He could have said the place gave him a bad feeling and refused everything. Instead he had an unpaid receipt, a name on a sheet, and no proof that the new question was unlawful. Demanding that every job arrive free of its commissioner's character would have left him very little to draw.

“A technical answer will require technical access,” he said. “I can carry the question. I'm not selling anyone's access.”

“You've already made that clear.”

Ilian gathered the papers. He would have preferred an argument: it left the body something to do. Mael's polite farewell forced him to leave at the same pace at which he had entered. He did not know whether he had been respected or merely allowed to speak.

In the courtyard he stopped to arrange the slip. The crate he had heard moving contained machine parts secured with battens. There were no secrets to recognize in what he could see, only someone's poor work in leaving a nail projecting at hand height. Ilian pointed it out to a porter. The man bent it over with the hammer at his belt and returned to his load.

The response calmed him more than the interview. Something pointed out had been corrected. He crossed the gateway without knowing what he ought to have pointed out inside the room.

The street was hot, full of the sound of wheels on paving stones. He stopped before a shopwindow to check that the folder had not opened. Inside, the old receipt and the new request were separated by a blank sheet. The separation seemed insufficient. He copied the request onto that sheet, keeping the signed words and leaving out available with operator.

He stopped before crossing that phrase out on the original. He had no right to pretend they had not written it. Beneath the copy he added: clarify required attendance and authorizing person. He would let Veyne see the point that remained open. He did not want to present a paper more reassuring than the one he had received.

At the laboratory a man was closing a high window. Ilian recognized the smell before the doorway: oil, metal, and wood warmed by the afternoon. He did not enter with the urgency of customers who decided for themselves that they deserved an interruption. He waited for the man to step down and asked for Elias.

“He's finishing,” came the answer. “We aren't accepting any more deliveries today.”

“I'm not bringing a delivery.”

It was the same distinction Mael had used with him, and hearing it in his own mouth made him hesitate. He looked at the man whose hand rested on the door. For all he knew, this could be another person destined to work after hours thanks to Ilian's good reasons.

“I can leave a question,” he corrected himself. “If he's finished already, I'll come back when he tells me.”

The man pointed him to a space near the entrance bench. Ilian remained standing. From inside came Elias's voice, then the sound of something being returned to its own box. It was not the sound of someone rushing to rescue a customer. He liked it and found it irritating at the same instant.

While he waited, a gust moved a sheet near the door. Ilian held it down with two fingers. It was a parts list, and the first readable word was gasket. He read no farther. He placed an available weight on it and withdrew his hand, returning his own receipt to the folder before Elias appeared.

“Uren?” the young man said.

Ilian nodded. Elias had a trace of graphite on his fingers and one sleeve rolled farther up than the other. He looked tired and present, without the suspended expression Ilian remembered from the days when the boy's work had been a question of other people's permits.

“You've kept me waiting less than I feared.”

“I didn't know you were waiting for me.”

The answer contained neither an excuse nor discourtesy. Ilian smiled despite himself. It was a good place to begin, if he did not spoil it by asking a kindness before showing the work.

“It's after hours,” he said. “If you'd prefer, I'll leave the sheet and we can discuss it another time.”

Elias looked at the folder. “What is it about?”

Ilian took out the drawing and the signed slip. The receipt stayed inside. The young man did not owe him that money, and he did not want to seat him on Mael's side merely because he had arrived later.

He laid the drawing of the collar on the bench, with the section facing Elias. “An ordinary measurement,” he said. Then he touched the note he had added on the way. “And an attendance requirement they still need to explain.”
