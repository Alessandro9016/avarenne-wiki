Chapter 43 — The Open Drawing

Martin read the first instruction with the sheet so far from his face that Elias thought he was missing his glasses.

“Open the head.”

He looked up at the pump. Its head, beneath the workshop windows, had four bolts and two things that might have been handles. One of them was part of the pipe.

“Mine or the machine's?”

Yselle went on cleaning a brush over a jar. “Start with the more useful one.”

“See? That's why I married you. The instructions are always encouraging.”

Elias hid his smile behind his folder. He had returned to the workshop that afternoon with provisional dimensions and an excellent reason to keep the engineer's husband from touching the pipe. But Martin was not a visitor expecting entertainment. He had put his jacket beyond the reach of splashes, rolled his sleeves, and arranged the wrenches on the bench according to their openings. Chalk from his morning lesson still clung to the seam of one cuff.

“If you tell me,” he said to Elias, “she wins.”

Yselle put down the brush. “I'm not competing.”

Martin pointed to the sheet. “There is the first false statement of our experiment.”

Elias studied the drawing. A heavy line indicated the cylinder's upper rim; no line pointed to the head. He recognized the component because he had seen it open the previous day. The sheet would not have taught him.

“May I add an arrow?”

“You may record where my husband gets lost,” Yselle replied. “You may not help him find his way before he has become lost.”

Martin approached the machine. A container beneath the base caught the remaining water. The drive was stopped and disconnected; the handle rested along the frame. Elias watched the teacher distinguish the catch from the securing bolts. He chose the right wrench on his second attempt and did not try forcing the part that refused to move. He lacked information, not capable hands.

“First question,” Martin said. “Should I drain it?”

“You already did.”

“I did because you told me when I arrived. It isn't here.”

Elias wrote the sentence at the beginning. Before loosening the head, isolate and drain. Yselle bent to read it and pointed out a word to replace: this was not an ordinary lid, and two people were already giving it different names.

“Head,” he said.

“Head,” she confirmed. “The same word on the drawing, too.”

Meanwhile Martin had put the four bolts in a shallow box. Elias might have left them on the bench. Its rim would keep one from rolling into the channel, and now he could hardly believe he had not thought of it. He noted that as well, without turning it into a command. The sheet needed room for things that could actually cause a mistake.

The head lifted. Martin waited for Elias to take the other side, and they carried it onto two dry strips of wood. The gasket clung to the metal at one point. Yselle made a small movement with her hand, then closed it on her apron.

“Do you have a suggestion?” her husband asked.

“Several. I'm saving the most offensive.”

Martin did not tear the gasket. He raised its edge with a flat tool and worked it free. “Write that it shouldn't be lifted by that edge.”

Elias wrote it down. The component that most interested him, the removable liner, was now visible through the opening. A polished band caught the window light; farther down, the water left a smell of iron. Martin searched the illustration for the two lifting holes.

“These?”

Elias nodded, and Yselle coughed.

“He asked whether those were the holes.”

“And you gave him the answer.”

“They're the only two holes.”

“Then he didn't need it.”

Martin laughed and fitted the lifting tool. The component rose freely through the first stretch, then stopped with a sharp knock. Elias felt it through his arm: a brief stop, different from the long friction he had found on June second. He looked at Yselle. She did not move.

“It isn't the narrowing anymore,” he said.

“No.”

Martin tried turning the tool a little. The liner moved with his hand and stopped again, higher up. There was no distorted mouth; something prevented the component's shoulder from passing the edge.

“My wife has designed a machine whose removable component cannot be removed,” he declared.

“My husband skipped the third line.”

“The third line says: release the catch.”

“Exactly.”

“I released a catch. Have you hidden others as prizes?”

Yselle finally came closer. Beneath the removed head, a tab retained the liner's rim. It appeared on the drawing, very small, beside the linkage. It looked like part of a dimension line. Elias remembered moving it himself during the previous test; Yselle had only told him to hold the component up.

Martin lowered the liner onto its seat. “If I pull harder, what do I damage?”

“The tab,” Yselle said. “Then the rim.”

“And if I'm less strong?”

“You'll call someone and say it won't come out.”

“And what will he say about your pump?”

Yselle took the pencil from Elias's fingers. She looked less amused than before. On the clean margin she enlarged the detail, drew a line to the actual tab, and wrote a second name, which she then crossed out.

“Liner catch,” she said. “The drive catch stays the drive catch.”

Martin rested his hands on his hips and waited. Elias watched the sheet fill. He had spent the morning making distances exact, and now a man who had read three lines had shown him a different distance: the one between an object you knew and the paper you put into someone else's hands.

The released tab made a tiny sound. Martin lifted again, and this time the whole cylinder came out. The component was heavier than the drawing suggested. Elias brought the second support beneath it as the teacher set it down.

Yselle ran a finger over the place that had stopped the extraction. The surface was marked, but undamaged. Elias read relief on her face, though she did not offer it to her husband.

“You were lucky,” she said.

“I had an audience that wanted me alive.”

A cart passed outside. The wheels rattled the pane nearest the bench. For a moment the polished cylinder seemed crossed by a tree's shadow, then became only metal again. Elias rested his fingers against the rim and reached with Near Coordinates into the volume already opened. The relationship between the liner and its axis resolved with a quiet clarity, without written figures. He found the place where the narrowing had been; the correction had left an even passage. The caliper confirmed the measurement wherever it could enter.

Martin was watching him.

“You sense it before you measure it?”

“What I can reach. I don't know whether an alloy is sound because it seems straight.”

“I asked about the hole.”

Elias realized he had prepared to defend his entire profession. “Yes. This one, yes.”

“That must be satisfying.”

It was. He could not remember the last time anyone had said so without immediately asking what else he could do with it. He moved the caliper and showed Martin the difference between its reading and the reference he had reached. The teacher followed his own note with a finger, then asked whether Yselle had left enough room to insert that same instrument without removing the guide.

She took the drawing before Elias could answer.

They changed positions to put the liner back. Martin wanted the instructions beside the machine, rather than on the bench behind him; the damp sheet threatened to curl, and Yselle found him a wooden stand. Elias drew the direction of the gasket lip. Writing that it should be fitted correctly was not enough. If you fitted it backward, the sentence remained correct and the pump still leaked.

“How long have you taught?” Elias asked as Martin brought the component closer.

“Long enough to stop saying: it's obvious.”

“He says it at dinner,” Yselle observed.

“At dinner it's obvious you're about to steal my bread.”

The liner descended to its seat. Martin moved the right catch, checked the clearance again, and raised both hands. He waited for Elias to verify the fit before taking the head. This time the instructions made sense without summoning their author.

The water test took longer than expected. A drop appeared beneath the edge, so small that Elias first took it for moisture left by their fingers. Martin wiped it away, waited, and watched another form. Yselle did not blame the assembly. She had them drain it again, opened it with them, and found a fiber caught beneath the gasket. Elias raised it on the pencil tip. It looked like a pale hair.

“The cloth,” Martin said.

Yselle looked at the one they had used to dry the surface. The tear along its edge explained the fiber. They changed cloths, cleaned the surfaces, and closed the head. The second test left the metal dry. Elias watched the water reach the container in a steady stream and wished the three empty spaces on the shelf could fill simply because the work was possible now.

Martin wanted a copy of the sheet. Not to keep: he would ask someone at his school, capable with tools but unfamiliar with the prototype, to read the passage about the tab.

“Without taking him the pump,” Yselle said.

“Were you afraid I'd tuck it under my arm?”

The engineer gave him the damp sheet and kept the clean one. Then she looked at Elias's dimensions. Her smile faded over a list marked with two red strokes. He recognized the missing liners and materials still to be obtained. The prototype worked, but sending a clear drawing would not be enough to receive what they needed.

“I'll take the final dimensions to Marcel tomorrow,” he said.

“Before noon?”

“Before.”

Yselle nodded and folded the list without hiding it. Martin had stopped joking. He took the full container to the drain with both hands; Elias cleared his way. When he returned, the teacher moved the empty bolt box beside the drawing, where anyone entering would find it.

The last thing Elias added was not a measurement. He drew the little box, the head on its strips of wood, and the liner resting horizontally. On the sheet, at last, the parts removed from the machine had somewhere to go.

He was about to close his folder when Martin asked him to wait. He had found another problem: the sheet told them to replace the head before they could see whether the catch was in position. Whoever did the work could close everything without knowing whether the tab had actually engaged.

Elias returned to the machine. A side slot revealed part of the tab. From where he stood, the metal's reflection seemed sufficient; from Martin's side nothing showed. They exchanged places, and the view disappeared for Elias too.

“A notch here,” he said, touching the outer edge.

Yselle stopped his pencil. “On the actual catch or on the drawing?”

He looked at both. “Both. So you can see when it's home.”

The engineer found a point and made the mark on the accessible surface without weakening the tab. Martin crouched to check that the notch showed from the worker's side. Then he and Elias lifted the component a little: the catch held it. They would not have to discover that by dropping the liner during some future repair.

Yselle put the point away and erased a long sentence. She replaced it with the small drawing of the notch.

“Now I can send this,” Martin said.

“You can send a copy. I'm keeping this.”

Elias watched her take the drawing to the window to see it whole. It no longer looked like an elegant project for display. It bore fingerprints, one damp corner, and uneven details. Only then did the engineer smile, as if recognizing a machine she had previously seen from too close.
