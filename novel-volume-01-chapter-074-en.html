Chapter 74 — Number Nine

The woman at the Mutual's door held a ticket as though it were a prescription. Elias stopped to let her enter; she showed him the number in its corner and asked where she should wait.

“For whom?”

“For the healing.”

Behind her, a man sat on a crate with one bare foot inside a slit shoe. Elias looked across the yard. Two other people held identical slips. One bore the number nine. Someone had placed a bottle of water and a bunch of carnations on the gatekeeper's table, where deliveries were usually signed for.

Gaston called from inside. “Come here before you promise anything.” He also showed him a warning from Yselle: a man had robbed Martin at school and injured his hand. He had received treatment; requests for workshop access needed checking. Elias read the news again before turning to the queue.

Elias had promised nothing. He followed anyway. Pump number one was working behind the wall; water reached the tank and the attendants filled their containers without climbing the stairs. It was the result they had wanted. To reach it that morning, Elias had to pass between two people convinced he could bring about another.

Noemi stood at the counter with the new instructions. Around her right wrist she wore the handkerchief she had used before the injury: no blood, no dressing. When she caught him looking, she pulled back her sleeve.

“I don't want to have to show everyone.”

“Who sold those tickets?”

“That's what we're trying to find out.”

Gaston set the handover document before him. They needed to check the reference for the spare tested on pump two, access to the tools and the service shut-off. Elias checked the lines. Another hand had corrected the old instruction board; the drainage container had a new, more convenient place. Signature, date, number one. For once, the technical work took less time than talking to the people waiting outside.

Noemi signed the handover. “The original goes to Yselle. You can collect your copy at the workshop this afternoon. Now help me with something that doesn't fit the drawing.”

They went outside together. The woman with the prescription rose so quickly she dropped her ticket. Noemi picked it up and returned it without reading the number aloud.

“What's your name?”

The woman told her. The name did not help Elias; he did not know her, did not know which neighbourhood she had come from or how long the journey had taken. He realised he would have preferred a famous name whose claims he could confidently deny.

“They told us to come before the changeover,” she explained. “Afterwards he sees the workshop people.”

“No healer is seeing anyone here today.”

The woman looked at Noemi's wrist. She did it shamefacedly, then stubbornly. “But you're the one.”

“Yes.”

“Then don't say it's a lie.”

Elias heard Gaston stop behind them. He wanted to take the ticket, read it, find the misprinted detail that would put everyone on the same side. The slip did not say the duke would be there. It said access to applications and bore the Mutual's name above a serial number. At the bottom, in smaller type, were the words registration contribution.

“How much did you pay?” Noemi asked.

The woman answered quietly. A man beside the door laughed without amusement. He had paid more.

“They told me it included a place to accompany my brother.”

Neither seemed eager to report the seller. They looked at Noemi as though she had changed the terms. Elias understood that an accurate explanation might cost them the only thing they had really bought: the belief that they were already in the right queue.

“The duke is in Rovenne,” he said. “He left on the eighth. He hasn't given us appointments to sell.”

“Do you know him?”

It was the man on the crate. His foot protruded from the slit shoe, a greying bandage over its instep. Elias said yes and saw that yes draw the others towards him.

“Then write to him.”

“I can send a question. I can't tell you what he'll answer.”

The man stared at him. “I didn't ask you for his answer. I asked you to write.”

Elias lowered his eyes. Gaston brought another chair; the woman helped the man into it without asking whether he preferred to stand. Noemi addressed those still waiting in the doorway.

“If you want ordinary care, I'll tell you where to go. If you want to leave an address for a request, we'll take it without payment. That number doesn't buy healing and doesn't put you ahead of anyone.”

“What about the money?” someone asked.

Noemi looked at Elias. They could not return money they had not received. Saying so felt like a second injustice, but they said it. Gaston went to fetch a guard from the nearby post, taking one of the tickets only after asking its owner's permission.

The seller returned while they waited. He did not look like a priest. He was a carefully shaved man carrying a folding table under one arm and a shallow cashbox. He greeted Noemi like a colleague who was late.

“I told you there was interest.”

She had never seen him before.

He set the table against the wall. Elias put a hand on it before he could unfold it. The wood was warm from the sun. He used no faculty; the gesture seemed too flimsy for all the people watching him.

“Who authorised you?”

“I'm not selling cures. I'm collecting applications. It's printed there.”

He opened the cashbox with his free hand. Inside were numbered slips, coins and a small bottle of ink. He pointed at the small print, pleased to have anticipated the question. Elias thought of Mael Vaur's signed slip and, for a moment, hated every clarification written at the bottom of a page.

“You said someone would be seeing us today,” the woman said.

“I said the applications would be received.”

“You made me bring my husband.”

The man looked at the slit shoe. For a moment he lost his sentence. Then he recovered it: “To describe the case more accurately.”

Noemi took the ticket he was about to offer a new arrival. She held it between finger and thumb, away from the cashbox.

“Write your name on this. Your full name.”

“I don't answer to you.”

“Then wait for someone who can ask you.”

He tried to close the cashbox. The coins were still on top of the slips; one fell onto the paving and rolled beneath the table. The woman bent to retrieve it, but her husband stopped her by the arm. The seller looked towards the opening onto the street.

Elias shifted his weight into his path. It was a different decision from stopping a pipe: he did not know whether the man had a knife. Martin's bandaged hand came back to him. He called the gatekeeper by name and asked him to stand beside him.

The seller stopped looking for the quickest way out. “This is unlawful detention,” he said.

“You're waiting for a guard,” Noemi replied. “You may hold your cashbox. You may not walk away with these people's money while we explain why you took it.”

The guard arrived with Gaston. She did not arrest the man merely because Noemi pointed him out. She questioned the buyers separately, read the ticket and asked who was responsible for the Mutual. The seller repeated the distinction between cures and applications. The guard asked where the collected applications were to be delivered.

He had no address.

For the first time, nobody needed to add an explanation. The woman looked at the table, then at her husband. The man lowered his head and began tying his shoe around the bandage again, although it was not yet time to leave.

The seller tried once more to address the husband with the slit shoe. He said they could reach an agreement without involving the authorities. The man turned towards his wife: his wish to get the money back at once was so plain that Elias felt an absurd impulse to pay and end it all. He did not have the sum. He had not even received the six crowns for his work yet.

The wife showed the guard her ticket. “You wrote what I told you on the counterfoil. Where will that go?”

The seller stopped looking at her husband. The box held names, addresses, descriptions dictated by people who believed they were speaking to an authorised representative. The guard asked to see them and set the booklet aside. Noemi asked that they not be read aloud in the yard. Nobody had paid to tell passing strangers about their illness.

Elias saw the woman relax one hand, only that one. The number remained clenched in the other. They would have to return to give statements, perhaps lose another day's work, before knowing whether the money would be repaid. The seller had sold her that time as well, without writing it anywhere.

The guard recorded the amounts people said they had paid and asked two of them to accompany her to give statements. The cashbox remained closed, held for examination. Nobody received an immediate refund. The seller left in the same clean jacket he had worn when he arrived; now he walked in front of the guard and no longer greeted Noemi.

Elias folded the table. He noticed his hands shaking only when the catch slipped away twice. A man offered to help. It was the husband with the bandaged foot; he had risen to join him.

“Sit down,” Elias said.

“I'm not made of paper.”

They folded it together. The woman picked up a chair to carry indoors, as though she must pay that way for the time they had lost. Noemi stopped her and asked for an address. She did not write illness on the first line. She wrote the name the woman had already given and Elias had failed to remember.

When the yard emptied, Noemi remained seated on the low step. Her prosthesis was properly positioned, but she had been on her feet longer than expected. Elias brought water from the service supply and she reminded him it was not for drinking. He went back inside to find a jug from the drinking-water reserve.

“Don't laugh,” he said when he returned.

“I haven't enough breath.”

She drank. Elias looked at her wrist and this time made himself look at her face as well.

“Does it bother you that people know about the injury?”

“It bothers me that I have to become a promise to everyone I meet.”

She did not ask him to make the news disappear. She said she would write to Mirel Osta through the contact arranged for future questions. She wanted to describe the tickets and ask how to collect requests without making people believe a service already existed. She would include her own question without placing it above the others.

Elias sat beside her. Behind the wall, the pump's sound changed as an attendant shut off the delivery to the full tank. Both turned their heads; both recognised the normal stop and returned their attention to the yard.

“There'll be more people tomorrow,” he said.

“Probably.”

“Even without tickets.”

“Especially without them. They didn't invent the need.”

The man with the injured foot waved from the doorway. He carried an envelope with the address for ordinary care under his arm. His wife kept her now-useless number as evidence of what she had paid. Elias watched them leave without a miracle and without even a queue in which to wait for one.

Before leaving, he returned to the register. The pump handover was signed, the spare correct, the trial successful. None of those lines was a lie. He left the document with Gaston to send to Yselle and copied the woman's request onto a separate sheet.

When he wrote the date, the tenth of June, he discovered that at last he remembered her name.
