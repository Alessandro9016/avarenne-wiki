Chapter 78 — The Price of a Miracle

Yselle held the letter between two pencil-stained fingers.

“This one isn’t mine,” she said before Elias could confuse it with the handover copy.

His name was written on the envelope. Below, somebody who had delivered it had corrected the workshop address. Elias recognized the forwarding mark and instinctively turned toward the door, as though the duke’s carriage might have deposited this too.

“It came by post,” Yselle clarified. “He’s in Rovenne.”

Elias accepted the envelope. He had not reckoned on how difficult it would be to decide whether to open it immediately. He had waited for it as an ordinary reply, and now he kept holding it by the sealed side.

Yselle returned to the bench without asking him to read it aloud.

Pump two stood still. The third retained its cloth folded over the top, clear of the grips. Beneath a window, a man measured material for a delivery unrelated to the duke. The workshop had already found other things to do with the presence of those machines.

“Your copy,” the engineer said, showing him a separate sheet.

Elias checked pump one’s number, the reference to spare parts and the date. Water had reached where it needed to go. The Mutual had received instructions it could use, not merely the memory of a demonstration. He put the document in his folder without folding it through the signature.

“And for you?” he asked.

Yselle followed his glance to the surface where she had kept the accounts.

“The new agreement is signed. What remains to be paid hasn’t vanished. But my shares no longer stand behind capital we don’t need.”

She paused, almost surprised by the pleasure of saying it to someone who had not participated in the negotiation.

“I haven’t asked you for another measurement,” she added.

“I’d already opened my notebook.”

She smiled. Then a man at the entrance asked whether a component could leave that afternoon, and Yselle answered first with a question about its destination. The miracle had not freed her from needing to know where things went. It had given her something to work with besides a list of missing parts.

Elias said goodbye. The letter stayed closed until the street.

He did not want its first lines to belong only to him. The decision felt very solemn until he caught himself holding the envelope against the sun to guess the length of the sheet inside.

He put it back in his folder and laughed at himself.

Livia waited near the room’s ordinary entrance. They had agreed to meet after work; Edith would come later. The copyist carried a parcel beneath her arm and the expression of somebody who had already decided not to answer a question.

“It isn’t a present,” she said when he looked at it.

“I didn’t ask.”

“You were doing it.”

They went in through the door. The journey required stairs, a turn, time to find the key. Elias felt habit begin erasing that route because it held nothing extraordinary. He retraced it in his mind while Livia unlocked the door. That very route brought them where they needed to go without asking anything of the Hinge.

The oval table occupied the room with the confidence of an object intended for more people than used it. Twelve chairs; three would suffice that evening. Livia set down her parcel, changed the chair she had taken and found one whose edge did not press against her leg.

Elias left the folder closed in front of his place.

“He answered?”

He nodded.

“You’ve read it?”

“No.”

Livia looked at him long enough to make him fear she knew about the experiment against the sun. Then she rested her hands on the table and stopped asking questions too.

Edith arrived breathless from the last flight. She removed her coat, spread it over the chair back and asked for water before saying hello. Elias passed her the glass. She drank half and looked at the envelope.

“You aren’t going to make me put a hand on that paper and swear something.”

“We might,” Livia said.

“Then I’m eating first.”

Bread and something separately wrapped emerged from the parcel. Livia pushed it toward her with the offended expression of somebody whose sensible intention had been discovered too soon. Elias broke the bread. They waited for Edith to set down the glass; then he opened the letter along its edge.

Alexander agreed to talk with them. He asked which questions they wanted to tackle first and said he had questions of his own about their work.

The lines fixed no place, took no ownership of their request and did not promise solutions to every problem they might care to write about. They answered people who had asked for a conversation. He wanted to know how to arrange it.

Elias read the final part again. The signature was the same one he had seen beneath the donation of the machines. His own letter did not yet contain a place for a signature like his.

He passed the sheet to Livia.

“It’s a yes,” Edith said, leaning far enough to read with her.

“To the conversation,” Elias replied.

“I know. We didn’t ask him to live here.”

Livia laughed and read the rest. Her finger stopped at the part about arranging the meeting.

“We’ll have to choose where.”

The ensuing silence had nothing to do with finding a table. Elias looked at the empty chairs, the broad surface, the distance between their three places. He had seen the duke bend to examine a liner seat, then make three complete machines exist. It would have been easy to picture him at the head of the table and call that figure a solution.

“Not here the first time,” he said.

Edith looked up. “Why?”

The question did him good. He could have hidden his fear inside a rule nobody had established.

“Because I want to be able to ask him something without watching to see whether this room convinces him. And I want you to be able to get up without feeling you’ve invited him into everything we have.”

Livia moved the sheet to the middle. “He’ll need to understand what he’s answering too. We aren’t an assignment from Yselle.”

“We wrote through her,” Edith pointed out.

“To get the letter there. Not to have her guarantee every question.”

Edith took another piece of bread. She thought while chewing, looking irritated at how visible her reasoning must be to the others.

“One meeting, then. Afterward we decide whether we want another.”

Nobody put the duke’s name above a chair.

“And if we don’t like what he proposes?” Edith asked.

“I’ll tell him,” Elias replied.

Livia looked at him. “Even if I like it?”

The ready answer stopped in his mouth. He had imagined taking the unpleasant part to protect them; in doing so he had already chosen where both of them would stand.

“Then I’ll tell him for myself.”

Edith pushed her chair back just enough to free her coat. “That suits me better. I don’t want to have to be brave about what you’ve decided to endure too.”

Her frankness made him lower his eyes. Livia did not smile to remove his discomfort. She turned the letter toward him and asked whether he truly wanted the meeting now that he had an answer he could use.

Elias thought of the nut on Marcel’s bench, the two views of the same box. Being safe from everything he did not understand was not enough.

“Yes,” he said. “Even if we disagree afterward.”

“Then let’s arrange it,” she replied.

Elias found a free page. The thirteenth was a day to propose, far enough off to receive an answer without pretending the road between two cities could fold because they were impatient. He wrote it at the top and left the time and place blank. They would have to find an available room and learn whether the day suited Alexander too.

“What do we ask him?” Livia said.

Elias had prepared an answer about the ability to create matter. He thought of Noemi’s skin becoming whole and the way she had searched for pain. The next question might occupy every evening of his life.

“I’d like to understand how he recognizes that what he creates is what he’s been asked for.”

Livia frowned. “The design?”

“That too. But he asked us for measurements, processes, the model. He didn’t produce a pump just because he knew its name. I’d like to know where what he has to learn ends and what he can do begins.”

Edith stopped eating. “For people it’s a different question.”

Elias looked at her. She was not only talking about Noemi.

“I don’t want to ask for healing on somebody else’s behalf,” Edith said. “I want to ask what one needs to know before offering it. If he can tell us. When I asked about Heart, I realized I kept picturing only the moment when pain ends.”

The sentence remained on the table. Livia did not try to complete it with a promise. Instead she picked up the pencil and wrote her own question about the difference between a preserved form and a thing truly present.

“This one is mine,” she said.

Elias read. He had not decided whether all three belonged in the same conversation; for the first time, though, the letter did not oblige him to prove his friends were interesting enough to deserve a duke’s time.

They were their questions. If Alexander had something to answer, they could listen without giving them away with the room.

“And what do we bring?” Edith asked.

Livia indicated the sheet. “A conversation, to begin with.”

“I didn’t mean a reward.”

“Neither did I.”

Elias thought of Yselle’s bench. The duke had needed somebody to show him part of the world before making another part exist. He did not know how rare it was for him to encounter that need. He could offer a problem he truly knew instead of pretending to possess a larger secret.

“I’ll bring a drawing and the test that changed it,” he said. “Not the Hinge drawing. The liner. If he wants to understand how we work, I’ll show him.”

Edith nodded. “Then I’ll bring what I don’t know.”

“Is it heavy?”

“Very. Don’t offer to lift it before I finish explaining.”

They laughed. The table did not become smaller; it simply seemed less empty.

Livia gathered their questions and left Elias room to write a proposal. Not an oath, not a list of favors. He put down the possible day, specified that they would confirm an ordinary venue and reread the sentence without adding an explanation of their entire lives.

The commercial request Ilian had brought lay in the other part of his folder. It still awaited a decision. He did not take it out to make the meeting with Alexander seem more urgent; the duke had not agreed to settle an unknown draftsman’s account.

When they finished, Edith put the bread back in its wrapping. Livia opened the parcel to check she had not left her notebook inside and found only a crumb. She brushed it into her palm. Elias folded the proposal for delivery the next day and put it away separately from the letter received.

All three stood. He was last to leave, after looking at the table once.

The Hinge still had a single opening available. Elias did not use it to save himself the stairs.

Outside, the evening air had lost the hot smell of workshops. From a nearby street came water striking stone: a bucket emptied, not the Mutual’s pump audible all the way here. Elias walked on without seeking a miracle in every noise.

He had received six crowns and an answer. Part of the work was finished; another part finally depended on a question he had chosen to ask.
