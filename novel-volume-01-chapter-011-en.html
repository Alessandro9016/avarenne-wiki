Chapter 11 — Marks on the Floor

The warehouse seemed smaller without the accident.

Elias noticed as soon as he entered. The crates had been rearranged in new rows, and a man with a broom walked through the place where he remembered a crowd. Light came through the same windows. It had no right to seem so ordinary.

At the threshold, Elias had stopped a moment too long. Gaston passed him with the folder, then turned without calling. That silent wait allowed him to enter by choice rather than follow someone.

As soon as he set foot inside, Elias sought the accident's location. A crate he did not remember occupied it. He moved it in his mind, superimposing the room he had reconstructed every night on the present one. The two images refused to fit.

The man responsible for access asked whether he needed a chair. The kindness brought blood to his face. He would have preferred a hostile remark he could answer with his prepared evidence.

“Not yet. Thank you.”

The man indicated where they could set their folders without obstructing work. Elias carried his notebook there and opened it at the warehouse plan. He knew it so well he no longer needed to look; for that very reason, finding the right page took him a few moments.

Etienne came beside him. The cap between his hands did not conceal his arm. He looked around and drew a longer breath than necessary.

“I didn't think they'd changed so much.”

Elias searched for something to confirm that, then realized he did not know which crates had already been moved before the test. He recognized his drawing. Not every day of the warehouse's life.

“We can mark the ones we remember for certain.”

Etienne nodded. The cautious plural did not seem to disappoint him. He only asked for another moment to look.

A man passed with a crate on a trolley and asked them to make room. Elias moved at once, then felt a flare of anger. Did they really still have to be careful not to disturb anyone here?

The wheels stopped against the toe of his shoe. Elias drew back his foot and the trolley passed. The crate brushed his jacket; he felt air move against the sleeve and found again the point where Etienne had worked. “Here,” he said, before forgetting which relationship he was looking at.

Marcel asked him to indicate where he had stood at the beginning of the work.

Elias went to a pale stone in the floor.

“Here.”

He did not activate Astrolabe. That day he was the witness to an earlier measurement; the examination belonged to Marcel. The distinction weighed on him, but it did not prevent him from remembering.

Etienne stayed near a pillar. Gaston opened the file on a clean crate. The person responsible for admitting them accompanied them to the bench, then stood far enough away to let them work, not far enough to stop listening.

A workman asked whether he should stay. Marcel said they would only ask about what he personally remembered, if it proved relevant. He did not order him to leave his work to attend a demonstration. The man returned to his crates.

Elias watched him go and understood that he was looking for a face able to confirm his own account. It was not very different from searching for a favorable number without looking at the rest of the table.

“Do you remember the light?” Etienne asked.

He looked at the windows.

“Yes.”

“So do I. Some of the rest comes back better than other parts.”

Elias nodded. The memory of the accident was not a shared recording from which each of them could retrieve the same image.

The portable bench was still there.

Elias felt the same absurd anger he had felt at his own clean shirt: things allowed themselves to be put right too easily. The metal's surface did not preserve the moment someone had screamed.

Marcel set two fingers on the edge and closed his eyes. When he opened them, he indicated a point for Gaston to mark.

He had not read the past. He had grasped the present space with a precision a measuring rule would take longer to verify.

Gaston recorded the first dimension. Then the second.

Marcel had him check the rule against the same reference points too. The man overseeing their access came close enough to follow the reading.

“With your power, could you show us anything you wanted?” he asked.

“I could be mistaken myself,” Marcel said. “That's one reason to repeat the comparison.”

The man seemed to find the answer less impressive than he had hoped. Elias suddenly found it important. His mentor's faculty gave him access to a geometry. It did not oblige others to believe him because he had closed his eyes.

The measuring tape bent near the first mark. Gaston straightened it and asked the access supervisor to hold the far end steady. Elias saw the man seek the exact point on the edge, then let it slip to make room for the master's hand.

“Stay there,” Marcel said. “I need to read it from your position.”

There was nothing supernatural about the correction. Even someone who had never heard of Astrolabe could hold the end of a tape well or badly. Once it was stable, Marcel had the supervisor read the value before repeating it to Gaston.

Elias followed the distance on his plan. The section matched, but the sheet preserved the previous arrangement. He wanted to lift it beside the bench to show everyone the difference at once. He left it flat, where each person could see which reference he started from.

The supervisor asked whether the second reading should replace the first. Gaston showed him the two headings. For the first time, Elias understood that their conclusion must survive a question asked without bad faith. A correct number was insufficient if its presentation led a reasonable person to use it in the wrong place.

When the ordinary reading matched, the man moved to shade the sheet Gaston was writing on. It was the first time he had helped them without being asked.

“This isn't the position on my drawing,” Elias said.

“We can see that.”

Marcel's words took away his need to repeat it. Elias stepped back.

The problem was not establishing that the bench was different now. It was knowing when that difference had entered the work.

Etienne indicated the passage between two rows of crates.

“I came from that side. My arm didn't go behind it.”

The man overseeing them began to explain that the crates were no longer the same. Marcel asked him to preserve the observation without making it say more. Etienne had provided no coordinate, but a relationship between his work and the bench.

Marcel asked him to describe the route he had taken before the reading. Elias pointed to the bench, then the wall and the place from which he had checked the reference. Midway through, he heard his voice quicken. He was approaching the sentence he had repeated too often at home.

The master stopped him at a small detail: where he had rested his hand.

“Here.”

“On the edge or the plate?”

Elias hesitated. The memory was not a complete picture; he possessed the pressure of metal beneath his palm and the irritation of a cuff, but the object before him was turned differently.

“On the edge. The one facing the passage.”

Marcel had the distinction recorded. He did not ask him to repeat the power to prove he could still use it. Elias felt the relief only when the next question concerned the certificate.

The access supervisor wanted to see the reference on the plan. Elias turned the notebook toward him and let the man follow the line himself. It would have been easy to add that it was obvious. Instead he waited until the man reached the point.

“On this side, then.”

“In that position of the bench.”

Hammering came from the next room. The noise made him tense the back of his wounded hand. For an instant he recovered the sound of the accident; when the hammering continued in a regular rhythm, he managed to separate it from the memory.

Etienne asked whether the gauze needed changing. Elias said no. He wanted to apologize for being there with a tiny cut when Etienne had had to reconstruct whole weeks of his life. He realized in time that this would also ask the worker to reassure him about that.

He looked back at the plan. The values did not need him to suffer enough to be correct. He had to show them without burdening them with everything he hoped to obtain.

Gaston knelt before two mounting marks. Other traces lay beside them, older, one filled with dust.

“We can't put a date on every hole,” he said.

Elias crouched. How convenient it would have been to find a fresh wound in the stone and call it April twenty-first.

He only drew the marks.

The two sets of readings Gaston had kept had different headings and reference points. The first matched the arrangement described in Livia's copy; the second took the bench after the move. No magical transformation of the paper was necessary to see it, now they had chosen the right thing to compare.

“This value isn't wrong,” Marcel said, touching the first set.

Elias felt his throat tighten.

“I know.”

“And it doesn't become wrong because the second one is different.”

The man responsible for their access came closer.

“There's only one certificate.”

“That's precisely what we need to clarify,” Gaston replied.

Marcel had the tape laid along the first reference, then the second. Elias followed its end. The second reading was correct. He bent over his notebook, returned to the first, searched once more for a subtraction error.

“It can't be here and there at the same time,” he said.

“The bench can't,” Marcel replied.

Gaston ran a finger over the dates. Elias watched both lines until the numbers stopped seeming competing versions of one truth. There was a move between them. Somewhere, someone had gone on using the first sheet after making it.

Etienne waited until they had finished, then asked whether he could sit. Elias brought him a stool without making a display of generosity.

“I remembered the passage being narrow,” the workman said. “Not how narrow.”

“That's enough for what you're saying.”

Etienne looked at him with a relief Elias had not deserved until that moment.

Gaston brushed dust from the notebook's edge with the back of his hand. A corner of a page had folded beneath the folder, and Elias straightened it too quickly, almost tearing it. He left it alone when he noticed the gesture.

They had worked in the room and were leaving with something more precise. The place had grown no less ordinary. Across the bench, the man with the broom waited to reclaim the patch where their feet still stood.

Elias signaled that they had finished. The man came through at once and pushed the dust toward the door. For the first time, Elias could watch that movement without feeling it erased what had happened.

The supervisor cleared the weights holding down the paper off the bench. Elias thanked him before closing his notebook. The man nodded and returned his gaze to the work that had waited for them.

When they collected the drawings, the bench remained in its present position. They did not move it back to construct a picture of a more convenient truth.

Outside, Marcel stopped to reread the notes. The sun had dried half the paving. Footprints remained in the other half.

“Now we need to speak to Vern,” Elias said.

Gaston closed the file.

“Yes. And let him answer what we ask.”

At the door Elias turned. The bench remained in the second position; in his notebook the certificate still referred to the first.

“Let's ask when he stopped waiting for calibration,” he said.

Marcel closed Gaston's folder before the wind lifted its edge. This time nobody told him to ask a different question.
