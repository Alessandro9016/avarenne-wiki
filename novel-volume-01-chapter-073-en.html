Chapter 73 — An Unassigned Place

Elias Veyne's name was the second one Edran read on the envelope. The first, written larger, was his own. He expected nothing more from the evening yet, and that arrangement seemed almost a small offense: the recipient occupied so much space that one had to turn the paper to reach the sender.

Maurice had brought it with the business correspondence arriving from Lethorne. The carrier had taken almost four hours and waited at the gate for someone to receive the packet. No alarm, no message shouted from the courtyard. The letter had traveled the road he knew and now lay beside the cup left from dinner.

“Is it from Aunt Yselle?” Celine asked.

“It came through her.”

His sister had the book about levers under her arm. She seemed to have decided to carry it even when she was not reading. She looked at the envelope without trying to crane her neck to read.

“If you reply, can I put my sheet in the same post?”

“For Martin?”

“And for Aunt Yselle.”

Edran said yes before he knew whether he would reply. Celine thanked him with an absent nod and returned to a page marked by two ribbons. He opened the envelope with the paper knife. Inside he found a single sheet and three signatures: Elias Veyne, Livia Varn, Edith Torrel.

They were not asking for a pump. The first line brought relief so swift it seemed ungenerous. He went back to read it slowly.

Three people proposed a conversation about tools, abilities, and knowledge useful to an exchange. Elias named surveying work, Livia comparison of texts and documents, Edith ordinary study and practice. Questions and subjects could be agreed beforehand. For place and day, they were waiting to learn whether he was interested.

There was no moving story to reward, nor a promise that everyone would tell him the truth. The sheet left out enough to make the choice evident. No address to reach that night, no description of a rare object. A conversation, and three people willing to put their names beneath that word.

In the surname Veyne, Edran recognized the young man who had shown him water, weight, dimensions. He did not know the other two. Alexander's memories offered no forgotten encounter to attach them to; the present remained empty where it had not been lived.

“Good news?” Celine asked, still bent over the book.

“I don't know yet.”

“Your face doesn't say bad.”

He realized he was smiling. He folded the sheet along its original crease, then opened it again. He might have given that expression to any request simply because the sender was not asking him to save something. It was not a good reason to invite three strangers into the house.

They had not asked for the house.

He looked toward the door. Maurice was collecting the tray and asked whether a reply should be prepared for the first post in the morning. Edran said he would write it himself. The butler nodded and allowed him the time to do so, a form of service he valued more than tea that evening.

First he looked for Noemi's sheet. He had brought it from Lethorne with the other documents, keeping it separate from the financial ones. The paper contained questions about people who wanted to meet him, about those who did not want to be called forward before others, about pain and impairments not easily described in a line. It was another piece of work yet to begin, not a list of healings already promised.

The three people's invitation did not erase that sheet. He moved it beside the book he would leave for Mirel, so he would not use a pleasant novelty as a reason to forget what had come before.

The doctor came to collect a file shortly afterward. She wore her working dress and had already pinned up her hair to go out. Edran indicated the questions from the Mutual.

“Noemi would like to arrange some meetings. I haven't given her a day.”

Mirel looked at the sheet before looking at him. “Do you want me to write to her?”

“I'd like to know what you need before speaking with the people. You can note it here. Then I'll answer her.”

She read the first question. She did not ask him to demonstrate again what he had done for the cut. Instead she asked whether Noemi knew she might receive a different answer for each person.

“Yes.”

“Then let's begin with names and how they want to be contacted. This sheet doesn't contain all of them.”

Edran left her the paper. He did not need to pretend to be powerless to notice that he did not know those people. The memory of tissue joined beneath his hand was sharp, real. Equally real was the distance between a question written by Noemi and a person choosing to offer him their body.

Mirel picked up the file she had come for and told him when she would leave her notes. He did not assign her Elias's letter. She had not become the keeper of all his decisions merely because she could ask a precise question.

After she left, he carried the invitation into the study. The light was stronger there and the desk less occupied. The chair reserved for visitors stood on the other side of the desk; from that place, anyone entering had to speak toward the portrait behind the duke. Edran sat for a moment on the side opposite his own.

The surface seemed wider. From there he would have had to put his hands far enough forward for the man behind the desk to see they concealed nothing. Alexander's memories contained conversations held at that table, clothes, titles, and the sound of the door after a dismissal. Older memory carried other tables, other ways of forcing a body to appear harmless.

He rose without completing the comparison. That chair was not a chain, and destroying it would not improve the letter. He would not use the study for the first meeting.

Teren knocked with the final security update. The man in charge had a problem with an entrance to the courtyard, already watched, and a solution to carry out in the morning without changing the other shifts. Edran listened to the end. Only then did he show him the invitation.

“Three people want to speak to me outside this house.”

Teren read the names, then the place left blank. “Have you answered already?”

“Not yet.”

“Veyne is the man from the workshop?”

“Yes.”

“The other two?”

“I don't know them.”

Teren did not fill the silence with suspicion. He asked whether Yselle had added a recommendation; Edran showed him the envelope, which bore only the address. His aunt had delivered the letter. The rest was not hers.

“Once there's a place and a day, I can tell you how to get there and what is needed,” the security chief said. “There isn't a route to prepare yet.”

“I don't want to turn the conversation into an official visit.”

“I can work without announcing you to an entire street.”

He liked the answer more than necessary. Part of him would have liked to test everyone around him, force them to reveal the point where care became possession. Teren offered only the work for which he was responsible. Edran handed him the invitation again and waited for him to finish reading the last line.

“I'll reply that I'm interested,” he said. “The place will be agreed. Nobody comes here tomorrow as a result of this letter.”

Teren nodded. He spoke once more about the courtyard entrance and withdrew carrying his own instructions, not the duke's pen.

Edran remained before the blank page. The first formula that occurred to him promised availability in a tone so generous it erased the limits he wished to leave to choice. He discarded it. The second sounded like a summons. Merely changing the order of two sentences turned three people who had invited him into people called to present themselves.

On the first attempt he had written Dear Mr. Veyne. He stared at the formula. It would have been easy to ask him to convey his thanks to the other two, leave him the work of representing them, and continue knowing only the man he had already met. But the letter was not signed by Elias and two people accompanying him. One signature had a broad stroke, the other occupied little space; he did not even know which woman had held the pen for the text.

He turned the sheet and tried writing all the names beneath a single salutation. He did not know whether Livia or Edith was married, and he did not want to choose a title by guessing at a life. It seemed ridiculous to be stopped there after succeeding in creating three complete machines. The pen's point dried while he hesitated. He had to clean it, dip it again, and accept the blot left on the scrap.

In the end he used the names as they had written them. The reply did not have to resemble the ones a recipient was supposed to desire from a duke. It was enough to recognize it tomorrow without feeling the need to explain it.

He took a smaller sheet.

He wrote the three names, not Elias's alone. He thanked them for the proposal and said he was interested in a conversation on the subjects indicated. He asked to receive the questions they preferred to address first. For place and day they would find a solution suitable to everyone through Yselle's address.

He reread the sentence about the conversation. He had promised neither teaching, money, protection, nor membership. He did not want to add a list of everything he was not offering. It would sound as though he accused the senders of requesting things they had not written.

Instead he added: In turn, I would welcome the chance to ask some questions about your work. It was the missing part, and the hardest to leave on paper without reducing it to politeness. He did not merely want to examine useful people. He wanted to enter a conversation in which one of his questions could receive an answer he did not know.

Celine passed outside the study. She stopped with her own sheet already folded and set it on the edge, without reading the open page.

“You've decided to reply.”

“Yes.”

“Then don't forget mine.”

He looked at the smaller envelope. Celine had rewritten Martin's name after getting one letter wrong and left the correction visible. Edran put it beside his own. His sister did not ask where he was going, perhaps because he did not know either. She said good night and continued toward the stairs.

He signed Alexander Valcienne. The pen moved without hesitation, in the hand he knew from the household documents. Inside the letter there was no older name to explain, no power to display as a credential.

When Maurice returned, the sheet was dry. Edran handed over the reply for Yselle, asking her to pass it to the three signatories, and Celine's separate letter for their aunt and Martin. It would leave with the first post on the tenth. He did not demand that the road shorten because he had made a decision.

He remained in the study a moment longer. The visitor's chair faced the portrait, exactly where it had been. He had not moved it. But the place where he would listen to these people was no longer decided by the furniture, the title, or the fear of someone who did not know them.

Before extinguishing the lamp, he turned the copy of the reply toward him. Three names at the top, his own at the bottom. No seat occupied among them. For the first time in days, the thing he awaited was not a report of something that had already happened.
