Chapter 70 — A Teacher's Mistake

Martin had brought the wrong wrench, and for almost half an hour that fact allowed him to appear very competent.

The wrench sat well in his hand, did not slip, and reached the nut at an elegant angle. He had taken it from his demonstration box, where every tool had its place outlined on the bottom. Beside pump number two, already drained after the morning's tests, he showed the movement to the two workers still at the workshop. He had not brought a class to interrupt Yselle's work. These were people who would sooner or later need to open a similar assembly, and they had asked to see how to reach the rear fastener.

“The number tells you the opening,” he explained. “Not how much force to use.”

One of the two nodded. The other studied the sheet Martin had set on the crate. It had been corrected often enough to deserve a clean copy. He thought he could make one that afternoon, between a lesson and dinner. Since the security over the shares had been released, even a dinner had recovered a conceivable length.

He loosened the fastener a fraction and turned it back. The machine was stopped, open where necessary, with no water in the circuit. They did not need to repeat the gasket installation. The part tested that morning remained on the bench with its history written beside it.

“Would you like to try?” he asked the nearer worker.

The man put down Martin's wrench and took his own. Its opening was the same, but its handle was straight. He fitted it over the nut, attempted to turn it, and met the side support before completing the movement. He reversed it. The wrench fitted; his hand could not find enough room to work.

Martin looked at the support as though it had moved while he was talking.

“From the other side,” he said.

The man made room for him. Martin bent, took the straight wrench, and tried from the other side. The back of his hand struck the same obstacle. He did not even have the excuse of a pupil misunderstanding. The wrench obeyed the instruction he had written perfectly.

Elias, on the far side of the bench, stopped scraping at an erasure. He did not smile. Martin would have preferred it if he had: a smile would have offered him a line with which to escape.

“Yours has a bent neck,” the other worker said.

At last Martin looked at the tool he had brought. The offset in the handle was so familiar that it had become invisible. It raised his knuckles above the support and permitted a sweep the ordinary wrench did not have.

“Yes,” he said. “I showed you that one and described a different one.”

Yselle, discussing a supply order at the entrance, turned at the sound of the sentence. The morning's maker had already gone. She came to the bench without the anxious expression Martin had learned to look for over the past few days. She seemed annoyed by something small. It pleased him out of all proportion.

“What did you describe about it?”

He showed her the sheet. Reach the fastener with a wrench of the specified size. The size was right. Everything else was inside his head.

Yselle laid the straight wrench beside the offset one. “We can supply this.”

“We can,” the worker said. “But if I take it to be repaired, or someone borrows it for another job, the sheet doesn't know it's missing.”

Martin raised his pencil. He wrote offset wrench and added a small drawing. The man did not seem satisfied. Not hostile: he kept looking at the machine, as though waiting for it to provide the rest of the answer.

“This is what's in my box,” he said, touching the straight wrench. “I saw that other one for the first time this morning.”

Martin lowered the pencil. The exercise was not to find the word that would make his sheet correct. He took the man's toolbox and set it open on the bench. There were wrenches, pliers, a hammer with a darkened handle, and a strip of cloth with two pointed tools wrapped inside. No empty space shaped like his wrench.

“Then let's begin with what you have.”

The worker picked up the pliers and put them down again before touching the nut. “That would damage it.”

“Probably.”

“Not probably. I've already done it to another one.”

Martin nodded. The statement was worth more than an elegant supposition. Elias approached with a blank sheet and asked where they wanted to mark the obstruction. He had already attended a lesson on May thirtieth; since then he had asked other questions, worked, corrected drawings. Martin did not have a boy before him who needed to be taken back to the first line. He yielded the place beside the machine.

Elias drew the support, the nut, and the room needed for the hand. The worker stopped him before he finished: when working at the site, his toolbox would be on the opposite side. To fetch another tool he would have to turn around in the passage. On the sheet, the passage did not exist.

“Don't draw the whole room,” Martin said. “Mark where you're standing.”

The young man added a very ugly outline of two shoes. The second worker said nobody had feet like that. Elias enlarged one shoe out of spite, and for a moment they all stopped concerning themselves with Martin's mistake.

Using the straight wrench required a less brilliant solution than his demonstration. They could remove an auxiliary support with the machine stopped, clear the sweep, and then replace it; that meant extra fasteners, time, and an opportunity to forget something. Yselle did not want it as the usual method. She marked it as an alternative to be completed and checked before they gave it to anyone, rather than a sentence to improvise at the bottom of the page.

“A dedicated wrench costs less than the time we waste,” she said.

“Provided it arrives with the pump,” the man answered.

Martin picked up his own tool and held it over the box. Habit had already set him thinking of lending it. Then he pictured the following morning: himself at school, the empty outline on the bottom, someone at the Mutual looking for a tool that still belonged to him.

“I need this one too,” he said. “There needs to be one assigned to that job.”

Yselle looked at him with one eyebrow slightly raised. “A very bold financial proposal.”

“Wait until you see what I charge for the drawing of the feet.”

The older worker took the offset wrench and tried it. His hand cleared the support without striking it. He completed the slow movement, stopped, and repeated it from the starting point. Then he passed the tool to his companion. Martin watched both: neither used his grip, and the second held farther forward on the handle. It worked that way too. A detail he had not anticipated and did not need to correct.

The second worker glanced toward the door. Martin had already opened his mouth to invite him to repeat the movement when he noticed the man holding his folded jacket over one arm.

“Do you have something else to do?”

“A delivery to receive. I said I'd be back before noon.”

Martin reached for his watch and discovered he had left it in his jacket pocket. The man told him the time without waiting. The demonstration that should have taken a few minutes had eaten into the time allowed for his return.

“I'll do the last trial,” his companion said.

They were not asking permission to be uninterested in the machine. They had another job that would not disappear because Martin had found a good reason to detain them. He promised a copy of the sheet through the workshop, and the younger man left, stepping sideways around the crate they had placed in the passage. Martin moved it beneath the bench. The other man helped with his foot, then returned to the tool without demanding thanks. If Martin wanted to keep his attention, he needed to finish something.

They recorded shape and size on the sheet, with a visible reference to the fastener involved. Yselle added the tool to the equipment list. She did not sign a promise to supply it forever: she arranged for it to be procured for the machine and for its replacement to be identifiable. Martin tried to draw the profile of the neck and produced something resembling a crushed pipe. Elias took the sheet from him with rather vengeful politeness.

“I'll copy the shape.”

“It wasn't a pipe.”

“I didn't say it was.”

Yselle laughed. It had happened before during that week, but this laugh did not immediately have a page to defend. Martin put away his wrench and felt relief with a delay that annoyed him. He wished he had felt it the previous evening, when she told him about the signature. Instead he had asked three times what was still owed, as though the worst must be concealed in the best answer.

The remaining worker tried following the page from the beginning without his commentary. He reached the right point, searched for the drawing of the tool, and found it too far from the sentence. They moved the reference. Then he asked where to put the small fasteners removed for access. That question concerned only the alternative they had not yet approved, which Martin had left in the middle of the main page.

He crossed it out and gave it a separate sheet. It was not ready to be read as an instruction. The sound of the pencil was the only comment needed.

When they finished, the older worker set his own box beside his feet again. He looked at Martin, then at the corrected sheet.

“Now tomorrow I won't have to guess which wrench you had in mind.”

Martin could have turned that into a fine line to repeat at school. He left it in the man's voice. Instead he asked whether the number beside the profile was readable. The man said the second digit looked like a letter; Martin rewrote it larger.

He and Yselle remained before the bench while Elias carried the sheets to the other side to copy them. A call came from outside about loading a wagon, followed by an argument about where to leave it. The workshop was beginning to ask ordinary things of the person in charge of it again.

“Have you eaten?” Yselle asked.

“I was planning to, before my perfect demonstration.”

“Then you must be very hungry.”

He wiped his fingers on a rag and realized she too was waiting for a different answer. Since yesterday they had discussed the agreement as something to inspect from every side. He had not yet asked what she wanted to do once it was no longer urgent to do something else.

“Will you come home at a reasonable hour tonight?”

“That depends on how long you take to define reasonable.”

“Before I burn dinner.”

“So I have to arrive before you.”

Martin smiled, then stopped searching for a joke. “I'd like to have dinner with you. Without the contract on the table.”

Yselle held the rag between two fingers. “There's still work to pay for.”

“I know. I'm not asking for the table to be free.”

She tapped his arm lightly with the rag. It was not peace earned forever; it seemed enough to sit down to. She said she would come back after arranging the tool order. He promised not to choose a recipe requiring three hands.

Before leaving he picked up the demonstration box. The empty outline of the wrench was filled again. He could have kept that simple pleasure and forgotten the rest, but he left the lid open until he had copied the precise mistake into his notebook: correct size, shape unspecified. Beneath it he noted that the next demonstration would begin with the worker's toolbox.

Elias handed him the copy, still damp. In the margin were two shoes that finally looked credible. Martin indicated the larger one.

“Is that me?”

“That's the space you take up.”

Martin put the sheet in his folder, keeping it away from the oil. “Then we've saved paper.”
