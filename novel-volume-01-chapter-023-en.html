Chapter 23 — An Opening

The wall did not become transparent.

Elias would have found something like that easier to look at. Transparency still belonged to the world of windows. What appeared before the service entrance instead placed two locations side by side without granting the space between them the right to intervene.

Marcel had aligned the marks and turned the lever. The click had been small, almost offensive in its ordinariness.

Elias had prepared the load on the floor to keep the passage clear. The box closed with an ordinary catch. He opened it once, counted the pieces with Livia, and closed it again. He wished the precaution resembled everyday work less closely, as though a miracle deserved preparation made of the same extraordinary substance.

Marcel asked him to check that the ordinary route remained clear. Elias walked to the antechamber and returned empty-handed. The hall was where he had left it; the object they were about to use was not necessary to reach it.

That was precisely what made the gesture difficult to explain. A distance could be short enough to walk and real enough to justify seeing what happened when an apparatus treated it differently.

Livia returned to the destination and told him not to bring the load until he saw the landing place clear. Elias nodded. They did not rehearse the surprise of meeting by pretending something beyond them had summoned them. They had agreed a time, a place, and concrete work to observe.

The master positioned himself beside the lever. His hands bore decades of work, but the rank he lacked did not appear between his fingers to reward his experience. If the check revealed something needing correction, they would have to stop and seek expertise neither yet possessed.

Elias waited for Marcel to finish. The box's weight still rested on the floor. He would have time to lift it after the opening; he need not make waiting harder to prove himself ready.

First he had examined the imbued teeth and accessible surfaces, looking for deformation or wear that would require them to stop. Elias had assisted without confusing that check with the recharging neither of them could perform.

Now, beyond the opening, Elias could see the antechamber.

He knew how many steps it took to reach it by the ordinary route. He had checked the distance, the changes of direction, the location within the same lot. None of that knowledge was false. The passage nevertheless allowed another action.

“The load first,” Marcel said.

The test box contained ordinary pieces already weighed. Not a commercial sample, not medicines taken from the clinic, and not a person who had accepted risk merely because they were ill.

Elias lifted it. They had agreed on a single transit into the room, without returning through the opening; everyone would leave by the normal door. There was no need to approach the limits for people or baggage to verify a concrete use.

Livia was already on the other side. She had reached the room by the ordinary route with the material needed for the meeting. She did not gesture for him to hurry.

For a moment, he saw the hall side and the warehouse side as two perspectives his mind tried in vain to arrange in one drawing. This was no opening cut through the wall's thickness. Beyond lay a place he knew how to reach by walking another way.

Elias took the box in both hands. He did not lighten it to make the test more elegant. He wanted to know the agreed transit, not one improved by another exercise they would later have to remember to distinguish.

He lifted the load until its bottom cleared the edge. His grip pressed near the cut, but the gauze stayed put. Behind him, Marcel merely said he was watching.

Elias had imagined resistance, a temperature change sudden enough to close his eyes, or an instant when his body no longer knew where it was. The passage had produced nothing of the kind yet. His own expectation was filling the boundary with an unobserved danger.

He looked at Livia. Her hands were free and the landing surface ready. She was not smiling reassurance; she waited to see whether he needed to stop. Elias felt gratitude for the seriousness with which she let him perform a gesture they had both wanted to see.

He moved the box forward. Its far edge entered the antechamber and retained the same color. He still had to support its weight with his arms. The discovery was so simple he almost laughed. The need to take the next step without losing his grip restrained him.

Elias carried the box beyond the boundary, then advanced with his body.

He did not find the long instant his imagination had prepared. His foot touched the antechamber floor, and the rest followed. The smell changed: less work dust, more enclosed wood and lamp oil.

He turned.

Marcel was close enough to speak to him. For a moment the closeness made him forget that his mentor would have to take a different route if the connection failed.

“How do you feel?”

Elias set down the box before answering.

“Whole.”

“A start. Be more precise.”

No nausea. No loss of grip during the passage. He had not felt the apparatus drawing on his body, and using it had not given him the ability to open a threshold elsewhere.

Livia checked the box's contents with him. The pieces had arrived. The fact deserved a simple sentence.

When Marcel closed the opening, Elias saw the previous location return to occupy his side of the entrance entirely.

It closed before the ten minutes were up. The closure did not restore the charge.

In the register his mentor wrote the date: May twenty-third. Two openings used since the apparatus had been reactivated; one remaining from the initial reserve.

Elias remained before the register after the master finished writing. The first use's date did not change. The new one had consumed a possibility even though they had closed it before the maximum time.

He expected guilt for having wanted it so much. It did not come immediately. They had performed a chosen test within established conditions; its cost did not retrospectively turn it into waste. He need only remember that when the remaining charge seemed too precious for any use.

Livia asked him to move the box out of the entrance route. He lifted it beside the indicated chair. The load had passed from one side to the other without making any person present the owner of the space.

He remembered the warehouse worker and how he had asked them to leave the passage clear. The resemblance did not diminish the miracle. It made it habitable: after an impossible thing happened, someone still needed to enter without tripping over what they had left on the ground.

Marcel returned by the ordinary door. He took the time the route Elias had walked earlier required. That minimal delay showed precisely how the apparatus had changed their gesture, and how little of the rest of the world it had erased.

Elias sat only when the master reached the hall. Edith's place remained empty until the time she had agreed. Nobody filled it with an explanation about a lack of enthusiasm.

The second line mattered more than the wonder Elias would have liked to preserve without accounting.

Livia stayed beside Elias while the surface became merely a surface again. She asked whether he wanted to sit, and he said no. Not because he was not tired: he needed a few ordinary steps before explaining what had happened to his body.

He walked to the chair and back. The sound of shoes on the floor made him feel the time the gesture required with almost irritating clarity. The passage had not taught him to walk faster.

“I expected to notice more of the moment in between,” he said.

“More than you can describe?”

Elias considered the question. He had seen the place his load and foot reached change. He had lost no stretch of memory to fill afterward with solemn images.

“I expected it to be difficult to cross.”

“We prepared it so we wouldn't discover the difficulty while you were doing it.”

He nodded. The absence of disaster did not erase the preceding work. He put his palm to his chest: his heart was still faster than necessary, without proving injury or cosmic revelation.

Livia let him make the gesture and returned to the sheet. She asked him to describe the transit before listening to the account she could give from the other side. Elias found words shorter than he had imagined. Lift, advance, set down. They seemed unworthy of the wonder until he realized they let him recognize what he had actually done.

He wrote the three actions and the limits of his observation. He sought no adjective capable of restoring all his astonishment. That part would have to remain in his body a little longer.

Marcel then arrived by the ordinary entrance. Edith came last, at the time she had confirmed. She left her coat in the antechamber and stopped before the floor where the connection had recently opened.

“Is it over already?”

“The transit check is,” Elias answered. “The meeting isn't.”

She nodded. She did not seem offended at having missed a private spectacle.

They sat at the table. Twelve places, four people, one box Marcel moved beside his chair rather than leaving it at the center like an idol.

Elias opened the program.

He wanted to say something that would distinguish the meeting from every other work conversation. He looked at Livia, who was searching for a pen that had fallen into her case's lining, and abandoned the solemn sentence.

“The first thing is to say what we haven't verified.”

Edith rested her forearms on the table.

“I like that beginning.”

They had not verified an immobilized casualty. Nor a crowd. Nor operation after recharging. Nor a demigod. The test demonstrated the transit completed under those conditions, and the group would not offer a greater service merely because the word passage allowed people to imagine it.

Marcel added that recharging required a qualified Astrolabe III operator able to examine those marks. Neither his experience nor Elias's enthusiasm bridged the gap.

“Helren can't do it,” Elias said.

“Archive isn't Astrolabe,” Livia replied. “Even when someone reads well.”

Edith smiled, then indicated her notebook.

“Etienne authorized me to describe the condition of the limb and the observations I made myself. Not to hand over his entire file.”

Elias immediately recognized what he wanted to ask: that it be enough. But recovery did not owe him the favor of absolution.

“What do you need to prepare a usable extract?”

Edith opened her notebook. The meeting truly began at that moment, when the most extraordinary object remained closed and a person could set the limits of her help.
