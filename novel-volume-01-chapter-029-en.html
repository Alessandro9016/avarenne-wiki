Chapter 29 — Payday

The button appeared between his thumb and forefinger, black and perfectly whole.

Alexander looked at it and laughed softly.

He had created an object so small it seemed a mockery of his own past. The previous one had broken while he dressed. He could have remade the shirt, but at that moment the problem was a button, and he did not need to turn it into a demonstration of matter's origin.

He left it beside the sewing kit. No one was present. The room retained its silence, indifferent to a miracle destined to vanish beneath a jacket.

Before leaving the bedroom, Edran took the button between two fingers and held it beside the part of the shirt from which the previous one had detached. The diameter was right. The holes allowed a patient hand to sew it on without inventing a new method.

He felt a quiet satisfaction as he established that what he had made was not an object intended merely to look like the solution to the problem. It was something usable by whoever finished the work. The miracle did not need to preserve its spectacular quality in its use.

He left the button beside the broken fragment, distinct so nobody would take the wrong one. Then he went out in the shirt he already wore. There was no reason to create a clothing problem merely to repeat its solution before an audience.

In the corridor he found Maurice checking a service delivery. The butler looked up and asked whether he expected someone. Alexander said he would see Albin and asked when it would be convenient to resume the other household matters.

Maurice showed him a part of the day when service need not be interrupted twice. Edran accepted it. The world he had regained was no waiting room for his decisions; people lived in it who could work better if the duke remembered he occupied a place in time as well as hierarchy.

The administrator arrived with the updated statement. He had left earlier figures legible enough to show how they had changed. The boy Edran remembered would have seen his father's weight in those pages. Before anything else, he looked for the names of the functions to which this particular payment referred.

Later, Albin informed him the payment had been made.

Nine thousand eight hundred crowns for wages accrued at the residence, mills, and agencies. May twenty-sixth had arrived without requiring a sudden rescue of the ducal finances.

The extract of known transactions showed the balance after that expenditure: sixty-seven thousand seven hundred. Remaining commitments stood at seven thousand five hundred; the uncommitted portion remained sixty thousand two hundred. Paying an already committed sum did not subtract it a second time from the uncommitted funds.

“Good,” Alexander said.

Albin waited.

Edran realized how little there was to add to something owed that had been done.

“I don't intend to demand a ceremony.”

“I'm glad.”

The corners of the administrator's mouth moved slightly.

Albin showed him the summary of payments made and remaining commitments. Alexander followed the operation without confusing an already planned outlay with a new spending decision. The free amount was no reward for approving wages already owed.

“Nobody had to wait for an additional confirmation from me?”

The administrator described what he had handled within the recorded transactions' limits. He did not turn the statement into a promise that every financial problem of the estate was ended for ever. Edran nodded. He needed to know what had been done, not be told the household had ceased requiring attention.

For a moment the amount crossed his imagination as matter: metal, cloth, the labor of hands that would receive food, warmth, tools. He possessed abilities capable of changing how many of those things were produced. That was no reason to make an earned wage disappear inside an abstract consideration of his power.

“When someone asks for clarification, who can provide it?”

Albin indicated the ordinary channel for the relevant detail. Edran did not substitute his name for that work. He did not want to receive every doubt to become necessary to every answer.

The administrator closed the folder less hurriedly than on other occasions. Before leaving, he returned to a line to ensure the copy left behind was current. Alexander let him check. He had spent too long seeing efficiency as never hesitating, when sometimes a minute more in the right place sufficed.

Celine found him shortly afterward with his coat in hand and asked whether he was about to receive more people.

“I was thinking of going out. If you still want to show me where you'd decided to go.”

The girl studied him with the suspicion reserved for proposals born solely to mend a disappointment.

“You don't have to do it because I waited.”

“I can want to partly because of that too.”

She considered it. Then she asked Sabine whether they could change the order of what had been arranged for that afternoon.

Alexander did not answer for both of them.

They went into the residence's garden and continued along the route agreed with those responsible for access. Teren had arranged for Celine to be told which visits required confirmation. He had not turned every tree into an invisible guard or every outing into an escape to prevent.

The girl walked quickly when she found something to show him and slowed where she was unsure he would be interested.

Edran soon learned not to fill those slower moments with a question about her feelings.

Before a small pool, Celine stopped.

“You used to come here alone.”

Alexander remembered. A corner near enough to the house not to seem an absence, quiet enough to let the young duke remain unobserved as he tried to become his father.

“Yes.”

“You haven't come back now.”

“Not yet.”

The water's reflection returned the body's dark hair and golden eyes. For an instant he saw the boy who had inherited a house too large for him and no useful instruction for grief.

Celine sat on the dry rim. He stayed beside her without seeking a pose the memories would make reassuring.

“Has Etienne come back?”

“He's spoken to me. I don't know when he'll want to again.”

“I'll write to him myself.”

Edran waited.

“Not about his father,” she added.

“You don't need to explain as though you were asking permission not to hate him.”

Celine picked up a leaf from the pool's edge. She turned it between her fingers, then left it away from the water.

“Sometimes you say different things.”

It was not an accusation, which made it harder.

“So do you.”

“I haven't been ill.”

Edran did not offer her a false symmetry. He looked at the path they had followed.

“I don't know how to take you back to before.”

Celine was silent. Farther away came the wheels of a wheelbarrow. The house's life had not stopped requiring work merely because the duke wanted to speak to his sister.

“I didn't ask you to,” she said at last.

Celine turned toward the house. From the pool's edge, a window seemed close enough to reach in a few steps, but hedges and a change of level lay between. Edran recognized the perspective and let it remain a thought without demonstrating it.

“Do you still like this place?” she asked.

He could have answered with the duke's memory. It was whole, able to restore the light and scents of other seasons. It could not tell him whether the person here liked the place now.

“I like sitting here with you.”

Celine looked at the water suspiciously.

“That's an answer that avoids the place.”

Edran laughed. He said he liked the dry edge and hearing the household's work from the pool without needing to be inside. He did not turn every detail into a profound truth. Celine showed him a spot from which, before the sun moved, a reflection appeared on the wall.

It was something she knew and he had not yet looked at. Edran felt an almost childish pleasure. The boy's memories let him inhabit this body without pretending ignorance, but did not exhaust what the world might show him after awakening.

Celine glanced at the path and rose. She did not announce the walk had done her good. She said there was another stretch to show him before returning. Alexander followed, letting her decide what deserved the next piece of time.

They continued walking. At a fork he habitually took the direction preserved in the boy's memories. Celine touched his arm.

“This way.”

Alexander changed course.

Before going inside, Celine stopped to remove a leaf caught in her hem. Alexander waited in the wall's shelter. Through the door came the sound of preparations for the next service, a cadence of steps the duke's memories already recognized.

“Maurice says we must give warning before changing the time now,” she said.

“He is right.”

“You too.”

Edran nodded. It was easy to want the household to respect his time and call only what was granted to him consideration. Celine had found a way to return a rule even to the person most able to forget it.

The leaf would not detach without taking a thread. She stopped pulling and used her fingers to free it. Alexander watched without intervening. When she removed the small obstacle, Celine straightened with satisfaction unrelated to her brother's illness or the accusation against the baron.

“Next time I'm going that way,” she said, indicating the stretch they had left.

It was concrete enough not to resemble a promise that everyone had finally returned to living well. Edran accepted it for what it was. They could go out again; he need not decide the meaning of every future walk now.

He opened the door and let her pass. The house received them with its own sounds. For the first time since awakening, returning felt like the end of something he had chosen to do that afternoon, not a return to where everyone waited for his crisis to be explained.

On returning he found Nela ready to conclude one of the shifts that had accompanied the crisis. He thanked her without making gratitude an obligation to stay.

She told him to ask Mirel about what still required a doctor and the household about what finally required an ordinary day.

“And if I can't tell the difference?”

“Start by asking.”

Before Nela left, Alexander asked her to convey his thanks to those who had shared the crisis shifts. Not a summons: nobody should have to return to duty to receive a courteous word. She nodded and resumed her departure.

That evening the button was sewn into place by a hand that knew how. Alexander did not create another sixty thousand things to prove he did not need it.
