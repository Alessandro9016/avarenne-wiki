Chapter 67 — Her Aunt's Letter

Celine recognized her aunt's envelope by the stain before she even saw her own name. A gray thumbprint crossed the flap; Yselle had tried to clean her hands before writing and lost patience halfway through.

The book had remained in Alexander's hands. Celine looked at it, then returned to the letter.

“You carried the book all the way to Lethorne?”

“Yes.”

“Then you can carry it back to the library. The shelf hasn't moved.”

He looked down at the spine, and for a moment Celine thought she had offended him. Instead he smiled and set off. He still wore the dust of the journey, a pale line across his dark cuff. He had come home before dinner, tired enough to sit when Maurice suggested it, but not tired enough to escape her questions. He had told her about the road, Martin, the workshop. He had said too little about the pumps.

Celine followed, slipping a finger beneath the flap. She did not want him watching while she read. Nor did she want to go to her room. She chose the armchair by the window and pointed to the opposite chair with her foot.

The letter began with a complaint about Celine's garden design: the curved path forced anyone pushing a wheelbarrow to go all the way around a pool. Her aunt had added a rectangle in the margin and written: The poor man can get through here, unless you mean to work him to death. Celine swallowed a laugh. That passage spoiled the surprise of the avenue, but she had not thought of the wheelbarrow.

Then the handwriting grew smaller. Yselle wrote that Alexander had seen the machines, that they now had three, that one was already working at the Mutual. She also described a breakdown and specified that the part which failed had been old. No description of blood. No name for the person who had been injured. The next sentence concerned a dinner Martin had burned; Celine realized her aunt had put it there on purpose.

Alexander’s message had arrived the day before. It had already mentioned Noemi and the healed cut; it had not said who had healed her. Now Celine searched her aunt’s letter for the part her brother had left out.

She reread the stretch between the breakdown and the dinner.

“Where were you?”

Alexander put the book on the little table. “At the Mutual.”

“When it broke.”

“Yes.”

“Were you hurt?”

“No.”

She examined his hands, his neck above the collar, the line of his hair. She found no bandages. Of course, he could have had something dressed beneath his clothes. The irritating thing about grown-ups was that there was always another way for them to be right without having told the whole truth.

“Aunt Yselle says you were useful.”

“I'm glad she didn't write underfoot.”

She did not laugh. He stopped smiling.

“Someone was cut. We helped her. The pipe was repaired, and the pump worked the following day.”

“Did everyone help, or did you do something strange?”

The question came out louder than she intended. From the corridor came the sound of a tray. Celine waited for it to move away, clutching the letter on her knees. She had not decided whether she wanted the answer to be yes.

“I did something,” Alexander said. “Did Yselle tell you about the three machines?”

“She wrote that you have them. Not that you bought them.”

“I didn't buy them.”

The evening seemed to shift. The window reflected their two faces and the lamp, still unlit, ordinary things that had not received the news. Celine turned to look at her brother directly.

“You made them?”

“Using Yselle's design and her model. I have an ability that allows me to create matter.”

Her first questions were foolish and came all at once. Could he make a ship? A cake? A horse? Had their aunt seen? Celine bit her tongue before saying the third; she did not want him choosing the easy question and pretending they were all the same.

“How big?” she asked.

“The pumps are taller than your desk.”

“Three?”

“Three.”

She imagined a machine growing like a plant out of the floor and immediately erased the image. Perhaps it had appeared all at once. Perhaps it had made an enormous noise. Her brother sat with a fold of his trousers caught against the chair leg, and nothing in his stories had prepared her for this fact.

“Why didn't you write to tell me?”

“I wanted to tell you in person.”

“Convenient. You get three days to think about it, and I have to come up with something clever at once.”

He shifted his leg, freeing the fabric. “You don't have to.”

That answer made her angrier than a reprimand. She looked down at the letter. Yselle had written about her, too: if she still wanted to see a workshop, she would now find a great deal of disorder and something worth the journey. No promise of a date. An invitation real enough to hold on to.

Celine read it aloud to him. Alexander answered too quickly.

“For now, we'd better wait.”

“Wait for what?”

“Until they've finished the trials.”

“You said it works.”

“One machine works. The workshop is still a workplace.”

“I know. I wasn't expecting them to let me stand underneath the machine.”

She took the paper by one corner and straightened it, because her hand was shaking and she did not want him to notice. Alexander watched the movement. That had changed: before the coma he had missed certain things, or pretended to. Now he sometimes looked at her as if he had all the time in the world. It was not always pleasant.

“There was an accident,” he said.

“You've just told me. And you were there. I can't even go afterward?”

“That isn't what I meant.”

“Then what did you mean?”

He had to fall silent. Celine felt a petty satisfaction and lost it at once. She did not want to beat him. She wanted the journey, the carriage windows open, her aunt showing her something without first asking whether she was too tired. She wanted a story to tell that did not begin beside a bed.

A sheet from her afternoon's work lay on the little table. She moved it between them. She had copied the contents pages of two books on elementary mechanics, marking those the library owned. Beside one heading she had drawn a large, ugly question mark.

“I don't just want to watch sparks. I looked for the chapter on levers. The older book leaves out all the steps. The other one uses signs I don't understand.”

Alexander picked up the sheet, not the letter. “This is a ratio.”

“I know what a ratio is. I don't know why the weight of the arm disappears here.”

He read the line. “I can't tell you from a contents page either.”

At last, an answer that did not make her feel small. Celine got up to fetch the volume. She had to pull it from a row packed too tightly; a second book slid against her wrist, and Alexander reached out. She caught it first.

“It wasn't an emergency.”

“I saw.”

“You still made that face.”

This time he laughed, briefly. She set the volume down and found the page. The drawing showed a horizontal lever and two square weights. Apparently the wood weighed nothing. Alexander found the sentence on the previous page; Celine read it twice, annoyed. She had skipped that very sentence.

“Don't say it.”

“I haven't said anything.”

“You were about to.”

“I was about to ask whether you want to write to Martin.”

The suggestion caught her off guard. Writing to Martin meant showing her question to someone who taught for a living. He might answer with an exercise, or discover in three lines that she had misunderstood everything else as well. On the other hand, he had never treated her like a vase to be moved away from the edge.

Celine picked up the pencil she had left in the book as a marker. Its point was broken. She searched the drawer for a sharpener and found a ribbon, two buttons, and her receipt for returning a volume she had believed lost. Fortunately, the library contained those embarrassments too. Alexander passed her a pencil from the table without comment. She drew an arrow toward the lever arm and erased her first question. She would not write that the book was wrong. She would ask whether the wood's weight had been ignored only in the example or whether there was a way to include it in the calculation. That seemed a question less easily dismissed.

“I can write to Aunt Yselle and put in a sheet for him.”

“You can.”

“And the visit?”

Alexander leaned back. She recognized the temptation to postpone, that pause in which an adult searched for something else to do. This time she let him speak.

“Let's write to Yselle and ask when she can have you. With someone accompanying you, on a day she chooses. Not tomorrow morning.”

“I didn't ask for tomorrow morning.”

“I know.”

“You didn't just say yes to make me stop?”

“I said we can arrange it. If there's a reason to change the day afterward, I'll tell you.”

It was less than she wanted and much more than she had possessed five minutes earlier. Celine retrieved the letter. The middle crease had grown soft. On the back, Yselle had added that her nephew ate two portions of something Martin cooked after criticizing its appearance. That was something she could read aloud without consequences.

She did. Alexander protested at the absence of a reliable description of the dish. Celine asked whether his ability could produce a less ugly dinner; he answered that he would have to know the design. She laughed properly, and the sound seemed almost outrageous in the library where, for weeks, she had learned to keep her voice down.

Maurice came to light the lamps. He paused at the threshold only long enough to ask whether they needed anything else. Celine requested paper, then remembered it was already in the drawer and blushed. The butler did not point it out.

Alexander returned to the book about levers, but she did not begin her reply immediately. Inside her folder, underneath the garden drawing, lay another sheet. She had written Etienne Ravelle's name and then three lines, none of which she liked. His father's name appeared in conversations that stopped when she entered. She knew enough to hate that silence and not enough to fill it.

“If I write to Etienne,” she said, “do I have to let you read all of it?”

Alexander raised his head. The pause was different from the one about the workshop.

“No. But if there's anything about the proceedings, don't try to settle it on your own.”

“I don't want to settle it. I wanted to ask how he is.”

“Then ask him.”

He had not said Etienne was innocent of everything, or that his father would come home. Celine would have found either answer unbearable. She looked at the three lines: they discussed the weather and the garden, as if they could throw a sheet over the entire room.

“I don't know whether he'll answer.”

“Neither do I.”

She left that sheet in the folder. She did not want to write every letter of her life in one evening. To her aunt she first described the wheelbarrow, admitting only that perhaps the side passage would be useful. Then she asked about the visit. For Martin she prepared a precise question and a copy of the drawing with the square weights.

Alexander sat on the other side of the table. The lamp lit one hand; the dust of the road still marked his other sleeve. Celine tried to picture that hand making metal appear and could not. She did not ask him again. At the bottom of the sheet she added a line for Yselle: I want to see the real pump, not just the drawing.

Before she finished, she picked up her garden again. With her ruler she opened a passage beside the pool. It was too straight and annoyed her, so she drew an arch of roses over it. The wheelbarrow would still get through. That, she thought, was something she could allow without losing everything else.
