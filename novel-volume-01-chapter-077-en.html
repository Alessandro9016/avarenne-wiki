Chapter 77 — Work Paid For

Marcel made him count his payment before discussing the pump.

Elias arranged the coins on the sheet. Six crowns. The second had a nicked edge; he turned it over without knowing why and put it with the others.

“That’s everything,” he said.

The master pushed the receipt toward him. Elias signed where indicated. The work had a completion date, his name and an amount he could actually put in his pocket this time. He did not have to wait for somebody to decide whether his contribution had been grateful enough.

“Has Yselle confirmed the handover?”

“This morning. Noemi too.” Marcel collected the receipt. “Did you leave the instructions with the right copy?”

“The dry one, in the cover.”

“Then stop looking for another reason not to take the money.”

Elias wrapped the coins. Their little weight pulled at his jacket pocket, giving him a pleasure so precise that he smiled.

On the bench, the old regulator waited with its cover off. Marcel had arranged the springs in a box and found something he had not wanted to show him before settling the account: a thin wire caught beneath a grip. Elias recognized one of the repairs left unfinished during their visits to the Mutual.

“This isn’t included in the six crowns,” the master said.

“I was hoping not.”

“It’s included in your ordinary work.”

Elias sighed and removed his jacket. He put it well away from the oil, pocket uppermost. Marcel saw and said nothing.

The wire came free on the second attempt. Elias sought the source of the play, compared the seat with the neighboring component and understood where the edge had deformed. A small correction would suffice. Exactly the sort of work that, a few weeks earlier, would have seemed something to finish before he could begin his life.

Now it felt good to have an answer in his hands.

Marcel checked the result. The regulator moved again without catching. Elias closed it and realized he still had a question.

“I’d like to prepare for the second stage.”

The master did not look up at once. He finished putting the spare spring in its place, closed the box and only then looked at him.

“Since when?”

“Since before the Mutual.”

“I asked since when, not what you’re defending yourself against.”

Elias rubbed his thumb against the edge of the bench. “Since we tested the Hinge. First I wanted to understand why it worked. Then I thought about what stays mine when I don’t have that object.”

Marcel let him finish. Upstairs, a window struck as it opened; somebody propped it with an object that rolled away immediately afterward. They both looked up, waited for a complaint and returned to the bench.

“You want to travel through,” the master said.

“I want to learn to open a passage. Not just send a key.”

Spoken aloud, the desire seemed larger than when he had kept it among the things he would do someday. He had watched Marcel walk through the artifact’s opening. He had not watched a future version of himself emerge by right of apprenticeship.

“And if I told you to wait?”

“I’d want to know what I was waiting for.”

The answer brought an expression to the master’s face that Elias seldom saw: not approval, but interest unburdened by a fault to repair.

Marcel cleared part of the bench. He made two small marks, one beside the tray and the other near the spring box. Then he picked up a nut and laid it in his palm.

“Look here.”

The air above the first mark acquired an edge.

Elias bent closer before deciding whether it was wise. There was no frame: a portion of the bench seemed to look at the world from a different angle. Beyond its margin he saw the spring box much too close. Raising his head, he saw it again where it belonged.

Two views. One box.

Marcel introduced the nut into the opening. The metal disappeared from his palm and fell from the other end into the tray. Elias heard a single impact. The distance it had traveled was not the one his eyes still assigned to the bench.

Both openings closed. The master lowered his hand and rubbed his fingers.

“Not the Hinge,” Elias said.

“No.”

The derivative remained safely stored elsewhere. One of its initial openings was still available; that nut had not consumed it. Elias watched the space become uniform again and tried to separate what he had seen from the exercise he already knew.

“I could have transferred the nut.”

“With Small Transfer. Here it went through a threshold. I invested the ends myself.”

“Can I sense them?”

“They aren’t there anymore.”

Elias straightened, disappointed enough to make the master laugh.

“First I teach you to ask during the demonstration. Then we’ll see about the rest.”

Marcel retrieved the nut from the tray and put it in his hand. It was cold, one side dirty with oil. It retained no spark, inscription or answer he could extract by squeezing harder.

Marcel cleared the tray and had him put the nut on the bench.

“Now yours.”

Elias reached the little object and its destination. Small Transfer shortened the wait between the two positions: the nut left the wood and arrived in the tray, offering no edge into which he could introduce something else. A single sound again. The result resembled the demonstration enough to have deceived him before he had seen it.

He extended a hand toward the starting point. Marcel touched his wrist.

“What are you looking for?”

“An opening.”

“Did you make one?”

Elias lowered his hand. He had transferred the object; he was looking for the door of a different exercise. He picked up the nut from the tray again, and this time the metal seemed heavier.

“So the passage has to remain while something crosses it.”

“The passage has to exist. Then size, distance and duration will each demand their own work. Wanting to put something bigger through isn’t enough.”

He remembered the caliper clenched in front of the duke’s pumps. He had not wanted a second machine: he had wanted a way to understand what the first put before him. That question was still his now. Marcel gave him time to formulate it without filling the silence.

“You don’t need to convince me you’re interested,” he said. “You need to tell me where you want to begin.”

Elias tried to hold all the answers together and lost most of them. The Mutual’s handrail returned clearly: the point beneath his palm, his unbalanced body, Noemi needing to stay upright. He could have wished for more strength, more space, a world with fewer things to hold up.

“With the reference,” he said. “When I keep that steady, at least the rest stops carrying me away.”

Marcel nodded. He took a sheet and asked him to distinguish the exercise from what he had done with the other hand. Elias began with the handrail, not the key. The master stopped him at one position, had him repeat the movement without investing anything and moved the stool preventing him from stepping back.

On the second attempt, Elias realized he had tightened his arm when he only needed to change his footing. On the third he could hold the reference without stiffening his shoulder. He had gained no threshold. He had found something to practice the next day.

Marcel wrote down two instructions and left room for his observations.

“Preparing for the second stage isn’t only this,” he said. “We’ll discuss access too, and what you need to face it. Not today between one repair and the next.”

“When?”

The master looked at the calendar. He chose an interval still free the following week and marked it. Elias copied the note into his notebook. At last he had asked for a date without waiting for desire to become an emergency.

When he put on his jacket, the pocket reminded him of his payment.

A shop at the corner displayed used instruments. He stopped at the window. A small level had an almost intact case; beside it, the price of an incomplete device promised a bargain to anyone who could obtain the missing parts. Elias followed the place where its second support ought to have been.

He could have gone in and asked.

He took out the sheet on which Marcel had written the exercise. For that, the workshop and a little agreed time were enough. He put the paper away and walked on before imagining how easy it would be to find a support he had not yet begun to look for.

Lise met him on the stairs. She carried a folder so full she had to support it underneath and asked whether he had finished or merely decided to take a break.

“Paid,” Elias said.

She stopped on the step. “The six?”

He nodded. His sister showed him her free hand, open.

“To help you carry them.”

Elias laughed and took the folder instead. Lise surrendered the weight with a relief she tried to conceal. On top lay a drawing of a facade with an enormous erasure over the entrance.

“You changed the entrance?”

“The drawing. The entrance knows nothing about it.”

They went up. Adrien sat at the table and had left a place free without knowing it would be needed for a folder. Lise resumed her work; Elias laid the payment before his father and removed the wrapping.

Adrien looked at the coins. “The pumps?”

“The additional work. It’s finished.”

His father counted them once and pushed them back. “Where do you keep them?”

Elias had prepared three answers about spending them. The question made him look for the box he already used for his money. He found it beneath a book and brought it to the table. Inside lay small accounts that would buy nothing merely because he regarded them more optimistically.

He added the coins without deciding the fate of the entire sum.

“I’d like to contribute to these days’ expenses,” he said.

Adrien removed his glasses. He neither refused out of pride nor held out his hand from habit. He told him which purchases they would make together after comparing the week’s account. Lise asked whether that included a lamp that would not force all three of them to crowd beneath the same light.

“It includes discussing it,” their father replied.

“Excellent. I’ll start now.”

Elias let her show him the page. The erasure was not on the drawing she would submit, but on a trial copy. The entrance was wide; the problem concerned hinges that would make a leaf strike the step. He traced the arc with the pencil and stopped before correcting it for her.

Lise moved the pivot, considered the new movement and took the pencil from his fingers.

“Now I can make my own mistakes.”

Their father smiled over his work. Elias closed the box, put it away and realized he did not mind leaving a sum without a destination. He had asked for an exercise and a date; money could remain money until he had something to buy.

He had to go back to Yselle to collect a copy of the handover. He picked up his jacket and found the nut in the notebook pocket. He had carried it away without noticing.

He showed Lise.

“This isn’t the change.”

“I hope they paid you for what you understood too.”

Elias thought of the two openings above the bench. He would return the nut to Marcel the next morning. For that evening, he liked having something small that had really gone to the other side.
