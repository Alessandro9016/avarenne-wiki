Chapter 31 — On the Other Side of the Door

Lise had packed the model better than Elias packed his own instruments.

He noticed only after offering for the second time to check the ties. His sister handed him the box, waited for him to support it, then took it back without opening it.

“See? It holds.”

“I was about to say it looks well done.”

“You can say that without looking for a mistake first.”

Adrien had checked that the bottom would not give way under the weight. Lise thanked him without offering a second chance to redo the packing. She could lift and open the box herself: if they strengthened it with more material, she would have luggage larger than the model.

Elias looked at the knot joining the ties. He had already found at least two other ways to make it. They were not necessarily better; they were the ways his hands knew.

“I'll get my jacket,” he said, abandoning the third inspection.

Lise made room for him and gathered her papers. Their father reminded her where he had tucked the one received by post. She showed it without opening every other page to prove herself ready.

At the threshold, Elias realized he did not know whether she had eaten breakfast. He was about to ask in a tone that would involve Adrien too; he stopped and merely asked whether she wanted another moment.

“I want to go before I manage to postpone it in my head.”

It was a fear her brother understood without calling it inexperience. In recent days he had imagined the hearing so often that its not having arrived yet was almost reassuring. While a meeting remained future, he could prepare to answer even questions nobody would ask.

He went downstairs ahead of Lise, then moved aside to give her room for the box. She did not cling to his arm; she checked her load and continued. A flap of covering rubbed her skirt and she changed her grip before reaching the street.

Elias learned their pace. At his usual speed, Lise would have to hurry with something she could not hold like a folder. He slowed without announcing he was doing it for her.

At the narrowest stretch of pavement, they let approaching people pass. His sister held the model against her hip and resumed only when its edge no longer risked a collision. None of these precautions resembled an examination; they were already using the work of bringing something into the world without breaking it.

They walked to the interview. The morning of the twenty-eighth was warm enough to make him regret his jacket, and cool enough in the shade to prevent him from complaining decisively.

Lise held the box against her hip. She had brought the reply she had received, her notebook, and what had been requested for the meeting. She was not bringing a brother to present as supplementary evidence of her intelligence.

Elias asked whether she wanted him to wait inside or outside.

She looked at the door, then at the street.

“Outside. If they want to know about me, I need to be able to answer.”

“All right.”

“And if I take too long, it doesn't mean it went badly.”

“Agreed.”

Lise hesitated.

“Or too little time.”

Elias smiled. That small contradiction seemed the most familiar thing in the world.

He held the box while she adjusted her sleeve, then gave it back. The girl went inside.

Waiting without knowing how long a corridor was cost him an absurd desire to measure it. He could have taken its geometry if he had the right access; he would not have learned what was happening to Lise.

He leaned against the wall and left the power quiet.

A man asked whether it was the right entrance. Elias read his appointment notice only after being shown it, indicated the door, and wished him a good morning. He managed not to ask his own chances of returning his sister to a happy home that day.

Across the street two children were rolling a hoop. Each time it fell, the smaller one accused the paving of having the wrong slope. Elias could have supplied a precise reading of the gradient. He chose not to become the first adult to certify the boy's complaint.

The man with the summons lingered by the door. He was searching for a passage and did not seem ready to ask for more help. Elias asked whether he wanted the time checked too.

The man offered the page with his finger on the relevant line. He had already chosen what to share; Elias read it without seeking the rest of the matter. He indicated the reference and returned the sheet.

“Waiting for someone?”

“My sister.”

The man looked at the door, then at the box Elias no longer held. He could not reconstruct the interview from that absence. He wished him well and crossed the entrance when called.

Elias was left with a good wish he did not know how to answer. He had not asked for it. It contained no promise, assessment, or expertise to verify. Someone who did not know Lise had simply wanted her morning to end well.

He stepped away from the wall to stretch a leg. Every time the door opened, he could see part of the corridor, but no image let him count his sister's remaining minutes. He caught himself seeking a conclusion for his own case in the faces of those leaving.

A girl emerged with papers beneath her chin, searching her bag. One corner slipped; she caught it with a grimace and crossed the street without looking at Elias. He followed her until a cart hid her. She was the third person out. None had brought Lise back to him.

He returned to the place Lise would see on leaving. He had chosen it to be found easily, not to watch the entrance from every side. For now, that was the work she had asked of him.

Time passed.

Elias thought of the thirty-first, then the commercial comparison, then his family. Every thought tried to reenter the room where someone else was waiting.

The child with the hoop managed to propel it far enough to make the older one chase. Elias watched briefly, then looked back at the entrance. The game had improved without his contribution, and that pleased him unexpectedly.

He had spent weeks seeking what he should have prevented. Waiting for his sister, he realized how that question had devoured the rest of his days too. He could be responsible for work without being charged with making everything he witnessed succeed.

The door opened again. The man with the summons came out and nodded without stopping to report the outcome. Elias replied. They had not built an intimacy that required sharing everything merely because they had waited in the same place.

Sunlight reached part of the wall and Elias moved into shade. Lise could still find him. He need not make waiting more uncomfortable to demonstrate his participation.

When the hearing notes returned to mind, he let himself follow them briefly. He knew the folder's contents; the order of witnesses was not yet up to him. He did not take out his notebook to repeat the morning of the thirty-first while someone else lived the twenty-eighth.

A box being set down made him look up. He did not know whether the sound came from the relevant corridor and stopped trying to identify it like a measurement. He stayed where he could be seen, awaiting the voice entitled to tell him how it had gone.

When Lise emerged, the model was still whole.

“They made me change the weight,” she said.

“And?”

She showed him one of the ties.

“I moved this.”

Elias nodded too quickly. He stopped and let her finish.

They had asked how she reached the solution, what she would modify with other material, and why she had chosen that structure. Lise also recounted the attempt that sent pebbles beneath the cupboard. She did not conceal the help she had received. She could distinguish the cloth Adrien brought from the point of support she had found herself.

“Did they say when they'll answer?”

“They'll write. Not today.”

She walked a few paces before adding:

“There was one question I didn't know.”

“Which?”

Lise explained it. Elias could have improvised an answer convincing enough to make the problem smaller. He realized he was not certain either.

“We can look it up when you want.”

“Not to do the interview over.”

“To know.”

His sister finally let him carry the box. It was not a promotion to the role of savior; she was hot and wanted her hands free.

They passed an instrument shop. Elias paused to look at a caliper in the window, then realized Lise was looking at him.

“Three days,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Are you afraid?”

Elias raised one side of the box to avoid a passerby.

“Yes.”

Lise did not tell him everything would go well. Instead she asked whether he wanted her to wait outside.

For a moment the question made him lose the order of the things he had prepared to explain himself.

“I'd like knowing I can come back and tell you.”

Lise nudged his sleeve with her elbow. The box in Elias’s hand answered with a small sound. For an instant they both stopped.

“It hasn't broken,” she said.

“Do you know, or do you hope?”

His sister smiled, recognizing the kind of answer he would have given. This time they resumed walking before unwrapping it in the middle of the street.

Before climbing, Lise took the box back. She wanted to set it on the table and undo the ties herself. Elias surrendered the load without offering a reason to do it for her again. He followed her upstairs, close enough to open the door.

On the landing Elias heard Adrien’s chair scrape. Lise raised one corner of the box past the handle and asked him to hold the door. Their father reached them before they were both inside, but did not reach for the model. He looked at his daughter, waiting to hear what had happened.

At home, Lise put the box on the table without immediately opening it. Adrien took a chair and listened with his hands away from the model. Elias noticed: their father could have checked every joint again while she spoke, turning the morning into a verification of work done at home.

He let her tell it. When Lise reached the unanswered question, he did not say it surely would not matter. He asked whether she wanted to seek the reasoning in her notebook. She answered that she wanted to eat first.

Elias put his folder on the shelf. He still had work for the thirty-first, but no secret deadline forbade staying for a meal. When his sister resumed describing the interview, he could listen without seeking a similar enough question in his own future to redirect the conversation toward himself.

The box stayed closed until Lise chose to undo the ties. The model had traveled and undergone an examination. It did not need to succeed a second time to prove her account true to her brother.

That evening, before returning to his notes for the hearing, he found a new line on the sheet with the question neither of them had known how to solve. His sister had already begun looking.
