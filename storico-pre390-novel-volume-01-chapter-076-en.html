Chapter 76 — The Morning News

Varek took the newspaper out of Sybille’s hands before she had finished reading.

“That one’s sold.”

“I haven’t dirtied it.”

“You’ve opened it.”

The distributor ran two fingers down the fold, closed the sheet and tucked it under the arm of the waiting man. She accepted a coin, found the change without looking and called to a boy tying a bundle of copies too tightly. Sybille stepped back to let the customer pass. It was noon, and the sun had brought the queue to the wrong side of the stall.

Varek did not move into the shade. She was sixty-one, with a basket to watch and a street corner somebody else would occupy before she finished complaining about the heat.

“The top one,” she told the boy. “You’ve bent the pages.”

He loosened the string. A copy slipped free and Sybille stopped it with her shoe without stepping on it. She bent to pick it up.

“I’ll buy this one.”

“You will now.”

She paid. Varek put the coin into a different pocket and finished checking the bundle. Her hands were dry, two nails broken so short that merely looking at them made Sybille uncomfortable. Once she had seen this woman veil a luminous signal in shadow, leaving the lamp alight. Now she watched her fight a piece of string.

“We weren’t supposed to meet here,” she said quietly.

“You chose to come. I’m working.”

The boy shouldered the bundle and asked whether he ought to stop at the printing house before returning. Varek answered with three addresses and a reprimand about some wrong change. He left without looking at Sybille. A good sign, or merely a boy with other things to do.

The stall remained between them. Varek pushed over a copy of an old edition, open at an advertisement for a house.

Sybille did not touch it.

“That isn’t what I’m looking for.”

“I’m not offering it to you. Put your paper on it. Cover the lines.”

The sheet underneath carried a list of business addresses. None was a private home; Sybille recognized a paper merchant and the entrance used for workshop deliveries. Varek had written each name beneath a printed heading, leaving enough space between the letters to make them look like part of an account.

“Ilian asked about the surveyor,” she said.

“I know.”

“He asked. He isn’t bringing him along.”

“I know that too.”

The distributor stopped her finger on a line. “Then explain it to whoever sent you. I’ve been asked when he’ll arrive. There is no arrival. There’s a request for an inspection after hours and somebody who hasn’t answered yet.”

Sybille followed the queue reflected in the neighboring shop window. A woman carried an empty basket; a man was looking for the sales page without wanting to buy the paper. Nobody was listening closely enough to justify an escape. She disliked realizing that she was already choosing where to disappear.

“Mael doesn’t want another supposition.”

“Neither do I. That’s why I’m not selling him one.”

Varek slipped the sheet out from beneath the newspaper, folded it in the right place and put it away. The words lasted less time than the movements required to serve another customer. He wanted a piece of news he had heard shouted at the bridge. Varek showed him the page number and told him that headline belonged to yesterday. The man argued that it ought to cost less, then.

“You should have read it yesterday too,” she replied.

Sybille hid her smile behind the copy she had bought.

Once they were alone again, Varek leaned forward a little. “How often do you think I can ask about the same deliveries? Once, I’m a woman who knows the street. The third time, I’m a woman people remember.”

“Did they answer?”

“They asked if I wanted work for a nephew.”

“And you?”

“I told them if I had a capable nephew, I’d make him work here.”

A wheel struck the curb. Varek pulled the basket toward her before a cart could clip it; Sybille caught the other handle. For a moment they supported the weight of the newspapers together. The driver shouted something intended as an apology. The distributor answered loudly enough to make two people turn.

She did not use Silence. She wanted the driver to know he had heard her.

With the basket back in place, she checked the bundle beneath the stall. She drew out a folded sheet and opened it on her own side only.

“This has been paid,” she said.

Sybille recognized the creditor’s signature, not the amount. A stroke crossed the sum owed; below, somebody had written that nothing remained to collect on that account. Varek rubbed the margin with her thumb, checking that the ink would not come off.

“Then you’ve received something.”

“I’ve stopped owing something. I prefer the distinction.”

She returned the sheet to an inside pocket, close to her chest. The calmness of her voice did not keep Sybille from remembering how still that hand had been. Varek would not have shown the receipt unless she needed to believe in the result.

“Would you rather have money next time?”

“I’d rather choose what to pay. But you don’t decide that.”

Sybille looked beyond the stall. Her own house was still for sale; Varek’s settled account already had a paper to keep. She felt a brief, unpleasant envy, then thought of Ilian returning to argue about work he had finished. She did not know which of the three was closest to obtaining what they had been promised.

A group of boys ran up and stopped in front of the illustrated sheets. Varek let them look until the first put a wet finger on the paper. She moved it away with the back of her hand without interrupting the argument about price. One of the smallest asked whether the duke had really made a factory appear.

“Three pumps,” she said. “They’ll be selling the factory tomorrow.”

The boys laughed. The one who had asked stayed serious. Varek showed him the available article, found a copy with a torn edge and let him look while she served the others. Sybille waited until they were far enough away.

“Don’t buy rumors about the duke.”

“I’m not buying them.”

“You’re collecting them.”

“This is my street. I hear them even when you people aren’t paying me.”

Sybille had no answer to that plural. Mael could distinguish permissible news from inconvenient news inside a closed room; at the stall, a child had just asked aloud precisely what he no longer wanted to ask.

Varek pointed to a sheet at the bottom of the basket. “This one says the machine has stopped.”

“Which machine?”

“Exactly. It might be a workshop test. It might be a pump that hasn’t worked in years. Whoever tells the story can’t distinguish the Mutual from the street behind it. I’m not going to ask an injured person whether the duke has come back to fix the water.”

Sybille opened her purchased paper and found the workshop news. Nothing told her where Elias would be that evening. A nobleman’s name filled enough space to make the names of those carrying tools seem tiny.

“We need to know who answers Ilian,” she said.

“When they answer.”

“That too.”

Varek looked up. “It isn’t the same request.”

It was irritating because it was true. Following somebody who delivered a letter did not mean knowing the text inside. Seeing a technician enter a workshop did not prove he had accepted a job. Sybille had charged men for smaller distinctions they could not afford; now hers were coming back with a newspaper.

“The business address,” she said. “Not the house.”

Varek went still for an instant. “Whose house?”

“Anybody’s.”

The woman glanced at the pocket where Sybille kept the folded particulars. She could not see what paper it was; what showed was Sybille’s instinctive gesture to protect it from the basket’s edge. Sybille moved her hand.

“This assignment doesn’t include finding where people sleep.”

“Write that down.”

The reply came at once. Varek picked up a pencil stub and offered it wooden end first.

Sybille looked at the blank space on her newspaper. Mael could easily ignore the sentence if he wanted to; without it, though, there would not even be a sentence for him to lie against. She wrote business addresses, then added delivery of the reply and stopped. She had almost written the name of somebody to watch. She did not yet know who would carry the message.

Varek read without taking the sheet.

“I can do that while I deliver. If I have to leave my route, we discuss it first.”

“Don’t send the boy.”

“The boy carries newspapers. I haven’t asked you how to use him.”

Sybille felt the rebuke and let it arrive whole. Smiling to pretend she had been doing a kindness would not help. The boy was already coming back along the street, without his bundle, rattling coins in a box. Varek made him count out the change in front of her.

One coin was missing.

He protested, searched his pocket and found two. One belonged to him. She made him explain which, and he blushed to his ears before showing her the mark on its edge. Sybille waited. There was nothing quick about a person who knew her own stall.

Once the account balanced, Varek sent him to eat and closed the box.

A man stopped to ask for a paper on credit. His fingers were gray with dust and he carried a rolled apron beneath his arm. Varek opened a small notebook, found his name and showed him a line.

“This is yesterday’s.”

“They pay me tonight.”

She passed him the copy. He said he would bring both coins before she closed and set off almost at a run. Sybille watched the notebook close. That debt had required no different service, no address or invitation. It had a name and the price printed on the newspaper.

“Will he come back?” she asked.

“Usually.”

“And if he doesn’t?”

“Tomorrow I remember him. I don’t sell his life to recover two sheets.”

Varek slipped the notebook beneath the basket, leaving the joke where Sybille could not pretend she had failed to hear it.

“Does Mael want the updated address too?” she asked.

“The intermediate depot?”

“I saw the old yard. The pears really are there.”

Sybille nodded. Crossing out one destination did not provide another. “I need to see the access first. If a crate won’t fit, it won’t get narrower because the buyer’s in a hurry.”

“Will it only be a crate?”

The noise of the street went on between them. Sybille thought of the empty bench, the two chairs, Mael’s question about what a technician might know how to measure. She wished Varek’s question had remained a commercial one. She did not give herself that comfort.

“There’s nothing to transport yet.”

“That wasn’t what I asked.”

Sybille raised the paid copy. “Send the answer to the usual contact.”

Varek took back the pencil she had forgotten to return. She did not ask about the crate again. She watched her leave with an expression Sybille preferred not to try interpreting.

At the corner, sunlight ended beneath the portico. She could have concealed her step and emerged on the other side without offering the passersby a face. She kept walking, the paper beneath her arm and her pocket clear of the wall.

She had to take Mael a question still unanswered. It was accurate information. That did not make it feel innocent.
