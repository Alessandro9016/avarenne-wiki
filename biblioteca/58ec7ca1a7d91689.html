# AVARENNE
## Volume I — The Waking House
### Chapter 10 — The Ordinary Door

Edith Torrel looked at the key in Marcel's hand.

“You told me about a special room.”

“Not the lock,” Elias said.

Livia glanced at him, and he abandoned the second joke.

They had arrived separately. The warehouse still smelled of packaging, rope, and rain brought in on shoes. Marcel had checked the route. No one had summoned them across a city or torn them from another place. Edith had come because Livia proposed a limited discussion of Etienne's documents.

Marcel opened the door.

The antechamber held a different coolness from the warehouse. Beyond it, the dark walnut walls seemed to absorb their first words. An oval table occupied the center of the hall, long enough to make a chair on the far side seem distant. Old brass fittings reflected subdued light.

Edith stopped.

“Do you really intend to discuss my shifts in here?”

“We can do it outside too.”

“I was asking about the taste in furniture.”

Elias smiled. He realized he had prepared to defend the place against an insult that had never come.

Marcel showed her the ordinary door and the way back to the warehouse. The prepared connection remained closed. They did not spend one of the two remaining openings to save a few steps.

The hall was too large for the number of their voices. Elias had grown used to it alone, but Edith's first hesitation restored the scale of the place: the section of wall left dark, the distance between table and door, the chairs nobody had yet pulled out.

The nurse looked at the brass fittings without approaching.

“If I touch something, will I set a machine going?”

Marcel explained which parts could be left alone and where the clear route lay. He invented no story about the table's origins to make it more interesting. The room had a material past they knew only partly; they need only avoid confusing it with their own project.

Edith walked a few steps along the table. Her bag nudged a chair and she brought it round in front of her hip.

“Can people outside hear what we say in here?”

Elias opened his mouth. Marcel deprived him of an overconfident answer simply by indicating the still-open door. They could hear rope being dragged across the warehouse floor. Livia stood near the threshold and spoke a sentence in her normal voice; Elias stayed where he was, trying to distinguish every word.

They did not turn it into a test of perfect privacy. They merely realized how unserious it was to promise secrecy because a room looked fine enough to deserve it.

Edith let a cart pass in the noise of the street before asking again:

“What if I'm left behind?”

“The door is there. It doesn't only open for whoever brought us in,” Elias replied.

The question, he realized, also concerned someone who could not immediately find the courage to interrupt a conversation. He had imagined invitations, introductions, exchanged services. He had given little thought to a person who simply wanted to reach her shift without winning an argument to leave.

“If we need to finish at a particular time, we'll say so beforehand,” he added.

Edith checked the clock. She told him how much time she had that evening. Elias did not round it up to gain a little more attention. He noticed Marcel watching him and was grateful the man had not offered the small nod of approval he would have found natural in a workshop.

Edith ran a hand along a chair's back, then chose one near the side she had entered from. Her nursing bag sat at her feet, its handle turned toward her.

Elias sat within conversational distance. Twelve chairs did not mean twelve people hidden behind a project. At that moment there were only four of them, and one had not decided whether she wanted to return.

Edith noticed the sheet on which Livia had written Footnote for an earlier trial.

“Am I supposed to call you that?”

“Only if we decide to use our meeting names.”

“And yours?” she asked Elias.

“Northbound.”

The nurse looked at him, then at the door.

“You know you could be followed here even if you change your name?”

“Yes.”

He had needed to learn to give that answer without immediately adding an imaginary solution. No mask changed the route taken to reach a place. That evening they had shown their faces among people they already knew. They could not sell that relationship as perfect anonymity.

“One thing first,” Edith said. “What has Etienne authorized you to ask for?”

Livia set down a copy of the offer and the request for its attachment.

“A comparison concerning these. Not his whole medical file.”

Elias had already opened his notebook to the page where he had written examination. He closed it.

Edith saw.

“You can ask. I can say no too.”

“I'd like to know whether he can manage the waiting tomorrow.”

“I can discuss that with him. A healed hand doesn't take away his right to be tired.”

Marcel sat without choosing the head of the table. For a few moments the four listened to a distant noise from the warehouse. The size of the hall did not isolate them from the world.

Livia explained what they hoped to build. Elias added the services they lacked: a check, a competent contact, someone able to say when a problem had been framed badly. Edith let them finish.

“Who pays?”

“The person asking for the work, when we agree on a fee,” Elias said.

“And when they can't?”

Edith opened her bag to check the time on her small watch, then closed it without offering its contents to the table. Elias followed the gesture and realized how easily he had imagined a skill without imagining a day: visits, journeys back, a meal to find between shifts.

“We won't ask you to bring the clinic's medicines here,” he said.

She stared at him.

“Good. They aren't mine.”

The sentence took an ease out of the discussion he could not have named. If the group wanted to help someone, it first needed to understand what its guests had the right to offer.

He looked at Livia. They had no answer to sell as an established rule.

“Then someone must choose to bear the cost,” she said. “We can't promise other people's time.”

Edith nodded slowly.

“I like that sentence more than the table.”

“Let's take an example,” Edith said.

Elias set down the pen. This was the moment he had awaited: a concrete request, finally something to organize rather than another limit to list.

“Someone is ill. I can come, but I can't leave my shift. What do you tell them?”

“That we'll find someone else.”

“Who?”

He had no name to give her. Acquaintances of acquaintances came to mind, half-heard rumours, the promise that it would be easier when there were more of them. The future could not replace a person available that evening.

“If we can't find anyone, we can't announce that the problem is solved.”

Edith nodded. Livia asked who was authorized to know the sick person's name. The conversation became less brilliant and more useful. Elias stopped recording only answers. He also wrote down the questions they had failed to answer.

Marcel took the jug and filled a glass. The sound of water restored a detail he had forgotten: they had been sitting for some time without offering their guest anything.

Elias pushed the glass toward Edith. She took it with thanks, without treating it as proof of an alliance.

“And what would you want from me, besides understanding Etienne's case better?”

The young man looked at Livia. They had discussed information that could not easily be bought, skills a person did not possess merely by becoming a Bearer. Now he had to explain that desire without making the nurse sound like the missing part of a machine they had built for themselves.

“I'd like to be able to ask a question of someone who has the right to tell me I'm asking the wrong thing.”

“I'm already doing that.”

“Yes.”

They laughed. The table grew no shorter, but Elias stopped measuring all the distances between empty places as though they were a deficiency to remedy before evening ended.

Livia moved the sheet bearing the pseudonym. She put it beneath her papers, leaving on top a page on which any of the four could write. Edith asked whether they would keep an account. The copyist replied that first they must agree what to preserve and whom to show it to.

“Even when someone says something foolish?”

“Especially then, I'd want to know whether they're trying something out or making a commitment,” Livia said.

Elias thought how much he would hate rereading some of his remarks that evening. Even so, he left the pen in the middle of the table. If he wanted somewhere to learn from others, he would have to bear the possibility of finding his starting point in writing.

She asked who owned the keys, who could enter, and what stopped a participant from reporting everything elsewhere. None of the answers was an unbreakable oath. Elias felt the hall's allure diminish with every limit they listed. At the same time, he saw Edith relax her shoulders.

When she asked to see the exit a second time, he stood and went with her. They crossed the antechamber, reached the warehouse, came back. The door opened both ways with a real handle.

“You aren't missing an opportunity,” Edith said as they returned.

“What do you mean?”

“You look as though you'd like to show me something more spectacular.”

Elias looked at the marks he had not activated.

“A little.”

“For tonight, knowing how to leave is enough.”

At the table, Edith agreed to help Etienne distinguish a useful check from a visit requested only for the dispute. She did not join the circle. She did not promise every evening she had.

Edith shouldered her bag again. Its handle had marked her skirt where she had held it throughout the conversation. Elias rose too soon to accompany her and struck a chair; he put it right without making a joke of the noise.

Livia gathered her pages. She did not pass their guest a copy to make something not yet agreed look concluded. She merely reminded her of the channel through which they would arrange their next relevant discussion.

“If my shift changes, I'll let you know,” Edith said.

Elias nodded. He wanted to say the hall would always be available, but it was not in his sole keeping, and Marcel had a life long enough not to deserve that sort of promise made on his behalf.

They headed toward the antechamber. The chairs left in place no longer looked like missing guests. They were furniture: they would wait without judging how long it took to find people.

The warehouse light seemed brighter on their return. Edith slowed to let Livia pass with her folder, then pointed out a badly placed crate beside the door. Elias saw the obstruction. That, at least, was a question he could answer with his hands.

Marcel locked the door when they left. Elias waited for the click, then helped move a crate obstructing the way.

The hall remained behind him, enormous and silent. For the first time, it seemed large enough to hold a refusal too.
