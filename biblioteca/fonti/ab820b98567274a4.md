# AVARENNE
## Volume I — The Waking House
### Chapter 46 — Borrowed Ink

The word heirs ended beneath a stain that did not belong to the original.

Livia raised her sleeve and found the dark spot on her cuff. She had rested her arm on the sheet before the ink dried. In the document she was copying, the same word had thin, clean letters. In her copy it looked as though someone had rubbed a finger across a family's name to erase it.

She could not deliver it like that.

She set the sheet on the discard shelf and took another. Only a few pages remained, but the ruined one was dense: names, property shares, a sentence that resumed below the lower margin and seemed to have forgotten where it was going. The original manuscript at least had the courtesy not to lie about its own ugliness.

It was the afternoon of June fourth; the copyists' room held the street's heat. A colleague had gone out, leaving a chair in the passage. Voices came through the window; a woman was calling someone who, judging by her growing irritation, could hear her perfectly well. Livia moved the chair with her foot and shifted the inkwell beyond her sleeve's reach.

In her parcel, the cardboard for Edith's cover held its new fold. She had checked it on the way back from the workshop. The notebook did not need her to think about keeping it dry every moment. Now she wished her own arm could remember where the page ended.

She began again at the first line. With Held Moment she preserved the passage she was reading, clear enough that she need not keep searching for the names. Her fingers still had to form the letters. The ability would not bring her a finished sheet while she rested, and after two lines she paused to ease the tension near her thumb.

The clerk arrived just then.

“Someone is asking for you.”

Livia looked at her open hand. “For a copy?”

“He said he knows you. Uren.”

The name brought back a wrapped sample and correspondence that had not all been written on the same side of the truth. Ilian had not come to request an extract from the inheritance document. She checked the clock, then the pages still to do.

“May I see him at the entrance table?”

The clerk nodded. Livia covered the original, put down her pen, and took only her own notebook. Slanting light and the smell of warmed paper filled the entrance. Ilian stood beside the public table with his case pressed beneath one arm. His coat had a crease on the shoulder that seemed made by a strap's edge.

“I wasn't sure I'd find you here,” he said.

“You found me.”

“I won't take long.”

Livia looked at the case. “What do you need?”

He put it down without immediately opening it. The sides of his fingernails were marked, as though he had been working on something small and stubborn. “A different question. A simpler one.”

The copyist indicated a chair and took the one opposite. The table was wide enough for both sets of papers. It was not wide enough to ignore what lay in front of the other person.

Ilian took out a letter and a drawing. The component shown was a metal collar, split so it could close around an existing element. A gauge was to establish its opening and alignment; dimensions were blank where the actual object needed measuring. No illustration showed people in transit. Livia read the request down to the signature before speaking.

“You want me to find a surveyor?”

“I wanted to know whether Ruy could take it on. Or Veyne, with him. It's an ordinary collar. They aren't asking for a connection.”

“Have you seen what needs measuring?”

“The collar, yes. The complete assembly, no.”

Livia returned to the text. The first sentence concerned a comparison of external dimensions. The second requested confirmation of compatibility with the stated use. She stopped there. Ilian had followed her gaze closely enough to know which part she was reading.

“I already had that changed,” he said.

He opened his notebook and showed her an earlier draft. The initial question took up no more lines, but joined the physical comparison to a phrase about the usability of the whole. There were different words in the new text. Half of the phrase that concerned Livia had survived.

“What use have they stated?”

“What it says. A support for an apparatus.”

“Which apparatus?”

Ilian looked down at the drawing. “They didn't give me that to draw.”

The answer was dry, irritated above all by its own insufficiency. Livia knew that voice: it belonged to someone who had prepared for a question and discovered he did not have the answer he thought he had.

“May I read your request for payment too?” she asked.

He looked at her. “That isn't the work I'm asking you to do.”

“You put the two sheets together.”

Ilian removed the bill from the case and set it beside the letter. The balance for the previous work remained outstanding. A line added to the message said that defining the new inspection would allow the overall matter to be concluded more quickly.

Outside, the woman finally stopped calling. In the resulting quiet, the scrape of Ilian's heel on the floor sounded very loud.

“If I can bring an answer, I'll have something when I go back,” he said.

Livia did not tell him he could simply stay away. The purchased paper, the hours already delivered, and the client who decided when a matter was closed lay on the table before her; the draftsman did not need to describe his finances.

“I can compare these texts. I can't accept on Marcel's behalf.”

“I'm not asking you to.”

“Or Elias's.”

“I thought talking to you would keep me from arriving with the wrong question again.”

Livia looked at the newer sheet. The man had brought her precisely the work she knew how to do. Her annoyance shifted: it did not vanish, but ceased to point entirely toward the opposite chair.

She asked permission to copy the passage. Ilian nodded and moved the bill to make room. The copyist transcribed the whole sentence, then the one from the earlier version. Beneath them she added the date and circumstances in which she had seen them. She showed him her words before investing True Mark in the relationship between her observation and the text.

The mark looked no finer than the neighboring ink. Livia felt its precise hold, limited to the fact actually before her. She had not seen the client dictate the words. She had seen Ilian produce two sheets, and could preserve unambiguously which sentence appeared on each.

“This attests that you've compared them?” he asked.

“That these words were in the texts you showed me. Not that the collar is suitable.”

Ilian followed the lines with his nail. “Can you also note that I didn't ask you to certify it?”

She looked up. “Yes.”

She did so. She gave him the page, waited for him to read it, and copied the necessary passage for herself without keeping his correspondence. The bill returned to Ilian's case with a small rasp of paper against the edge. He straightened it immediately.

“I want to get paid without giving them another week,” he said.

“Then the technical question has to stand on its own.”

She took the collar drawing and indicated the blank spaces. They required an object, access, and someone competent to measure it. The other phrase required a defined use. Asking for one thing did not automatically obtain the other. Ilian did not need her to explain how to draw metal: he separated the two requests himself in the margin of his draft.

“Like that?”

Livia read it. “That I can take to Marcel.”

“Today?”

She glanced toward the copyists' room. The stained page still waited beside the original; nobody would rewrite it because a more interesting problem had appeared at the entrance table.

“When I've finished my work. I can't promise you an answer tonight.”

Ilian picked up the letter. He seemed to age as he folded it, then asked only where he could receive news. They agreed on the address already used by the workshop. Sending an answer did not require opening every place in Livia's life to him.

As he rose, the draftsman hesitated with his hand on the case. “Is Veyne working again?”

“He's working.”

Ilian nodded, and this time smiled enough to show her that the question had not concerned commercial information. Livia did not add the duke's visit. He said goodbye and stepped into the sunlight beyond the door, turning his shoulder to avoid someone entering with a bundle of records.

The copyist watched the copy of the two sentences for a moment. An artisan could carry out the new request without touching anything extraordinary. That was precisely why its words needed to end where the work ended. The collar looked harmless on paper; the way it was being paid for looked less so.

She returned to the room. Her place had retained the shape of her absence: the pen laid across it, the original covered, the clean sheet she had just begun. Her sleeve threatened to slip again; she secured it above her wrist with a ribbon.

She resumed copying at the heirs' name. This time she let each stroke dry before moving her hand. The ache near her thumb forced a short pause. Beneath the table she opened her fingers and closed them one at a time, feeling how little the tendon cared about the world's problems.

When she finished the page, the lower margin coincided with the end of the sentence. Livia checked it against the original and felt a simple, almost physical pleasure. She put the sheet where nobody passing could brush it.

The clerk returned to ask whether he could collect the file. Livia showed him the completed pages and kept the original beneath her hand. He looked at the discarded sheet; the stain seemed larger now it was dry.

“Should this be thrown away?”

“After the comparison.”

He said the man waiting had sent to ask twice. The copyist felt a sharp answer rising and stopped it long enough to ask the person's name. It was the one on the commission. No urgent arrival, no altered deadline; a man did not want to remain seated longer than expected.

Livia indicated the blank page. “Tell him this one remains. If he wants to come back, the file will stay here.”

The clerk went to report. She brought the two completed pages closer and checked the names. In a central line, the second heir's surname had a letter that could be read two ways. In the original, the pen had skipped over a ridge in the paper. Livia remembered the exact shape, yet that precision did not tell her which letter the writer had intended.

She marked the place in her copy's margin, called the clerk again, and had him take the question to the man who had supplied the original. He returned shortly with the name written on a scrap. This time the letter left no alternative. Livia attached the clarification and completed the line.

She had lost more minutes to that name. But the waiting man's delay had acquired a purpose. She could give him a file in which his brother did not become a stranger because of a faulty pen point.

One last copy remained. Before beginning, she put the note about Ilian in a different pocket from the novel installment and Edith's cover. The edges of the three papers had briefly made her think she could carry them all the same way.

The fresh page waited. Livia moved the inkwell to the safe place, chose a point that did not scratch, and wrote the first name with her hand still tired.
