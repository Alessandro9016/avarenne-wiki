# AVARENNE
## Volume I — The Waking House
### Chapter 72 — The Open Folder

Martin found the classroom door open and immediately thought of the windows. The day before, he had begged the caretaker to let some air in; now a draught was knocking the shutter against its catch. Three exercise books, a bag of chalk and a stranger's hat lay on the first desk.

The man was bent over the teacher's table. He wore a brown jacket too heavy for June and had opened the folder Martin had left there after the morning lesson.

“Are you looking for someone?”

“You, I believe.” The man straightened without hurry. “They said I could wait.”

Martin looked at the hat on the desk. Its green lining had come unstitched beside the brim. The visitor followed his glance and smiled, as though taking off his hat were introduction enough.

“About the pump work. I'm from the workshop.”

“Which workshop?”

“Your wife's.”

For a moment, Martin simply wanted to sit down. He had spent the morning teaching fractions and the early afternoon demonstrating how to reach a fastening. The local gasket had held. Yselle had finally eaten a meal without keeping an account beside her plate. He did not want another person coming to discuss the miracle.

“My wife has a name,” he said.

“Mrs Valcienne. She sent me for the instructions.”

The name was right. So was the sheet the man held up: a draft of the instructions, with the drawing Martin had corrected using a bent-handled wrench. It was not the copy intended for the Mutual. It was his attempt, covered in crossings-out.

“They aren't ready.”

“They're needed anyway. You can come back with me, if you like.”

The invitation eased some of his suspicion. Someone stealing a sheet of paper would not invite its owner to accompany him. Martin crossed the classroom, put down the parcel he was carrying and held out his hand. The stranger did not immediately return the sheet.

“I need the list as well.”

“What list?”

“Of the people who worked on it. For deliveries.”

Martin lowered his hand. His folder held no staff list. It did contain a reused business envelope keeping the papers together; on its back he had noted the times at which he could find Elias, Ruy and Yselle. It saved him interrupting other people's work. He had never shown it to a carrier.

The man closed the folder with the sheet still outside. “If you have another lesson, I can take the whole thing.”

“Put it down.”

The words came out quieter than Martin intended. The visitor was still smiling, but he had stopped meeting his eyes. He was watching the gap between Martin and the door.

Martin moved. Not far enough to block him: far enough to make him ask to pass.

“Who sent you?”

“I told you.”

“Tell me what shoes my wife wears at work.”

The smile vanished. Martin did not wait for an answer. He caught the folder; the man pulled and the metal clasp gave way. Papers fell between them. The visitor's free hand struck him below the ribs.

It was not a storybook punch. Martin felt the breath leave him without permission and folded against the table. He tried to call the caretaker, made an absurd noise, and felt ashamed of that noise before he felt afraid.

The man shoved him past the corner, picked up the envelope and the instruction sheet. He left the arithmetic lessons on the floor. He knew what he had come to take.

Martin grabbed the chalk bag and flung it at his face. The cloth struck a shoulder. A white cloud opened between the door and the window. The man coughed, knocked into a desk and lost his hat. Martin managed to shout.

“Stop him!”

A voice answered from the yard. The visitor no longer tried for the door. He climbed onto the desk beside the window. Martin caught the hem of his jacket; he felt the seam hold, then saw a short blade appear in the man's right hand.

He let go. The cut crossed his palm before he could draw it back.

The thief swung over the sill and jumped into the lower yard. It could not have been more than five feet, but Martin felt as though he had watched him fall from a roof. The caretaker reached the door as the man ran towards the passage to the street.

“The steps!” Martin said, pointing outside.

The caretaker misunderstood and went for the stepladder. By the time he returned, the yard was empty.

Martin remained standing with his hand pressed against the previous teacher's apron, still hanging from its nail. Blood spread through an ink stain years old. That detail angered him more than the pain.

The caretaker looked at the hat. “One of your pupils?”

“No.”

“I saw him come in. Said you were expecting him.”

Martin sat on the first desk. The room suddenly seemed too close: low ceiling, sunlit dust, problems on the blackboard for people who would arrive that evening. Someone would have to clean up the blood before the lesson.

“Fetch a guard,” he said. “And someone who can bandage a hand.”

He did not ask for a miracle. He asked for a clean bandage and had to repeat himself, because the caretaker had heard only the first half of the sentence.

The woman who kept the gate brought water and strips of linen. She made him open his fingers one at a time; his ring finger trembled. The cut was not as deep as the blood had suggested, but she would not let him clench his fist at once to prove it.

“You saw the knife and kept hold of him?”

“No. I let go.”

“Good.”

That good clung to him like a rebuke. He looked at the table. The thief had not taken the coins in the stationery box. He had not even touched the clock, which belonged to the school and was worth more than the folder.

When the guard arrived, Martin showed her those two things first. He described the hat, the jacket, the right hand. Asked how tall the man had been, he pointed at the doorframe and realised he could not say. He remembered the smile perfectly, but a smile did not fit the description box.

“Why did he want those papers?”

Martin began explaining about the pump and saw the guard's expression change when he mentioned the duke.

“Was it his material?”

“No. Mine. The duke hasn't entrusted anything to me.”

The guard crossed out a word. She asked who had seen the envelope with the times. Martin listed Yselle and Elias, then stopped. The thief might have been looking for it; he might also have found it while rummaging. Claiming he had known its contents beforehand would have been convenient and false.

“He chose it,” he said. “I don't know whether he knew it was there.”

Before writing, the guard asked to see the yard through which the man had escaped. Martin accompanied her to the window. Beneath the sill, an empty planter held a footprint in its soil; beyond it, the passage divided between the street and a shop's back entrance. The caretaker had watched the street. Nobody could say which route the thief had taken after the first corner.

The guard asked who owned the rear premises and sent the caretaker to fetch him. Martin waited until he returned with an elderly man irritated at having to interrupt his work. He had seen no escape. He had seen a brown jacket cross his passage half an hour earlier, heading towards the school. The man had stopped to ask whether that was the teachers' entrance.

Martin felt the room behind him grow smaller. The visitor had not stumbled upon an open folder by chance. He had sought him out, in a place where he should have found only a teacher. Martin asked the guard to include that detail in the statement. She replied that she would hear the owner separately; Martin must not lend him words he might later be unable to recognise.

The statement took longer than the theft. Martin could not stop watching the corridor. Every footstep sounded like Yselle's and, when she finally appeared, he wished he had sent her less precise news.

She stopped in the doorway. She looked at the stained linen, the blackboard and the caretaker holding a broom. “Who?”

“I don't know him.”

“What did he take?”

“My sheet. The times on the back of the envelope.”

Yselle closed her eyes for a moment. Martin waited for her to ask why he had tried to stop him. Instead, she took his uninjured wrist, checked that it was warm and asked whether he could walk.

“He didn't cut my leg.”

“I can see that. I asked whether you could.”

The hardness in her voice finally allowed him to stop making jokes. He nodded. The guard said the hat would remain as evidence. Yselle requested a copy of the statement, then searched the room with her eyes for the missing sheet, as though the thief might have forgotten it in another corner.

They found only a strip of paper beneath the second desk. It did not belong to the folder. It was a parcel label with half a printed word and a small rectangle drawn in pencil. Below it, someone had written Uren and a time: after closing.

Martin did not remember seeing it fall. He pointed without picking it up. The guard took it by an edge, placed it inside the hat, then wrapped both. Before closing the parcel, she allowed Yselle to copy the two words.

“Do you know him?”

“By name. A draughtsman,” she replied. “He doesn't work for us.”

The guard said she would have it checked. Martin looked at the rectangle. It could be an innocent reminder carried by a man who no longer had anything innocent about him. He disliked how much he wanted it to mean everything.

Outside, Yselle adjusted his sleeve so it would not touch the dressing. The ordinary gesture made him want to tell her a second time about the punch, the blade, the way his voice had failed. Instead he only said, “He knew you were my wife.”

“So do the people who buy my parts.”

“I asked him about your shoes.”

She looked at him. For the first time since reaching the doorway, her expression was almost amused. “Which ones?”

“The ugly ones.”

“You'll have to be more specific.”

They laughed badly for two steps. Then Martin had to stop because the pain beneath his ribs was returning with his breath.

Yselle did not promise they would find the man. She said the instructions could wait that evening and Elias would be warned as soon as he returned. At the Mutual they would check requests for access. The authorities had the report. It was enough to begin, too little to make him feel safe.

“He didn't want the machine,” Martin said.

“No.”

“He wanted to know when we weren't there.”

Yselle did not answer. She accompanied him along the busiest street, walking on the side of his injured hand. At the school gate, an evening pupil was waiting with an exercise book under his arm. He asked whether there would be a lesson.

Martin was about to say yes. He saw the chalk on the collar of his jacket and understood that, for once, turning it the other way round would not be enough.

“Tomorrow,” he answered.

The man nodded, disappointed. He knew nothing about the duke, the miracle or the stolen sheet. He had given up an hour's work to arrive on time. Martin watched him leave and finally felt anger without shame.
