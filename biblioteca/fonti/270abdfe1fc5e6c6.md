# AVARENNE
## Volume I — The Waking House
### Chapter 72 — A Conversation to Propose

Elias had already closed the caliper case. He opened it again out of habit, then stopped before taking out the instrument: there was a drawing on the bench, not a part to measure. Ilian gave him time to notice without explaining his trade to him.

“Is this what you discussed with Livia?” Elias asked.

“The reduced request. There's a signed clarification.”

The slip bore the name Mael Vaur. Elias read it before returning to the collar. Compatibility of the ordinary part, no test of the complete device. The distinction limited the work but offered no surface on which to place the calipers.

“Where is the specimen?”

“They need to specify that.”

“And who's making it available?”

“That too.”

Ilian did not try to improve either answer. Elias showed him the note about the operator's presence. The man opened his folder and placed an earlier copy beside the clarification, where the phrase still appeared in full.

“I asked whether they meant a visit on site. They said yes. I haven't agreed to anything else on your behalf.”

Elias drew the sheet closer to the light. One possible section had an arrow, the other a question. Ilian indicated the gap in the drawing and explained which parts still required checking. That, at least, was a problem Elias could understand without deciding whether to trust the customer.

“To give you a figure, I need to know whether checking a loose part is enough. If I have to reach an installed machine, the time changes.”

“I know.”

“And I have to be able to get there without someone discovering afterward that I shouldn't have been there.”

“That's exactly the question I took back.”

Ilian removed his hat. Its edge had left a line across his hair. Elias might have mistaken weariness for impatience and answered badly; he remembered how Marcel looked at him when he could not stop justifying a question.

“I'm not saying no to the part,” he said. “I'm saying I don't yet have a job I can say yes to.”

Ilian set the hat on a free corner. “I can report that.”

From the laboratory came the noise of cabinets being shut. Elias did not want to detain people finishing work for a commission that did not exist. He collected two sheets to reuse and copied the missing information: location, authorization, condition of the specimen, nature of the required attendance. He left room beneath for the answer.

“Don't come tomorrow morning thinking I've arranged a visit.”

“I haven't arranged one either.”

“If they send me these details, we'll discuss it. Then there needs to be an agreement on payment. Separate from what they owe you.”

Ilian looked at his folder without opening it. “Livia told you.”

“She told me there was an outstanding balance.”

“There still is.”

Elias offered neither to surrender it on his behalf nor to work well enough to make them pay it. He made a second copy of the question and left the first to Ilian. The draftsman folded it far more carefully than the paper deserved.

“At least this says what's missing.”

“It isn't an acceptance.”

“I heard you.”

Ilian smiled, and the small rebuke did Elias good. They had understood one another before the last repetition. When the man left, the bench had acquired a pending question and no new commitment. Elias closed the calipers again. Outside, the air seemed clear enough to preserve part of the day for something he had chosen himself.

Livia was waiting at her work address, with a stack of copies already tied. Edith sat near the window, holding the novel open at a page she was not reading. Elias saw Livia's thumb pressed against the side of her hand and asked whether it hurt.

“Yes. And no, I don't need you to become a doctor.”

Edith closed the volume on her finger. “We were beginning to wonder whether your duke had ordered another twenty pumps.”

“My duke is in Rovenne.”

“Long-distance ownership, then.”

Livia moved the copies to make room for him. Elias described Ilian's visit first, so she would not discover later that another part of his life had passed their door. He showed the question without giving the customer a face he had never seen. Livia read the sentences, then compared the new limits with the note she had kept.

“Better than before,” she said. “Still incomplete.”

“I haven't accepted.”

“Good. Now stop proving it to me and sit down.”

Edith moved a chair with her foot. Beneath her book she had the sheet on which she had already tried the beginning of the other letter. Elias recognized Your Grace and two lines crossed out so vigorously they had nearly pierced the paper.

“Was it too much?” he asked.

“It sounded as though we wanted to ask him for a roof.”

Livia picked up the sheet. “You wrote that we wish to submit a modest initiative to his benevolent attention.”

“I wrote it to show you how it sounded.”

“It sounded like the roof.”

Elias rested his elbows on the table and noticed he had brought a graphite stain. He wiped it with the edge of a scrap. The duke had never asked him to write benevolent attention. He had listened while Elias discussed dimensions and water, and had made three machines appear whose material origin Elias still could not comprehend. He did not know whether that difference made inviting him absurd or more necessary.

“Why would he come?” he asked.

Edith took the scrap away before he spread the stain. “To learn something he doesn't know.”

“We don't know what he doesn't know.”

“We can begin with ourselves,” Livia said. “We haven't spoken to him.”

She was right. Elias had thought about the duke's power, the materials, the healing. The other two had not even had a conversation with him. In a letter full of his own admiration, he would have turned them into appendices to the workshop visit.

“What do you want to ask him?”

Livia drew the blank sheet toward her. “How he chooses which requests to answer. I've spent the afternoon separating a false word from a badly written name. I'd like to understand whether someone who can do a great deal still sees that difference.”

“Write it shorter,” Edith said.

“The question was for us.”

“Then I want to know whether you can learn something without promising an entire life in exchange.”

Edith was no longer smiling. Elias let the sentence remain on the table. She had no Effigy to set beside theirs, yet she had asked questions to which their exchange already owed part of its existence. He did not want to write a plea for her to be given something she had not asked for.

“I'd like him to see a piece of finished work,” he said. “Not just the times when something is missing that he can give.”

“He's seen the pump,” Livia objected.

“I don't mean that.”

He stopped. For a moment he had wanted to set the entire room before him, the table, the places they did not fill yet, and wait for a man so powerful to find them interesting. It was the same haste with which he had written successful too soon. He took the pencil and wrote conversation on a new sheet.

“We aren't inviting him to join. We're inviting him to talk.”

“And somewhere other than our place,” Livia said.

Edith nodded. “Somewhere we can also finish and leave.”

The first version became short almost by necessity. They introduced themselves by their ordinary names and the fields to which each could contribute: surveying and technical work, comparison of texts and documents, observations and questions from a reader without powers. Edith crossed out without powers.

“If he wants to know, I'll tell him. It isn't my job title.”

Elias put down the pencil. She replaced the phrase with ordinary study and practice. It did not seem perfect, but finding a formula into which Edith fitted better was not his decision.

They gave no location for their group, named no objects in their keeping, and included no aliases. They wrote that they sought a conversation about abilities, tools, and knowledge that might be useful to one another. No request for funding. Livia crossed out that qualification: it already sounded like the beginning of a request for funding. She replaced it with a sentence about questions to be agreed before the meeting.

The venue remained. Elias suggested Yselle might propose a suitable outside space, if she wished to help them. Edith pointed out that they would be handing her their problem along with the envelope. Instead they wrote that place and day would be agreed after a favorable reply. No compulsory appointment and no seat assigned beforehand.

Livia dipped the new nib and began the copy. Elias held the corner steady until she told him he would stain it. He withdrew his hand. Her writing came out narrower than in the morning's copies; it was not only fatigue, he thought, but a desire to fit her own voice into a space nobody had already decided for her.

“What if he has an administrator answer?” Edith asked.

Elias looked at the heading. “We'll have an answer.”

“Not to the question we're asking him.”

Livia raised her pen. “If he doesn't want the conversation, we can't make his administrator want it. But I'm not writing three more lines to prevent that.”

Edith nodded and turned her book facedown. Elias abandoned the idea of adding a reference to the pumps that would make ignoring them harder. That was precisely the work for which a payment had been agreed; he could not use it as a little chain around the recipient's wrist.

They read the text aloud. The first time Livia corrected a repetition. The second time Edith said they could have read it without knowing the recipient performed miracles. Elias waited for a third, but it was unnecessary. All three signed.

When they passed him the pen, he hesitated over the space beneath his name. He did not need a grander qualification to fill it. He signed in the handwriting he used on work sheets, then made room for Edith, who had to turn the page to avoid resting her wrist on the fresh ink.

He took the envelope to the workshop before the correspondence left for Rovenne. Yselle had the order book open and a strip of cloth tied around an ink-stained finger. Elias told her who had written and what they were asking. She did not immediately hold out her hand.

“Do you want me to recommend it to him?”

“I'd like you to get it to him.”

Yselle took the envelope. “I can do that. He'll read the rest for himself.”

She did not ask to see their premises or learn things the letter did not contain. She had the envelope added to the business correspondence entrusted to someone already traveling toward Rovenne. The journey would take hours, just as the duke's journey had: Elias could picture the paper in the carriage much more easily than the recipient reading it.

He returned to the other two empty-handed. Edith had reopened the novel; Livia was comparing the final copy against its original. They had not waited motionless for his return.

“Has it gone?” Edith asked.

“It's going with the correspondence.”

“Then now I can read to the end of the page.”

Elias sat down. He had one question to take to a customer and one answer to await from a duke. The two had not resolved each other. Livia passed him the sheet of Ilian's request to put away, then showed him the point in the novel Edith found unbearable. For that evening he could still argue about something nobody had signed.
