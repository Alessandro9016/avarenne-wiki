# AVARENNE
## Volume I — The Waking House
### Chapter 28 — The Second Position

Marcel did not reactivate the bench.

Elias had feared someone would want to repeat the accident to prove they understood it. Instead, his mentor brought wooden strips, string, and measuring references, and had the necessary space cleared without asking anyone to put an arm where Etienne's had been injured.

“Today we reconstruct the positions,” he said. “Calibration comes afterward. Let's not confuse a demonstration with an authorized test.”

Gaston arranged the two series on a crate. Vern stayed near the passage he had wanted cleared, his hands in his jacket pockets. Etienne had chosen to be there. He would not put his body in the place of a missing measurement.

It was May twenty-sixth. Five days remained until the hearing.

For the first time, that count did not seem merely something time could do against Elias.

The portable bench allowed the alignment of a local field to be checked against the points reached in the working arrangement. The certification concerned that relationship. Moving the furniture did not move the warehouse's reference points with it.

The access supervisor had a crate moved away from where they would stretch the reference. Elias waited until the load was set down. On first entering the warehouse, he had not known which movements changed the arrangement and which belonged only to that day's work.

Now Marcel asked him to look before explaining. The slats did not mark a truth found in dust: they made visible the two positions established from documents. The floor could be the same without remembering its history for them.

Gaston opened the first series. Elias recognized his writing in the part he had copied and another hand in the later notes. For weeks he had wanted to reduce everything to one sheet clear enough to acquit him. Seeing two kept separate finally seemed precision rather than proof he had complicated the problem.

Vern remained near the passage. He sought no place to lean his back. Occasionally he looked toward work continuing farther away, but called nobody to escape the verification.

Etienne sat until asked to describe the point he remembered. Elias stopped checking whether the worker seemed recovered enough or offended enough. He was there because he had chosen to witness the comparison, not to make Elias's position more convincing.

Marcel stretched the first length of string. He did not fasten it wherever convenient: he had them find a reference everyone could agree on before continuing. It took two attempts, and nobody seized the chance to declare the experiment worthless.

Elias felt relief at witnessing work that did not depend on the absence of all hesitation. A correct measurement need not appear inevitable from the first movement to be checked.

Marcel stretched the string between the marks they could verify and asked Gaston to read the first series.

Elias recognized his own distances. He did not need to close his eyes to want to defend them.

“If this was the first position,” his mentor said, “that reference is consistent.”

Vern looked at the string and the edge of the passage. He said nothing.

The second series described the bench after its relocation. Gaston read it without altering the first. The two reconstructions could not occupy the same place simultaneously, even when recounted beneath a single heading.

Marcel asked Elias to indicate on the sheet which measurement he had personally taken. He did not ask him to declare he had witnessed every move.

He answered and stopped.

“You can continue,” Vern said.

Elias looked at him.

“As far as I know.”

The lot supervisor took a hand from his pocket, then put it back.

Marcel used Near Coordinates on the present volume and had the corresponding point checked with the measuring rule. His power gave him a real perception of space. The ordinary comparison let Gaston follow the result without possessing Astrolabe.

They did not read who had walked on the floor a month earlier. They did not need to in order to show what the two series said.

The supervisor asked how both series could contain correct values. Marcel indicated the references and left the tape to Gaston. Elias followed the space with his gaze while the two men repeated the reading.

A number did not carry the wall it had been measured from. A moved bench could retain parts and settings without retaining the relationship a certification described. Elias had understood that before the accident; he had not understood how easily it could disappear inside an abbreviated account of work.

“So you're not remeasuring to make the first one fit,” the supervisor said.

“We're reconstructing what it referred to,” Marcel answered.

Vern looked at the cleared stretch of passage. He was not speaking yet. Gaston merely asked him to confirm it was the point his explanation referred to. He said yes.

Elias was tempted to address him at once. He could hear the sentence: now you see. He held it back. Vern did not need humiliation before the tape to acknowledge a decision; if he chose not to, the comparison would not become false.

Marcel moved to the second reference. He added no difficulty to make the movement more serious. The error was concrete enough not to require invented guilt around it.

A slat slipped when Gaston changed position. Elias bent, then stopped because Vern's hand was already nearer. He let the man pick it up. The gesture solved nothing; it merely prevented Elias telling himself that even keeping the test orderly was a task to which the other could not contribute.

Etienne went to the edge of the passage and indicated a distance with his healed arm.

“I worked from here. I remember that.”

Marcel asked him not to add a number he did not remember. The worker nodded, almost relieved to be a good witness without becoming an instrument.

Vern was still holding the strip he had picked up earlier. He brought it along the edge of the bench without setting it against the reference; the angle remained visible to everyone.

“The stated margin seemed sufficient.”

No one answered quickly.

He looked at the bench.

“I should have requested the new check.”

Elias felt the desire to seize the sentence and run outside with it. He left it in the room long enough for Gaston to ask whether he meant the decision taken at the time.

“I knew it needed doing again after the move,” Vern said.

Etienne lowered his arm.

The truth did not arrive in a flash. It came spoken by a man who, until then, had tried to make his choice bearable by recounting it in pieces.

“Did you order it?” Elias asked.

Vern looked at him. Then he said yes.

Gaston wrote, read it back, and let the man correct anything that did not correspond to his words. Vern did not withdraw the answer.

Elias lowered his eyes to the string. Vern had not disputed the distance. He had not disputed the need for calibration either. The man Elias had sought for weeks stood before him, and there was no measurement left to which he could address the final question.

“Why did you keep using that sheet?”

Vern rubbed his thumb against the strip of wood. He looked at Etienne before answering.

“Because if I’d had it canceled, the lot would have stopped. The men were here. I thought the margin would be enough.”

“Knowing it needed to be done again.”

Vern pressed his lips together. “Knowing.”

Elias lowered the notebook. For weeks he had sought the mistake that would make everything comprehensible. The man had chosen between two costs and convinced himself that one of them would never come due.

The access supervisor watched Gaston as the answer was recorded. Elias wondered what he thought. Before, he would have sought embarrassment in his face at having believed the wrong person. Now he had no energy to imagine every spectator's defeat.

Vern released the slat onto the bench. The splinter had not cut his skin; his thumb kept returning nonetheless, seeking something to do.

“You can't change what you've already asked me to remember,” Etienne said.

The lot supervisor looked up. The worker had spoken not of money but of the account he would take to the hearing. He did not want an honourable part in another's decision at the cost of precision about his own.

“I'm not asking you to now,” Vern replied.

Gaston let the silence pass and reread the previous passage. The sentences were not becoming final testimony, but neither could they be turned into a conversation nobody had had a chance to revisit. Everyone was hearing the words in the same room.

Elias put his sound hand on the folder's edge. It prevented him seeking a pen to write too, overwhelming the instant with another record. He had listened. He could say which questions had been asked and which answers received.

Etienne waited for Vern to look at him again before asking about the thirty-first. Elias recognized in the pause work no ability could perform for him: giving a person time to choose whether to say before others what he had just admitted.

“Will you say that at the hearing?” Etienne asked.

Vern ran his thumb along the splinter on the strip.

“Yes.”

It was a promise. Gaston recorded it as such, not as testimony already given.

Gaston read the written words. Vern interrupted at one point to specify the decision's timing, then let him continue. Elias waited for the line's end without trying to fix with his own voice what he wanted to preserve.

“I didn't think anyone would get hurt,” the lot supervisor said.

Elias looked at him. The sentence could provide concealment, but could also remain part of the truth without erasing the rest.

“You decided to move it anyway.”

Vern nodded. He sought no other person in the room to whom to attribute the act.

Etienne put on his cap. He pulled its edge too far on one side and had to adjust it. Vern watched without finding anything to add. Elias bent to gather the first slat: the comparison was over, but neither man seemed ready to leave the place to the other.

Marcel checked that the references were recorded before removing the string. Removing it did not make the demonstration vanish; it left it in the sheets and people who had followed every step. Elias helped gather the slats, retaining the distinction between the admission received and what must still happen before the competent office.

The promise for the thirty-first seemed enormous. It could be kept or broken. At least it was a promise Vern had spoken, not a hope built in the workshop while he was absent.

They finished the check without activating the field. They secured the space again according to the site's conditions and left the bench where they had found it.

Gaston closed the notebook only after comparing the last note with the confirmed answer. Elias waited for that gesture before leaving. His haste to feel free must not interrupt the work that let others follow the reason.

Outside, Elias paused before saying goodbye to Etienne.

“I'd like to celebrate.”

The worker looked at him for a moment.

“So would I. When I know what happens to my pay.”

He was not forbidding him to be happy. He was showing him where the day was not over yet.
