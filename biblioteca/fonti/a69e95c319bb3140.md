# AVARENNE
## Volume I — The Waking House
### Chapter 1 — The Wrong Measure

The wall lay eleven centimeters beyond the back of the cabinet.

Elias kept his eyes closed.

He could feel the chipped edge of the workbench beneath his fingers. Beyond the wood, beyond the floor and the cabinet's legs, the room continued to yield its distances. These were not images. He could not see the screws lost in the dust or the color of the wallpaper. He knew where things ended.

And something back there ended too far away.

He opened his eyes. The back of the cabinet was dark, flat, perfectly ordinary. A cobweb joined its frame to the wall.

“Eleven centimeters,” he said.

Marcel Ruy stopped filing.

“You wrote down nine.”

“I know.”

“Then write eleven.”

Elias looked at his notebook. He had drawn the nine with a confidence he now found irritating.

“There could be a false wall.”

“There could.”

“Or a sealed recess.”

“That too.”

“Or the cabinet has a double back.”

Marcel put down the file.

“You could also move it.”

They moved the cabinet.

Behind it was a low, disused recess, a length of pipe sealed into the masonry, and enough dust to make Elias regret his curiosity. Marcel handed him the measuring rule without comment. The recess was eleven centimeters deep.

In his notebook, Elias crossed out the nine with a single stroke, leaving it legible. He wrote the correct value beside it.

His mentor returned to the vise. His gray hair was flattened on one side, with filings caught in a curl. Elias considered telling him, then decided that a man who could deliver a lesson without leaving his stool could put up with a little brass in his hair.

The sound of a cart came in from the alley. The window rattled, and two replacement glass panes clinked against a box on the shelf. The workshop smelled of oil, damp wood, and freshly worked metal. Someone called a name in the courtyard. No one answered.

It was May eighteenth. Thirteen days until the hearing.

Elias turned the page before he could count again.

On the next sheet he had drawn the warehouse where the test had taken place. The lines were tidier than the real room: no crates, no people, no sleeve caught in the wrong place. The portable test bench occupied a rectangle in the center. He had copied the distances twice, once in ink and once in pencil.

“You don't have to work on that now,” Marcel said.

“I'm taking a break.”

“From work?”

“From the cabinet.”

His mentor resumed filing. Three strokes, then another pause.

“Has a letter come?”

Elias shook his head.

“Did you go to the office?”

“Yesterday.”

“And?”

“My permit is still restricted to work within the workshop.”

“You knew that already.”

“So did they. It was a very evenly matched conversation.”

Marcel blew away a shaving. He did not smile, but the corners of his eyes tightened.

The warehouse drawing had not changed in four days. Elias had checked the subtraction until he began to doubt he knew how to subtract. The measurements recorded before the test were correct. He could have sworn to that, and not because he was especially fond of his reputation. He had repeated the checks.

The problem was what had happened afterward.

The sound always returned before the image. A sharp blow; then Etienne's arm where it should not have been. Elias had reached out and stopped a finger's breadth from the skin. He did not know whether pulling would free the man or finish what the field had begun. He remembered the sleeve dragged sideways, the cry that never became a word. Everything else arranged itself badly around that suspended finger.

Etienne could hold a glass now. The bench, though, was still there. Two sets of correct measurements, with an injured man between them. Elias had checked the figures until the paper wore thin: nowhere could he find the moment the world had stopped obeying him.

“Elias.”

Marcel's voice brought him back to the vise. He was holding the file motionless above the metal, unable to remember raising it.

Someone knocked on the open door.

Etienne Brac stood on the threshold, cap in hand. His jacket was too warm for the morning, and he held a folded envelope beneath his thumb.

Elias stood so quickly that his stool struck the bench.

“How's the arm?”

Etienne bent it.

“It bends.”

“I meant—”

“I know.”

Marcel indicated the chair by the window. Etienne sat down. Before setting down his cap, he ran his palm over the surface to make sure there was no oil.

Elias took a cloth and wiped the spot Etienne had already wiped.

“Edith says I need to start using it normally again,” Etienne said. “So you can both stop looking at it.”

They both looked at the envelope.

Etienne handed it to Marcel.

“They gave me this.”

His mentor unfolded the sheet. He read without moving his lips, then passed it to Elias.

The offer took up half a page. An amount, a date, a space for a signature. The rest was written in sentences that seemed designed to make a man read them twice and feel ashamed of the first attempt.

Elias reached the waiver of further claims. He went back. Read the sentence about the test again.

“This makes it sound as though you accept their account of the accident.”

“Does it sound like that, or does it say that?” Etienne asked.

Elias was already searching for another way to put it when he realized it was exactly the right question.

“I don't know.”

The workman nodded, without pleasure.

“That's what I said.”

Marcel wiped his fingers on his apron.

“Livia needs to see it.”

“That's why I came. You told me she understood those sentences.” Etienne looked at Elias. “I'm not asking you whether I should sign. I want to know what I'm signing.”

Elias already had an answer ready, one too big for him: don't sign, wait, we'll find the document, we'll prove everything.

He kept it behind his teeth.

“I can take it to her,” he said instead.

Etienne took back the sheet.

“I'll come too.”

He folded the letter along its existing creases, with a care Elias found difficult to watch.

“My wife says if I wait much longer, the shops will be deciding what happened. The baker's first.”

Elias lowered his eyes to his notebook.

He could not remember the wages Etienne was owed. Eleven centimeters behind the cupboard—those he knew. Elias closed his notebook.

“When did it arrive?” Marcel asked.

“Last night.”

“The envelope.”

Etienne handed it over. Marcel held it to the light, then gave that back as well.

He had not found a confession hidden among the fibers. Elias was grateful he did not pretend otherwise.

They were deciding where to meet Livia when she appeared in the alley.

Elias recognized her by her step before she reached the door: quick, but without the final burst of speed most people used to pretend they were not late. She carried a document case under one arm. Seeing Etienne, she stopped at the threshold.

“Good morning. I was looking for you.”

“Me or him?” Elias asked.

“Today, finding both of you suits me.”

Livia came in and set her case on a clear part of the bench. Marcel spread another cloth beneath it. She waited for him to finish.

Etienne showed her the letter.

Livia read it all the way through, then a second time, her finger held beneath one sentence. She did not speak at once. Elias realized he was holding his breath and let it out before anyone could notice.

“This sentence makes payment conditional on your acceptance of the attached account,” she said at last.

Etienne looked at the empty envelope.

“There wasn't anything else.”

“Then something's missing.”

“Can they put it in afterward?”

“You shouldn't sign a reference to a text you haven't received.”

“Shouldn't.” Etienne rubbed his thumb. “And if I don't sign?”

Livia handed the sheet back.

“I can help you ask for the attachment. I can't promise when they'll pay you.”

Elias wished at least one of them had a better answer. For a moment he resented her, irrationally, for having spoken so precisely that she had left no room for a comforting illusion.

Livia opened her case.

“I've brought the copy of the original position too.”

A drawing appeared on the bench, one Elias knew almost as well as his own notebook. The first time he had seen it, the relief had been so strong he had felt foolish. It was authentic. It was incomplete. Those two things still seemed incompatible.

Livia held down one corner while he moved the lamp.

“What is it supposed to show us?” Etienne asked.

“Where the test bench was when I took the measurements,” Elias said.

“Before I got hurt.”

“Yes.”

Etienne studied the lines. He pointed to a rectangle along the wall.

“Are these the crates?”

“The ones that were there at the time.”

“And this is the bench.”

Elias nodded.

The workman stayed silent long enough to make Elias want to explain everything: the reference point, the deviations, the values no one had questioned until someone had been injured. He bit the inside of his cheek.

Etienne raised his head.

“I remember where I was working. I can't tell you whether it was two steps farther to the right.”

“You don't have to,” Elias said.

He had said it too quickly.

Livia glanced at him. No obvious reproach. It was enough.

Etienne returned to the drawing.

“Who brought the bench in?”

“I don't know,” Elias admitted. “I arrived afterward.”

“Then perhaps you need someone who brought it.”

Marcel leaned against the vise. The curl full of filings fell across his forehead.

“I'd say so.”

Elias looked at the notebook lying open beside the drawing. For four days he had been asking the same figures for an answer they did not contain.

He picked up his pencil.

“The warehouse should have a record of the transport.”

“Perhaps,” Livia said.

“Or someone paid the men.”

“Perhaps.”

This time Elias smiled despite himself.

“Are you always this encouraging?”

“When you're trying to write down a certainty before you've found one.”

He did not write who had moved the bench. He wrote who might have seen it being moved, leaving the rest of the line blank.

Etienne picked up his cap.

“I can ask the others. But first I want the attachment.”

“The attachment first,” Elias agreed.

The workman stood, thanked Marcel, and left with Livia, who was explaining how to word the request. Elias watched until they turned out of sight. He had not offered to go with them. His permit did not prevent him from taking a walk. It prevented him from turning up at the warehouse to conduct another inspection. The distinction weighed on him more than he had expected.

Marcel took up the file again.

“You can finish the regulator,” he said.

Elias sat down. On the vise lay the small piece of brass with an initial engraved along its edge. Ruy marked even the pieces he would discard.

He set the piece back in the vise with the engraved side facing him. The jaw marked the metal if he tightened it too far; if he did not tighten it enough, the piece shifted at the first stroke. Before the accident, that balance had seemed something he had learned once and for all.

Now he hesitated even here. He opened the vise and started again.

Marcel turned the lamp. The line emerged from shadow and Elias's file finally found the edge. Neither mentioned the hesitation. In the silence came the scraping of metal and the wheel of a cart passing too close to the window.

“Can you see better like that?”

Elias tilted his head. The brass reflected a bright strip of light.

“Yes.”

The first stroke of the file was short. He was not measuring the warehouse or preparing an answer for a man who had not questioned him yet. A shaving lodged between the tool's teeth. He cleared it with the brush and tried to find the same angle a second time.

For a while they worked without speaking.

Then Elias looked up at the cabinet, still out of place. Behind it, the recess stood open, ugly and full of dust.

“Shall we put it back?” he asked.

“After you've cleaned.”

Elias looked at his mentor.

“You'd already found it, hadn't you?”

Marcel kept filing.

“It's my workshop.”

Elias laughed. It did him good, and for a moment he felt guilty about that too.

He took up the regulator again but left his pencil between two pages of the notebook. On one side were the recess's eleven centimeters; on the other, the bench he remembered measuring correctly. He had found the first error by moving a cabinet.

For the second, he would have to find whoever had moved something after him.
