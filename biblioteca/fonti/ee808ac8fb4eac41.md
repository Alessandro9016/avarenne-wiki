# AVARENNE
## Volume I — The Waking House
### Chapter 32 — What a Father Says

Etienne brought a sentence instead of a question.

“He says he didn't do it for himself.”

Alexander let him in. He did not immediately show the papers he could have used to answer. Etienne had not crossed the city to argue with a file.

It was May twenty-eighth. The conversation with his father had taken place under the conditions set by the office managing his custody, not in a room prepared by the duke. Alexander had not been there and had imposed no words to be repeated afterward.

“Did he tell you what he did?”

Etienne stayed beside the chair.

“He spoke about the gift. He said he couldn't leave everything to a boy who wouldn't listen.”

Edran felt a harsh reply rise so easily he did not have to search for it. He let it reach his mouth and then did not speak it.

Etienne looked at him.

“You don't have to make him better because I'm here.”

“I don't intend to.”

“Or explain at once that now I ought to understand.”

“All right.”

Alexander offered him the seat without placing the proceedings' papers before it. His friend had not requested another reading of the evidence; he had arrived with his father's voice still close enough that he must repeat it to understand where it ended.

Etienne's collar was folded on one side. The boy whose memories Edran possessed would have straightened it with a joke, a gesture too familiar to require a question. Edran kept his hands on the table.

“Did you see him today?”

Etienne explained only what he wanted to tell about the meeting. Not every question, not every hesitation. Alexander did not exploit pauses to reconstruct the entire conversation; he was listening to someone returned from a father, not cross-checking an interrogation conducted elsewhere.

The letter sent after the draft's acquisition had reached him. He knew enough not to turn the crime into a sentence extorted from an innocent man by another's will. He seemed no less lost for it.

Edran felt the discomfort of having nothing to resolve with a precise act. He had identified Alexander's killer and had evidence preserved. The son's pain was no technical residue to eliminate before considering the job complete.

Etienne looked at the chair and pulled it farther from the table. The duke let him choose the distance. He could not ask him to stay until everything was recognizable again and call that friendship.

The young man sat. He had slept little; this time fatigue did more than make him look less cheerful. It took away the interval between a thought and the decision to speak it.

“He asked whether I'd prefer our family to lose every role.”

Alexander recognized the trap without a power. A criminal choice was being placed back in the son's hands, provided he agreed to call it a choice made to protect him.

“And what did you answer?”

Etienne looked at the table's edge.

“That I hadn't asked him to kill my friend.”

The word entered the room without an embrace. Edran did not try to use it to recover everything else.

“I'm sorry,” he said.

Etienne looked up.

“For what?”

“That he tried to make you choose whether to be grateful.”

The young man ran a hand over his face. He did not cry. Alexander stopped expecting a recognizable shape of pain that would let him know what to do.

The young man rubbed his thumb along his cuff's seam. He had stopped seeking a sentence that accused his father clearly enough to authorize grief for him.

“As a child, he told me to keep my word even when it didn't suit me.”

Alexander waited.

“I don't know what to do with that now.”

Edran remembered a voice from his own childhood, good things learned in a world that had not therefore stopped crushing him. He did not use the memory to steal his friend's place. He said a useful rule would not begin to cause harm merely because its teacher had chosen to violate others.

“But speaking of it sounds as though I'm finding something to defend him.”

“You can speak of what was yours without absolving what he did.”

Etienne looked at the window. The thought brought no immediate relief; it left a task harder than repudiating every earlier day. He must distinguish what he wanted to keep from what he was being asked to justify.

“I don't want to become the one who knows how to hate correctly,” he said.

“I am not asking you to.”

“They ask without saying it.”

Alexander nodded. A room falling silent at a surname was enough to invite a position before a person knew what he wished to tell. The duke's title did not exempt him from that pressure; it could increase its effect even when he believed himself kind.

He said they could stop discussing the proceedings if Etienne wished. He did not answer at once. Then he chose to ask about findings already received, as though examining the verifiable part again let him find support while the rest changed shape.

They discussed the corroborating material obtained. Etienne had received the information the office could communicate to him; not every record, not access to the lives of everyone heard. The draft existed. The gift had been adulterated. Guilt did not need to become hereditary to be grave.

“I don't want my surname to enter here as a plea for forgiveness,” Etienne said.

“It isn't something you can pay for him.”

“And I don't want to lose every opportunity because letting me stay would be too convenient for everyone else.”

“You can submit the application you prepared. It will be considered for what it requests.”

His friend stared at him for a moment.

“I don't know whether I want that position anymore.”

Alexander nodded. He realized his most useful answer was, once again, not to turn a right into a compulsory direction.

“Then you can change your application.”

Etienne looked toward the window. The branch was still on its new support. It held no special meaning for him; Edran was grateful for that small freedom.

“Celine wrote to me.”

“She told me she would.”

“She didn't ask me about my father.”

The relief in the young man's voice almost became shame. Alexander did not take it away by explaining what his sister had really wanted. The letter belonged to a relationship he had no right to summarize for both of them.

Etienne turned the glass between his fingers without lifting it. Light gathered in the water and shifted as the rim moved. Alexander followed the reflection to avoid staring while his friend decided whether to speak again.

“I wonder whether visiting means letting him use the family as an excuse again.”

Edran did not prescribe a visit. The boy could neither repair the crime nor lessen it merely by sitting before his father. But he could choose what he would listen to and when to end a conversation.

“If I go back and he says the same thing?”

“You do not have to find a better answer every time to deserve to disagree.”

Etienne looked at him. The sentence did not resolve affection, anger, or fear of becoming like a man he no longer fully recognized. It at least offered a boundary he need not reconstruct in every conversation.

“I don't want to ask you how to love him,” he said.

“I could not decide that for you.”

For the first time that morning, lacking a solution did not seem to Edran a failure to disguise. He possessed enough power to remove a man's ability to refuse an answer. He did not want to treat a friend's pain as another thing to correct until it reached his preferred conclusion.

Etienne drank. The glass returned to the table with a slightly excessive sound. He moved it away from the papers and watched the cleared space. He had not yet decided what to do tomorrow. The room could contain him in that doubt without turning it into a promise to obtain before he left.

They stayed silent long enough to stop expecting a final sentence.

“I'm still afraid of what you can do,” Etienne said.

Edran did not look away.

“I understand.”

“I don't want you convincing me not to be.”

“I won't.”

The promise did not erase his material ability to violate it. Alexander did not try to sell it as a magical defense. It would matter only through the limits he respected even when no one could compel him.

Etienne stayed for a glass of water. When Maurice entered, he asked whether he could have something to eat; he had not had breakfast.

Maurice waited only to learn what he might bring. He did not ask whether remaining in the duke's house after such a difficult visit was appropriate. Etienne found time to thank him before he left, with a courtesy that did not yet have to justify his surname.

The butler provided it without asking him to deserve his appetite first.

Maurice offered him a more comfortable place for the plate. Etienne moved without treating every consideration as suspicious. Edran felt relief and forbade himself to read it as reconciliation already beginning.

The butler asked whether they wanted anything else. Etienne requested only water. The world could still contain him as a person who had forgotten to eat, not exclusively as Marcel Ravelle's son.

“The application wasn't entirely mine,” he said after a while.

Alexander waited until he swallowed.

“There were things I thought I wanted because they seemed the right way to be useful. Now I read it and don't know which were really mine.”

Fear of Will made the sentence more painful but did not exhaust it. A family could give directions without any supernatural ability. Edran did not offer certainty that he could recognize every authentic desire from outside.

“You can leave it on the table for a day. You do not have to submit it to prove your father has not taken your courage.”

Etienne nodded, then asked whether he spoke as friend or duke.

“At this moment, I am trying to listen as a friend.”

Etienne broke the bread. A piece fell beside the plate and he picked it up before answering. “Try listening when you haven't got advice, too.” Alexander sought a reply and did not find one quickly enough. The young man drank some water. For a few moments they simply ate.

Etienne stayed seated. The conversation did not become easy, but for a few minutes they discussed a sheet unrelated to recounting the crime. Edran let that small space produce no new commitment to demand before he left.

Alexander watched him break the bread and remembered a younger boy imitating his father with a walking stick too large for his arm.

“I don't know whether I'll come again soon,” Etienne said as he rose.

Edran opened his mouth to answer that he need not justify himself. His friend spoke first.

“Don't tell me I have permission.”

“I wasn't saying that.”

Etienne looked at him, and for a moment it was almost one of their old arguments. “You were about to.”

Alexander accompanied him to the door. Only then did he remember his hat and take it from Maurice. He would answer Celine, he said. He left Edran the following day without an appointment.
