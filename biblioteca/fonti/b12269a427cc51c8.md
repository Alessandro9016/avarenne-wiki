# AVARENNE
## Volume I — The Waking House
### Chapter 65 — Yselle's Signature

Yselle recognized her own name on the new document before understanding what was making her hand grip so tightly.

It was not the pen. That still lay before the representative, beside the sheet that would release the shares. It was the table's edge: she held it between thumb and forefinger as though it were a piece she had to keep from slipping. She loosened her grip and began at the first page.

The folder returned from the bank contained the rewritten text and attachments arranged in the order agreed that morning. The capital was reduced. Money already used and earlier work had not been swept away by the reduction; they stood in the section that still required settlement. Supplies replaced by the gift no longer appeared among those to be financed.

Martin had brought an extra chair without finding anyone to assign it to. He left it beside the table and came to stand by Yselle. She passed him the copy to compare references, keeping the original herself. The signature at the bottom would be hers.

“Let's begin with the collateral,” she said.

The representative opened the separate sheet. He did not hold it away or preface the reading with a speech about the value of the concession. He indicated where the new agreement replaced the previous terms and where the bank declared release of the lien on the shares.

Yselle read the whole sentence. Then she read it a second time, because the first time she had mainly heard the word release.

“Effective upon signing,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Not after the later installations.”

“Those remain work commitments in the forms indicated. They do not maintain the previous collateral.”

Martin found a reference and indicated it without interrupting her. Yselle followed the line: it still pointed to a paragraph in the old agreement. The referenced text was to be replaced, not preserved alongside the new one.

“This needs removing here,” she said.

“And in the attached copy,” the man added.

They corrected both points and initialed them. Yselle did not feel she had just foiled a trap. She had found an error that, left there, would have become a much harder discussion on another day. The correction showed; she did not mind that it showed.

On the nearby bench pumps two and three occupied the same space as that morning. They were the fact that had changed the negotiation. They had not signed for her and would not become false if the ink needed a minute to dry.

The representative returned to the capital section. “This is the amount remaining to be financed under the revised plan. Commitments already made are recorded here. What has been advanced isn't canceled.”

“It's the account we compared.”

Yselle followed it line by line. In her mind the old sum had occupied an almost physical place: she saw it when she entered the workshop, opened a letter, or Martin asked why she was doing the same accounts again after dinner. The new figure did not ask her to imagine less than had happened. It asked her to stop paying in advance for a fear whose cause had gone.

Real work and obligations remained. She wanted them on paper so they could be finished.

The support item had recognizable limits: what the workshop would deliver, what it would show the operators, checks of spare parts and instructions. There was no promise to be available at any hour for any future machine, nor a sentence obliging Alexander to go on creating every necessary part. The gift was documented by the attached deed, with the objects already present and identified.

“For the next demonstration,” the representative said, “you've indicated tomorrow.”

“Yes. The ninth. On the second pump, here, disconnected. Service at the Mutual continues with the first.”

He found the line. “A check of locally made replacement parts.”

“And how to work on the component without calling its creator. Not another installation delivered tomorrow.”

The man noted the clarification in his calendar. Yselle realized she was looking at that date without feeling the floor vanish. The ninth had become a day with work again, instead of the day after the possible loss of her shares.

Martin moved to let her reach the sheet. The copy matched. The donation did not appear as a loan, Alexander received no shares, expenses already owed remained acknowledged. The collateral would be released by this agreement, not left pending as a reward for future obedience.

Yselle picked up the pen.

The nib left a darker line on her name than she expected. She did not stop to correct the stroke; she finished the signature and dated the document. The representative took it, signed for the bank, and moved to the release sheet. Yselle followed that signature too. The little scratch of the pen was an ordinary sound.

They finished with copies and references to the attachments. When the representative handed her the counterpart intended for her, Yselle checked that the release was included. It was. The shares had not been seized, sold, or transferred to her nephew. The lien she had accepted for the previous plan ceased to hold them.

She placed the document before her.

The paper lifted slightly in the draft from the door. Yselle rested the ruler on it. A man passed through the workshop with a cart and asked whether he could cross. Yselle said yes. She had to move her chair to give him room, and that ordinary motion finally let her breathe fully.

The representative gathered his papers. He asked where to send communications about the new plan. She confirmed the workshop address, for her attention. She did not give the address of the house in Rovenne.

“The duke already has a copy of the donation,” she said. “This matter concerns me.”

The man nodded and noted the address. At the door he stopped to look at the pumps again, but this time did not bend to search for their price in the metal. He greeted Martin, thanked him for the copies, and left.

Yselle waited until his footsteps had faded from the yard. Then she turned to her husband.

“I still have my shares.”

Martin looked at the sheet and then her. “Yes.”

He did not add that they had always been hers. He would have been right in a sense narrow enough to hurt. He knew what she meant. He took her hand, the one that had gripped the table, and opened it with his thumb.

Yselle laughed. It came out too high and ended before becoming something she would need to hide. She sat again and pulled the unused chair closer for him.

“Now will you tell me to stop working?”

“I'd like to try on a day when I have some chance.”

“Today you might.”

Martin watched her, surprised. Then he took the signed papers and moved them away from the edge where they might spill a cup. “Five minutes, then.”

They spent them without opening a folder. The door stood ajar; from the yard came someone looking for Elias and asking where a box had been left. Yselle knew the answer, but her husband showed her five fingers and she stayed seated. The box was found without her.

At the end of the allotted time Martin picked up a sheet left beneath the ruler. It was the reduced alternative she had prepared before her nephew arrived, when the work still had to bend to what they could afford. One edge was full of calculations; on the other stood a repeatedly drawn outline of an object that would not fit the available space.

“Are we keeping this?”

Yselle took it. The silence of that evening returned, the fear of mistaking a smaller project for a free choice simply because it was the only one she could afford. It had been a serious alternative. She had been right to prepare it, and now did not have to carry it out to prove the work had not been wasted.

“We keep it with the earlier stages. Not among the sheets to carry out.”

Martin found a folder and offered it open. Yselle marked the edge so the superseded version could be recognized. She did not call it failed. The paper had done what it could until something better arrived.

Her husband stayed looking at the troubled margin.

“That evening, did you want me to tell you to give up?”

Yselle hesitated. “I wanted to be sure you'd stay even if I had to.”

Martin took the ruler from her hand and put it on the table. “I didn't marry the estimate.”

She smiled, then rested her forehead against his shoulder for an instant. The collateral had stopped tightening around her shares. To stop tightening around everything else, she would need that sentence too.

When she rose, she already knew what to do the following day. She called a worker and asked him to leave pump two free for the replacement-part test, with the tools within reach on the bench. She had the updated instruction sheet set aside. Maintenance had not become a punishment for those who could not create matter; it was the work that made receiving it useful.

Then she went to find Alexander.

She found him near the entrance with some of his luggage already ready. Teren was speaking to one of the two guards; the other was checking a bag strap. Her nephew saw the sheet before her smile.

“Signed?”

“Signed. Capital reduced, collateral released. What was already owed stays in the account.”

He did not reach for the agreement. He waited for her to show him the release clause, read it, and returned the paper.

“I'm glad,” he said.

Yselle wanted a sentence large enough to answer everything. Instead she hugged him briefly because the sheet might crumple between them. Alexander placed a hand on her back. She drew away laughing at her own care for the paper.

“I'll put this somewhere safe. Don't leave without the letter for Celine.”

“I'm waiting for it.”

She returned to the table with a sheet different from those just signed. Her niece's letter was still folded beside the folder; it contained a garden that would drive anyone crossing it with a wheelbarrow to despair. Yselle followed the path with her finger, then drew a more direct passage in the margin.

She wrote the protest against the curve first. Then she told her about the three machines, the first installation working, the old section that had failed, and Martin's bread. She avoided making Noemi's wound family news to distribute, leaving Alexander what he had already chosen to tell. She did not avoid the failure. Celine would find the hidden question even inside a cautious sentence.

When she sealed the envelope, a gray fingerprint remained on the flap. She rubbed her thumb, looked at the mark, and decided her niece would recognize the sender anyway.

The new contract was in the closed folder. The letter waited on the table, ready to leave with Alexander. Yselle looked at both and realized that for once they were not asking for the same part of her life.
