# AVARENNE
## Volume I — The Waking House
### Chapter 39 — The Part They Could Replace

Yselle Valcienne put a dirty component in his hands before asking his name.

“This part works,” she said. Then she pointed to its other half on the bench. “This part costs us.”

Elias held the cylinder in both hands. A polished band ran around its inside; near the rim, the metal turned rough. The hand that had recovered from the cut protested at the weight. He looked for somewhere to put it and found only a sheet covered in figures.

“Not there.”

He stopped. The engineer took away the sheet and cleared the bench.

“Now.”

The cylinder landed with a low sound. Elias realized he had been holding his breath.

“Veyne. Elias.”

“Good. Don’t drop it to shake my hand.”

Behind him, Gaston set down the folder. Marcel took off his coat and stood looking at the two halves. It was the second of June; for the first time in weeks, Elias had entered a workshop without first having to explain why a piece of paper allowed him to be there.

Yselle’s workshop was narrow at the entrance and much longer than it looked from the street. A row of high windows lit the main bench. At the back, beneath a stationary belt, a pump stood on its base. Dried water marks covered the floor around it.

On the shelf beside it were three empty spaces. Elias followed the rectangles marked on the wood, then turned back to the cylinder.

“Which bit works?”

Yselle pointed to the smooth band inside.

“The piston runs here. When this surface wears, I want to take it out and replace it. The rest of the machine ought to be able to stay where it is.”

“So it’s a removable liner,” Marcel said.

“That was my intention.”

Gaston bent closer. The engineer’s black hair was tied well enough to keep it out of her work and badly enough for a strand to escape whenever she moved. She pushed it behind her ear with her wrist, leaving a dark mark on her temple.

“Is this the third?” Gaston asked.

“The third I’ve tried getting out without cursing the designer.”

“And the designer?”

“Listens and takes notes.”

Elias smiled before remembering the woman’s surname. She had already moved to the other side of the bench.

An opening in the prototype had been left uncovered. Yselle brought the liner to it without pushing it all the way in. The component seemed to find the right position on its own: a recognizable shape, one rim stopping against another, the places where it needed fastening.

“It works. We’ve run the tests on the bench circuit. The problem comes afterward.”

She pushed the liner into place, temporarily secured the cover and handed Elias a long wrench.

“Try imagining that six months have passed.”

He looked at the clean metal of the fastenings.

“I’m taking it out?”

“Preferably before the season changes.”

The wrench went in from the side shown on the drawing. Elias sought the first fastening. He reached it, found his grip and tried to turn. The handle struck the support.

He lifted it to change the angle.

It struck again.

Behind him he heard the faint scrape of Marcel’s beard against his collar. The master had tilted his head. He said nothing.

Elias tried the other side. To reach it he had to tuck an elbow behind the pump; the end of the wrench slipped off the fastening.

“You aren’t obliged to beat the tool,” Yselle said.

“I’d give it one more chance.”

“The tool or yourself?”

The wrench struck again. Elias pulled it out and looked at it as though some part of the trouble ought to be visible on the handle.

Gaston spread the drawing on the bench.

In the drawing, the support did not cover the area where the handle needed to turn. The line was correct. So, apparently, was the measurement beside it. Elias followed the front view with a finger, then the plan.

The cover hid the place where the free space narrowed.

He put his palm against the pump’s body and let the distances come.

The visible surface ceased to be merely the thing preventing him from looking. Beyond the rim he sensed the nearby geometry: the cavity, the stretch through which the wrench needed to change its angle, the thickness represented in another view. For an instant the component offered him both faces without letting him choose the more convenient one.

The handle could not make that turn.

“Here.”

Yselle followed his finger.

“Which here?”

Elias realized he was pointing to something the others could not see. He took his hand away and picked up the pencil. On Gaston’s copy he marked the relationship between the inner rim and the support.

“The wrench reaches the nut. There isn’t room to bring it back after I’ve turned it.”

Marcel asked for the caliper.

With two tools and a few minutes’ work, they reached the spot. The ordinary measurement found the narrowing Elias had sensed. Yselle moved to read it herself; the strand of hair fell again, and this time she left it there.

“How much should we take off?” she asked.

Elias already had an answer on his tongue. He looked at the support.

“I can tell you how much room is missing. Not how much metal you can remove from that.”

“At last, someone who doesn’t want to improve my machine by sawing through it.”

She released the cover. Marcel held the piece while she lifted it; Gaston noted a second measurement. Elias traced the path with the loose wrench. Without the cover, his hand found room. That was precisely the advantage they did not want to have to buy by taking everything apart.

Yselle drew the original sheet toward her. She crossed out a measurement, left the old one legible and sketched a small additional view.

“We bring the access round this way. The reinforcement can stay here. I needn’t make it thin to defend the first drawing.”

Marcel stopped her at the new path. For several minutes Elias held the sheet still while the others discussed it. The engineer’s hands moved between paper and component without seeking a perfect match: when there was none, she went back to the paper.

The work Gaston had asked him to do began taking shape. Actual measurements. Access for the wrench. Room for the arm of whoever would remove the liner.

“Don’t draw his,” Yselle said, catching him gauging Marcel’s elbow by eye. “He already knows every way of making a space look small.”

The master stretched his arm over the drawing.

“It’s a skill that takes years.”

The pump test came afterward. They reassembled what they had opened and cleared the measuring tools off the bench. Gaston took the receiving vessel; Elias stood near the one from which the water would be drawn.

Yselle checked the level, then started the bench mechanism. The piston began to move. Elias felt the first stroke through the base and up through his shoes.

Water appeared in the pipe, stopped, advanced again.

“Don’t trust the first spurt,” the engineer said. “Wait for it to work.”

Elias watched the vessel fill. He remembered blood washing away from Etienne’s arm and found himself keeping his hands too far from the rim. The water continued on its way. The noise was loud, but regular.

Then it changed.

A hollower beat fell between two strokes of the piston. Elias raised his head. Yselle pointed out the lower vessel: the level had dropped beneath the point where the intake needed to remain.

She stopped the mechanism.

“Air,” she said. “The machine hasn’t discovered an opinion about water.”

Gaston emptied the upper vessel into the lower one. Some splashed onto Elias’s shoe. He stepped back and the engineer looked down.

“Too late for the distinction of a dry visit.”

That quick smile reappeared, the one that never seemed to interrupt her work. Elias accepted the cloth and wiped the water off the floor before they began the next test.

The pump worked. It did not need his enthusiasm to do so.

When they stopped it, Yselle let the test section drain. The full vessel was heavy enough to need two people; Marcel took the other handle without waiting to be asked.

“Where is it supposed to go?” Elias asked.

“To the Mutual’s depot,” Gaston said. “We’ll go there this afternoon.”

Yselle showed him the site drawing. A low tank, a run of pipes, a vessel higher up. Elias looked for the height and found it marked provisionally.

“You didn’t take that measurement yourself?”

“It’s a design measurement. I want the one on site. And I want to know whether whoever works there can reach the shutoff without climbing onto a crate.”

A voice from the back of the workshop asked where two packages should be put. Yselle answered without looking; a moment later she raised her head.

“Not on the drawings. No, not the old ones either.”

She went to receive them. Elias saw Gaston follow their arrival with an expression he might not have understood had his colleague not immediately looked at the empty spaces on the shelf.

The packages were small. They contained material, but not what was needed for three machines.

Yselle returned with an envelope and slit its edge. She read one line, then another. She folded the letter without returning it to the envelope.

“They aren’t coming?” Marcel asked.

“Not by the date they proposed.”

The master indicated the cylinder.

“We can revisit those after we have the measurements.”

“I know.”

She put the letter beneath a weight, away from the water. When she turned to Elias, she once again had the attention of someone asking for one thing at a time.

“Two days for your drawing. Can you do it?”

“If the site lets us take the measurements.”

“Noemi will let you take those. You’ll have to earn the rest.”

Gaston had started gathering the drawings. Beneath the site plan was a list of components in three columns. In the first, Elias recognized the body they had just opened. In the second someone had underlined a date; the third was almost empty.

“How many did you order?” he asked before managing to stop himself.

Yselle followed his gaze.

“What we needed. Not all from the same supplier.”

“If it’s only this part missing, can’t you make it here?”

The engineer picked up the rough cylinder and had him tilt it toward the window. The polished band seemed to widen.

“We can machine it. We can’t expect the part to turn out well because the last one turned out badly. Look where the wear began.”

Elias found the area. Something his exercise had not told him joined the measurement of the space: how a surface endured thousands of passes. He handed the cylinder to Marcel. The master turned it slightly and asked about the tool used.

Yselle answered at once. Then she took the component back, set it beside the good sample and left the letter where it was.

“I still need the measurement,” she told Elias. “If the way we obtain the parts changes, I don’t want to discover afterward that they can’t be removed.”

Elias looked at the empty rectangles on the shelf. Three spaces, he thought. He did not ask what they were worth before knowing which machines they were meant to hold.

“And for the parts?”

Yselle put away the wrench he had been fighting.

“For an answer that concerns me, until the eighth.”

She added nothing. Elias wrote that date beside the delivery date, then drew a line to keep them separate.

Before leaving, he asked to try the tool’s path once more on the dismantled liner. Yselle left him the component and returned to the letter. This time he found his grip at once. Without the cover, it would have been very easy to convince himself the problem was solved.

He put the cover beside the component again. That needed to be in the drawing too.
