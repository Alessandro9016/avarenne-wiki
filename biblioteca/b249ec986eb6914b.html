# AVARENNE
## Volume I — The Waking House
### Chapter 63 — A Name on the Envelope

Aurelie's name occupied less space than all the words Alexander had abandoned writing to her.

He had put it on the first sheet to avoid beginning with a title. Then he added a sentence about his improving health and crossed it out: it sounded like an administrator's notice about the condition of a building. The second sentence, more affectionate, said something he had not yet decided he could say.

The name remained.

In Yselle's house the rooms were dividing between tomorrow's work and work left until the day after. From downstairs came the sound of a folder closing; a chair was dragged near the window to keep the papers from damp. The letter to Celine had gone out that morning. Alexander had kept the simpler promise and now faced the one nobody had asked him to renew in writing.

Aurelie Belcour. Twenty years old. He had known her since childhood.

The duke's memories did not arrange her like a portrait in the frame of a contract. They contained the voice she lowered for a joke, the way she could point out his mistake without offering it to the whole table, a shared suspicion of adults who thought they could make them comfortable by suddenly leaving them alone. They also held boredom, waiting, words said too late. No declaration of love hid behind a detail to turn it into a story's inevitable conclusion.

During the previous year they had personally confirmed the engagement. Alexander remembered his own assent and hers. They were not simply two signatures added by others. The memory was clear enough to prevent Edran telling himself a convenient tale of imprisonment he could break.

Yet that clarity did not decide the present for him.

He rose and went to the window. Rain had left a dark line along the sill. Below, a man carried in a crate, complaining that tomorrow someone would expect not to find it right there. Another voice told him to put it where it did not block the door. The quarrel belonged to the world Alexander had learned to recognize in a few days: people who could oppose one another without becoming enemies of an eternal order.

He wanted a sentence equally concrete for his own situation.

He could imagine meeting Aurelie. He had enough memories to predict a tone, not an answer. He did not know what she had thought of the coma, the recovery, the weeks when part of her life had remained suspended before a distant bed. He could not deduce it from the fact that the duke had once known her wit.

There was no letter from her on the table to answer. Alexander stopped seeking the comfort of a question already formulated.

Yselle knocked on the open door carrying a small bundle. “These are dry.”

They were things he had left after the failure. He took them and set them on the chair. His aunt saw the sheet before Alexander thought to cover it. She did not touch it.

“Are you writing to her?”

“Trying.”

“She's one of the few people who can recognize when you're trying to sound more serious than you are.”

Alexander looked at the crossed-out line about health. “Then I'm starting at a disadvantage.”

Yselle laughed softly. She had a pencil tucked into her jacket lapel and noticed it as she sat: she removed it before breaking it. The sound of wood against the table made the name's presence less solemn.

“Last year you knew how to argue,” she said. “I didn't always find you in agreement.”

“I remember.”

“I wasn't asking whether you did.”

He lowered his eyes. Since waking, so many people had asked whether he remembered something that he had learned too quickly to hear the question even where it was absent. Yselle had simply spoken of the past as she might have before the coma.

“I don't know where to begin,” he admitted.

His aunt looked at the sheet without reading it. “With what you want her to know.”

Alexander thought about the difference between the body Aurelie would recognize and what she would find in conversation with him. He did not want to give Yselle the secret so he could ask her for an answer. “I want to meet her.”

“Then ask her.”

“What if the way I speak is different?”

Yselle studied him. Not as though she had discovered another man in the room; with the more ordinary and difficult concern of a relative who had watched someone she loved change.

“She might tell you,” she replied. “You might hear her say it.”

He realized he was still waiting for reassurance that would spare him the meeting. His aunt did not give it. Instead she asked whether he wanted to send the letter with an ordinary delivery: someone would take one last packet into central Lethorne that evening. They could entrust him with this too.

Alexander said yes. Yselle rose to leave him to write, then turned back at the door.

“Don't promise you'll always be as you were just because you think it'll make answering easier for her.”

“I haven't written that.”

“Good.”

Once she had gone, Alexander sat. He took a new sheet but left the crossed-out one beside it. He had no need to pretend he had found the first sentence at the first attempt.

He wrote that he was well enough to travel, that he was staying with Yselle in Lethorne, and that the next day he would return to Rovenne. Visits could not be timed merely to prove he had nothing left to fear; he would not rush to see her tonight to remove a difficulty from the table along with the ink.

Then he said he wished to meet her and speak directly, when it was possible for both of them. He did not ask to see her before his departure. He offered to agree on a later day without choosing it for her.

The letter could have ended there, correct and too distant.

Alexander took up the old sheet and looked at the name. A minor memory returned: as youngsters, during a visit, he had mispronounced the name of a place, and Aurelie had supplied the right form within a sentence, allowing him to repeat it without being corrected before everyone. Later she had teased him in private. The duke had protested against the second part and retained his gratitude for the first.

They had found themselves beside a window that could not be opened because, according to the adults, it would send a draft across the whole table. The young Alexander had insisted he had used an accepted pronunciation. Aurelie had asked accepted by whom.

“By someone who's been there.”

“You haven't been there.”

“Exactly. He has.”

She had pretended to consider this with attention so serious it made him suspicious. Then she asked whether that someone had also taught him to order water, since at the table he had twice received wine he did not want.

Alexander had turned toward the glass left full. He found nothing to answer, and Aurelie laughed without hiding behind her hand. She had a way of laughing that immediately ruined the possibility of pretending to be two older people. The memory held it whole: the warmth by the closed window, the sound of a spoon set down badly, his own irritation destined to end before the visit.

Now Alexander drew the glass of water on his table closer and drank. He was surprised to find himself smiling alone. The scene did not tell him how Aurelie would react to a letter written after his awakening. But it reminded him that the young woman had been able to surprise him even when the duke considered himself experienced enough to predict her. To meet her, he would not have to reconstruct a lost feeling with more solemn words. He would have to leave her room to find an answer he had not prepared.

It was not declared love. It was a person who had known how to hold consideration and wit together. Edran wanted to know whether he could know her that way now too, instead of merely seeing her through a complete and closed memory.

He added that he would prefer not to leave their next meeting solely to their families' formulas. The sentence risked sounding like an accusation against a decision they had also made themselves. He rewrote it: he wished to speak to her as they had already done when personally confirming their engagement. No announcement of a break; no renewal turned into a promise of love he did not remember making.

He set down the pen. Part of him could have filled the page with perfect accuracy, using every memory to choose the most suitable word for the young woman he knew. Precision would not give him what he lacked: Aurelie's answer, present, spoken by her.

In the corridor Martin looked for Yselle and received an answer from the opposite room. Her husband asked whether a copy should remain open; she said to close it, then corrected herself and called him back. Life with another person was not solved even in this house where they seemed so good at finding each other. Alexander felt relief in the sound of their steps returning together.

He reread the letter aloud, quietly enough not to include the corridor. In the passage about waking he had written that he had returned. He stopped at the word. For Aurelie it would mean her friend coming out of the coma. For him it contained something he was not telling her.

He did not replace it with an even more distant formula. Instead he wrote that he wished to speak about his recovery and what he wanted to do in the months ahead. It was true and did not force her to understand a secret through a riddle.

The signature took less time than the salutation. Alexander Valcienne was the name that would open the envelope, the title that would accompany the sheet into Aurelie's hands. He did not try to erase its weight by choosing only his first name. He signed and watched the ink turn dull.

When he went downstairs, Yselle was placing her own packet in a delivery folder. He showed her the sealed envelope. She checked the address so it would reach the right destination, then gave it back. She did not ask to read the contents.

The courier arrived shortly afterward, annoyed by the rain that had begun again. He checked the number of packets, arranged them in his case, and listened to the destinations. Alexander entrusted him with the letter to Aurelie in person. It was a quick gesture; the man's hand hesitated no more than it had before the workshop papers.

“If you find it closed?” the duke asked.

The courier explained how it would be left for the next delivery. No door forced open by the title, no promise to bring him an answer that night. Alexander nodded and let him go.

From the doorway he watched the case move away down the street. He had not decided a marriage with a page or freed Aurelie from a choice she had not asked him to dissolve. He had asked to meet her. That could be enough for the evening, provided he did not mistake waiting for an answer.

He returned to the guest room and picked up the crossed-out sheet. He folded it with the name inside. Tomorrow he would have precise things to do before the journey; for the question just sent, there was no reading to anticipate.

From the corridor Teren asked whether departure was still planned for after lunch. Alexander confirmed, adding that he would wait for news from his aunt before getting into the carriage. The security chief nodded and returned to his men.
