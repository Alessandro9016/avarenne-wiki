# AVARENNE
## Volume I — The Waking House
### Chapter 12 — A Day's Work

Vern Soller did not receive them from behind a desk.

He was dividing a job among three men, and finished before turning to Marcel. One of the three came back to ask him to repeat a measurement. Vern did so without irritation, pointing to the place where he did not want an estimate.

Elias found it unfair.

He would have preferred to discover an incompetent man, someone who got everything wrong and had never had the right to make decisions for others. The lot supervisor knew his work. That was a part of the problem Elias could not erase.

One of the men returned with a tool unsuited to the task. Vern sent him for another, then added an explanation rather than merely correcting him. Elias recognized the patience of someone who knew how much time was lost when an order was only half understood.

That patience irritated him more than the first brusque answer. He could not even promise himself that next time he would easily recognize a dangerous person.

Etienne was looking elsewhere. He did not seem occupied with constructing an image of Vern that would contain all his anger. He wanted something more precise. Perhaps that was why he had less need to hate him in every gesture.

“Are you finished?” Vern asked.

The lot supervisor waited for an answer that did not immediately come. Elias was looking at the three men behind him. Their presence made him want a sentence that would show at once who was wrong; he feared that hesitation before them would become another proof of incompetence.

Marcel passed him the folder without opening it. That small weight put his hands back to work.

“We want to compare the readings with the time the passage was cleared,” the master said.

Vern looked at the door. People uninvolved in the conversation were coming through, trying to work out whether they could pass.

“Come to the side.”

They moved far enough not to block the entrance. It was no concession of privacy, and Vern made no attempt to send Etienne away. The worker rested a palm against the wall, then removed it at once because of the dust. Elias saw him wipe it on his jacket and thought he would remember that gesture more easily than the entire opening sentence he had prepared.

Vern still had a tool in his hand. He put it on a crate to listen better. A small patch of sweat remained on the metal.

Vern called an instruction toward the continuing work. One man answered and left with the tool on the crate. Elias watched the quick, habitual movement and felt Etienne's wrist in his hand again. The same man knew how to keep the lot working and had chosen something that injured him.

He sought the beginning of the first series in the folder. Gaston helped by turning two pages, then left him room to speak.

“We’ve compared the two positions,” Marcel said. Gaston closed the folder on the first series, keeping a finger between the pages. A worker’s footsteps made Vern turn; Elias waited until the foreman was looking at him again.

“The bench was moved,” Elias said.

Vern looked at him.

“I've never said it's stood still since they built it.”

“After my measurements.”

“We cleared a passage.”

The answer came so easily that Elias lost his next sentence.

Marcel gave him time to find it.

“The certificate stayed the same.”

Vern looked at Gaston's file.

“There were declared margins. And a day's worth of men already here.”

“Would recalibration have taken another day?” Marcel asked.

“It would have stopped the lot until you'd done it again.”

“You have my signature,” Elias said. “Not my presence after I left.”

Vern turned toward him. “I also have a crew paid to work. The margins were declared.”

“For that position.”

“Then show me the difference.”

Elias had the plan in his folder and an answer ready in his throat. But the warehouse was over there; pointing at a line from the corridor would show nobody the field. Marcel touched his elbow before his tone could do the evidence's work. Elias moved away, angry with him too.

Gaston opened the file.

“The second set is later. We can establish that without arguing over margins.”

Vern took the sheet. He held it far enough away to read comfortably. Elias watched his fingers on its edge and realized he was waiting for a tremor, a sign of fear that would grant him a simple satisfaction.

The man returned the page.

“Bring it to the hearing.”

“It will be there,” Marcel said.

Etienne, who had stayed near the door, spoke only then.

“My day's work wasn't recovered.”

Vern turned to him.

“I know you've lost work.”

“Then don't ask me to sign that everything's consistent with someone else's mistake.”

Etienne had approached without leaving the wall. He was not holding his arm in front of him. Elias looked briefly at his notebook, ashamed of how easily the worker's healed body became an argument whenever he wanted either to deny the harm or make it more visible.

“I've read those sentences,” Etienne told Vern. “If I sign, I have to stand by them outside this place too.”

The man tried to answer about the proposal's preparation. Etienne let him finish, then asked whether he agreed with its contents. The question lacked the elegance Elias would have sought. It had the weight of a person who would go home with his signature on the sheet.

Vern moved to take the document. Gaston handed it over, open at the right passage. For a few moments, only the sound of workers putting away tools remained. It was worse than a hostile audience: nobody seemed to know beforehand which side they would want to applaud.

Elias felt a sudden fear that Vern would find a sentence different from the one remembered, an explanation he would not know how to answer. He realized he was holding his breath and released it.

Marcel did not fill the silence. Nor did he offer a more accommodating version of the question to make the answer easier. He waited with Etienne.

The lot supervisor brushed the margin of the sheet. He seemed older than he had while assigning work. Elias forbade himself to make evidence of that too: a long morning could tire an innocent man, and knowing that would not erase what they were asking Vern.

The three men behind them had stopped talking. One lowered his eyes to the piece he was carrying. No one needed to understand every figure to know what was being discussed.

Vern ran a hand over the back of his neck.

“I didn't write every sentence in that offer.”

“But a sheet of paper didn't make the decision about the passage,” Elias said.

Vern looked back at him. Weariness had hardened his face.

“No.”

Elias searched for his name on the proposal Gaston held open. He found it in the reconstruction, absent beneath the signatures.

“When this was prepared, did anyone ask you whether the bench had been moved?”

“I answered the questions they asked me.”

“Not this one?”

Vern looked at Marcel. “Do you want to conduct the interview again here?”

“I want to know whether I can take this answer with me,” Gaston said, indicating the notebook.

The foreman turned back to Elias. “I wasn’t asked in those terms.”

Gaston wrote that down too. Elias could not tell whether the answer made him angrier: the form had managed to ask Etienne for a signature without asking Vern where the bench stood.

Elias looked for Marcel's eyes. His mentor gave no gesture of victory. He let that no remain what it was: an answer about a real decision, not a confession to every imaginable responsibility.

Gaston recorded the words, then read them back so Vern could correct them. The man did not ask to change them. Instead he looked at the workmen waiting behind them, as though only now realizing that his way of making decisions had become something other people could observe.

“You can ask them to change it,” Etienne said.

The supervisor did not answer immediately. Then he said he would examine the request. It was less than a promise and more than he had said when they arrived.

Outside, Elias walked too quickly. Marcel caught up without calling him.

“He knew.”

“He spoke about margins.”

“He knew.”

“And you have to keep the words he said. Not the ones you hoped he'd say.”

Elias stopped. His injured hand throbbed beneath the gauze.

“It wasn't difficult to understand. If you change the reference point, the measurement doesn't apply to the new position.”

“No.”

“Why did he do it?”

Marcel looked toward the entrance.

“You can understand a rule and think that, just this once, the cost of following it is too high.”

Elias remembered yesterday's stairs, Gaston saying he had used them a hundred times. The resemblance irritated him. He did not want every error in the world to become an excuse for Vern's.

“That doesn't make him right again.”

“I wasn't trying to make him right.”

Etienne came out after them. He had spoken to a colleague and seemed less tired, although he had been the only one to ask to sit.

“Is the inspection over?” he asked.

Marcel nodded.

“Then I'd like something to eat before going back.”

Elias wanted to review every note immediately. He surprised himself by asking where.

At the first crossroads Etienne stopped at a shop window. He read the price on the glass and searched his pocket. Elias had already reached for his own purse when Marcel pointed across the road: a cart was blocking the crossing. They had to wait.

“Here or there?” Etienne asked.

Gaston looked at both signs. “They let you sit down over there.”

“There, then.”

They crossed together. Elias reached the door first and held it open; Etienne passed him without checking whether he had gone back to hunting for his notebook. He had chosen somewhere to eat, and the young man would have to follow his lead in that too.

Etienne chose his bread. He sat near the wall, leaving Gaston the place where he could stretch his leg. When Elias tried to talk about the bench again, the worker instead told them something his wife had said the evening before. He had tried putting something away at home without asking where it belonged. She had found it in the first place he had already checked, and now used the incident as her best argument in any discussion about order.

Elias saw Marcel draw his plate closer. The man listened with a different attention from the one he had given Vern; the beginning of a smile had appeared on his lips.

“It might have been there from the beginning,” Gaston said.

“That's her position.”

“And yours?”

Etienne broke the bread with his healed hand.

“That she could have spared herself waiting until I'd searched everywhere.”

They laughed. Elias had heard similar stories many times at the workshop; that day, he found it surprising the worker still possessed one to tell. He himself had reduced Etienne in his mind to the man injured during the test.

When Etienne resumed eating, Elias left the notebook closed. He was learning nothing he could present to an office. He was finally ceasing to demand proof of his innocence from everything the other man said.

The shop kept filling with small sounds. A plate arrived at the next table, someone asked for water, the door admitted a cool gust. Elias managed to remain seated long enough to feel hunger return.

It was no use to the hearing. Elias listened to the end.
