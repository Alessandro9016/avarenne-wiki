# AVARENNE
## Volume I — The Waking House
### Chapter 44 — The Security

The pot had begun to burn while Yselle searched for the right sheet.

Martin came over from the table, lifted the lid, and turned off the heat. He opened the window before facing her. Someone was beating a rug in the courtyard; with every blow, a gray cloud passed the sill.

“A difficult choice,” he said. “Would you rather eat smoke or dust?”

“There are carrots.”

He looked into the pot. “There were.”

Yselle put the papers on the stool, finally beyond the steam, and took two plates. Her husband left her the clean spoon. The upper part of dinner could be rescued; the lower part would require a thorough scraping. She served him with an irritated precision that neither he nor the carrots deserved.

Martin tasted it. “The salt survived.”

“Eat it before it changes its mind.”

They sat. It was June third, and enough light still came through the window to read the headings on the papers. Yselle had promised herself she would not touch them until after the meal. Instead she watched the fold of the sheet on the stool, where one word remained uncovered: security.

Her husband pushed the bread toward her. He said nothing. That absence of a question made her wish, with unreasonable impatience, that he would ask.

“The liner came out,” she said.

“I noticed. I played an important part.”

“I mean without distorting. After the second assembly, too.”

“And the drawing is better.”

Yselle turned the spoon against her plate. The food seemed too hot to eat and too cold to justify leaving it there. “If a drawing were all we needed, we would be finished.”

Martin stopped with the bread in his hand.

This was not the first evening they had discussed the project. He knew about the material problem, the June eighth deadline, the difference between a demonstrated machine and an order someone was prepared to support. He knew his wife had pledged her shares. He had asked how much risk there was, and Yselle had answered that the security was proportionate to the work. That was a precise sentence only until you tried living inside it.

“I brought the contract,” she said.

Martin finished chewing. “For me?”

“So we can look at it together.”

“I thought you had already shown it to the people who needed to read it.”

“They've read it.”

He did not immediately reach out. That small delay was worse than a reproach. Yselle got up, fetched the contract, and put it on the clear stretch between their plates. A wrinkle in the tablecloth prevented it from opening flat; she smoothed the cloth with her palm before turning to the clause.

Martin read slowly. He taught mechanics; interpreting other people's precautions was not his livelihood. He returned to the start of the paragraph and followed a line with his nail.

“It says here they can claim against the pledged shares.”

“Yes.”

“And what remains yours to decide if they do?”

Yselle had imagined questions about money. This one entered the place where she had stopped looking for an innocent word.

“Less than now.”

“Yselle.”

She pulled her plate closer, needing something to move. “The part that lets me oppose a decision wouldn't be the same. And I might never get it back.”

Martin lowered his hand. Another blow sounded from the courtyard. Less dust came in now; someone had moved the frame. Yselle could have described that person and where they worked instead of remaining seated opposite her husband.

“When did you sign?”

She told him without checking a calendar. She remembered the room, the scratching pen, the dry skin on the knuckle of the man who had shown her where. Nothing in the memory resembled an impulse. She had calculated, negotiated, rejected other forms of commitment. That was precisely why it had been easy to tell him she had settled the matter.

“I wasn't asking your permission,” she said.

“I didn't say you should have.”

“I know.”

Her husband leaned against the chair back. The wood gave a creak she had known for years. “You told me you were risking part of the value. You didn't tell me you might lose your say in what happens afterward.”

She looked at the line beneath his nail. She had read it many times. It had not changed because Martin was reading it now.

“I thought I was making it smaller for you while I could still solve it.”

“For whom?”

Yselle did not answer. It was a question he sometimes asked pupils who had reached the correct result with the wrong figure. She would have preferred him to use it at school.

Martin picked up his spoon and immediately set it down. “I'm angry. Can I be angry without you having to prove the project is good?”

“The project is good.”

“I know.”

The certainty with which he said it made her feel worse, then better somewhere she could not have named. She took a breath long enough to smell the smoke still in the room.

“I don't want to end up asking a board for permission to do the work they keep me there to do.”

Martin looked at the contract, then at her. “And I don't want to find out afterward what price you've accepted to avoid that.”

The bread remained between them, untouched. Yselle found the table edge beneath the cloth. It was chipped at the corner where they had struck it bringing their first bench into the house; Martin had never sanded it properly. Facing the contract, that household flaw seemed something solid to rest her thumb against.

“Alexander is coming on the fifth,” she said.

“I know.”

“I haven't asked him to cover the security.”

“I know that too. I read the invitation while you were looking for an envelope without stains.”

She closed her eyes for a moment. “He might want to see something that wasn't made to receive him.”

“It will be good for him. For you too.”

“He can say no.”

Martin frowned. “To what?”

Yselle realized she had already assembled a request in her mind that she had never written. It was not money, yet. It was the expectation that a nephew who had become a duke should find his aunt's possible failure interesting. She rubbed her forehead, perhaps leaving another workshop stain.

“To taking an interest. To coming back. To wanting a part in this.”

Her husband nodded. He offered no consolation borrowed from someone else's wealth.

They finished eating with the contract open. Martin rescued the last carrots that did not taste burned and carried the pot to the water. She told him to let it soak. He tried with the spoon anyway, made an unbearable sound, and admitted she was right about that metal, at least.

Yselle cleared the table. She returned with a clean drawing board, the lamp, and the estimate. Her husband stood behind his chair, still far enough away that they could not pretend everything was resolved.

“I want to prepare the smaller version,” she said.

“One machine?”

“One service line, to begin with. The same required height, less simultaneous work. We'll have to tell the Mutual what changes.”

Martin took the chair. “How much of what you've already paid for remains useful?”

At last it was a question she could answer with an object. Yselle opened the drawing and pointed to the removable liner and the guide. The courtyard survey remained useful; so did the work on the surfaces, the gasket test, the correction to the internal passage. Components ordered but not obtained needed a separate distinction. She could not cancel a cost merely by erasing the line from a drawing.

She made two lists. Martin removed their nephew's letter from beneath her elbow before she could stain it with ink. It was not enough tenderness to make peace, but it was the gesture of a man who intended to remain at that table.

“Doesn't that reduce the time to fill the tank?” he asked, indicating one change.

“It increases it.”

“Then don't just write lower capacity. Write how long they'll wait, once we have the figure.”

Yselle erased the phrase. That wait had arms, buckets, and a woman who had to choose who could use the courtyard. She would not leave Noemi to translate a saving into all its consequences.

Before going on, Yselle searched the drawer for the pipe sample she had kept. She put it between the bread and the lamp. Its edge had the flare she had rejected for the larger version. It might be enough for the smaller one; she still had to check whether the connection left the locking ring accessible.

Martin brushed the crumbs aside and held the component while she set the drawing board on edge. The line passed too close to the support. Yselle shifted the outlet, but her husband made her try with a real wrench from the box near the door. The handle struck the wood before completing its travel.

She looked at the marked point, then at the sample. The smaller version would not come into being simply by erasing two machines. She removed the pipe from the table and began a second view in the margin, leaving room for the hand that would have to tighten the ring.

They drew until the window became a black mirror. Yselle did not like the smaller version. It had the humiliating appearance of a compromise that genuinely worked: it preserved the part of the project she wanted to defend and lost some of the service she had promised. Martin checked the lever's travel and found an obstruction she had allowed to pass through a support. Yselle corrected it without arguing. Her hands had resumed work before her irritation surrendered.

Eventually he got up to wash the pot. This time its bottom came away in soft flakes. She heard him humming a tune that never ended in the same place, and had to stop herself from calling merely to hear his reply.

When he returned, she showed him the smaller drawing. “If nothing changes on the fifth, I'll send this with its limits. I won't wait until the eighth to say something is missing.”

Martin studied it. “And if things change?”

“I'll redo the figures.”

“All of them?”

She met his eyes. “The ones I haven't told you about, too.”

Her husband laid his hand on the edge of the drawing without covering the lines. Yselle rested hers beside it. They did not immediately take each other's hands. It was strange how much space could lie between two thumbs on the same sheet.

“I'm teaching late tomorrow,” he said. “Leave me the paper where I can find it.”

“Which paper?”

“The one you're afraid of.”

Yselle wanted to make a joke. Instead she slid the contract toward him. Martin folded it along its existing creases, with a care that did not remove his anger. He left it beneath the lamp beside the smaller alternative, and only then brushed her fingers.

She remained at the table after he had closed the window. No sound rose from the dark courtyard. The pump in the workshop was stopped, dry where it should be, capable of working. June eighth had not moved farther away. The security clause seemed no less dangerous.

Yselle took a fresh page and wrote Noemi's name at the top. Beneath it she listed the things she would have to tell her if they chose the smaller version. The first was time. The second was what they had already promised. At the third she raised her head and called Martin, because he had seen something in the lever's travel that she wanted to check again.

Her husband answered from the next room, his voice full of the bread he had finally decided to eat.
