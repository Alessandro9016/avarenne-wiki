# AVARENNE
## Volume I — The Waking House
### Chapter 7 — The Things That Continue

Albin Sere had waited until the following day to bring the accounts. The act of consideration had clearly cost him more than the climb to the study.

Alexander indicated a chair. The administrator arranged his case, checked that the ink was dry, and began with the balance.

“Seventy-seven thousand five hundred crowns. Seventeen thousand three hundred already committed.”

The figures in the duke's memory were different. Edran followed the transactions one by one, rediscovering the logic of a world that had not stopped beside his bed.

“So sixty thousand two hundred available.”

“Before any new decisions.”

“Have you ever had an owner capable of forgetting that part?”

Albin looked up.

“Rarely before his first purchase.”

Alexander smiled. The other man did not appear to regret his answer.

Wages were due on the twenty-sixth. The summary did not include names, but Edran remembered many: people the boy had met at the mills, in the courtyard, outside a door as they waited to be received.

“Continue with the commitments already approved. I don't want anyone waiting in order to prove they're indispensable.”

Albin made a note. He did not thank him for authorizing what was already owed. Edran liked that too.

The next folder concerned a road. Alexander remembered a maintenance request and looked for the point at which the work would fall to his private estate. Albin showed him that this matter belonged instead to the territorial administration.

“The money doesn't come from this balance.”

Edran looked at the two statements. Wealth and authority overlapped enough to create a confusion useful to anyone inclined to exploit it. The young duke had received enough instruction to recognize it, when someone gave him time to read.

“And my signature here doesn't replace a decision there.”

“Exactly.”

“Did you prepare two folders because I was likely to confuse them?”

Albin touched the edge of the one about the road.

“I prepare two because they are two.”

For a moment Edran saw another table, impatient hands around a map, people convinced that a victory would make every later decision simple. Albin's precision seemed an uncelebrated form of courage.

Alexander followed the edge of the map with his finger without touching the ink. In the duke's memories, that route belonged to a landscape seen from a carriage; in Albin's folder, it belonged to jurisdictions, requests, and people who would have to work on it.

“What if I left this house and had the people who work for me repair that stretch?”

The administrator waited to discover whether this was an order.

“I am asking you what would happen.”

Albin moved the private-property sheet aside, leaving the territorial map alone. He explained that commissioning work did not transfer to the landowner the right to decide for everyone who used the road. They needed to know who had authorized the access points, where the area to repair ended, what obligations would remain afterward. None of this made the road more complicated than it already was; it merely revealed people a line on a map had concealed.

Edran felt an old impatience rise between his ribs. He could have done something immense without asking for a load of stone. He could have prevented a body breaking in a pothole. He knew that, and had no intention of turning complexity into an elegant excuse to remain seated.

“In an emergency, I can act.”

“You can. Afterwards, someone needs to know what you have done.”

Albin was not disputing the power to prevent harm. He was asking not to discover one day that a direct order had shifted another cost, closed an access route, or promised maintenance in the name of an office that knew nothing about it.

Edran leaned over the map. The stretch he had indicated crossed more than one boundary. He returned to Albin's first word, access, and asked who used it today. The administrator turned the folder toward him. The mark on the paper stopped being a line to straighten.

“I want a question that can receive an answer,” he said. “Not a folder that circulates until someone forgets why it was opened.”

“Then let us begin with which stretch and which problem.”

Alexander looked at the map once more. The question brought him back to the present better than a recommendation to rest. If he wanted to be useful here, he would first have to learn about the place where he wished to exercise that usefulness.

He left the pen beside the statement. There was no signature to put at the foot of that thought.

He returned the file without signing it.

“For new requests, I'd like to see you rather than simply provide a signature.”

“I can arrange short meetings.”

“And if I say something foolish?”

The administrator met his eyes cautiously.

“I'll explain it to you.”

“In front of someone else too?”

He considered the question with a new attentiveness.

“If waiting would cause harm, yes.”

“Good.”

Albin did not smile. But he moved his pen, as though he had finally decided this conversation was more than a test of a convalescent's faculties.

Alexander could have made him more confident with a word. He found he preferred the slight discomfort of still being judged.

In the duke's memory, that answer would have stung a pride still young. Edran found it restful.

He asked about the household documents next. Albin immediately distinguished the estate registers from the receipt of gifts: Maurice was better placed for those. He did not try to become competent at everything in order to preserve his position.

“Ask him to come when he's free. There's no need to interrupt the service.”

The folder remained open between them. Alexander noticed a minute correction in the margin, a figure rewritten by a tired hand.

“You dealt with all this during the crisis.”

“The parts that fall to me.”

“It's still a great deal.”

Albin ran his thumb along the fold of the case. For the first time he had no answer ready.

Albin's silence lasted long enough for the clock's ticking to come to the foreground. Edran watched the administrator's knuckles, still on the folder. The boy whose memories he possessed might have sought a polite phrase to end the awkwardness. He found it more useful to give it some space.

“You were accustomed to working with my father.”

“Yes.”

“And now you have to explain everything to me again.”

“Not everything. Some things you remember better than you think.”

Edran did remember. Phrases heard without interest returned to him, the location of a folder, Lucien's voice when a decision could have seemed easier if a name were omitted. He had not lost Alexander's education. He had to choose what to do with it now that the house no longer expected a son standing behind the duke.

Albin reopened the statement listing commitments. He showed how they were distinguished from payments already made. Alexander let him finish explaining. Halfway through, he caught himself wanting to interrupt to prove he already knew how to read that part; precisely the sort of gesture that forced those working with a powerful man to guess how much more than him they were permitted to know.

Instead, he asked about a condensed entry. Albin explained it without offering a new expense to approve. The total remained the same. Their way of looking at it had changed: not a reserve of favors, but money someone would soon need for work already done.

“If you find me impatient,” Edran said, “that is no reason to skip an explanation.”

“I may find you impatient with many explanations.”

Alexander laughed. The administrator lifted his chin slightly, as though testing the strength of a support before putting more weight on it.

That caution brought an unexpected melancholy. He had known people who could contradict him without first looking at his hands. With some, he had shared hunger. He did not know what remained of them in the world, and would not force Albin into a dead person's place to spare himself the time of getting to know him.

“The statement is enough for today. We shall address the rest one thing at a time.”

Albin closed the road folder and left the wages folder open. He showed him where to find the deadline again. Edran looked at the number, then the hand indicating it. He had obtained something rare in an impossible awakening: a commitment that existed even when he stopped thinking about it.

Two voices came from the corridor. One belonged to Celine; the other was trying to convince her that waiting would be more appropriate. Alexander recognized Sabine Varel before seeing her at the door.

“You said I could ask,” Celine said.

“I did.”

Sabine carried a book with a sheet tucked between its pages. Celine reached to take it, then gave up, as though she had to prove before her brother and the administrator that she had not come in for something too small.

Edran remembered the weight of an audience. People with real hunger had waited for him, alongside others ready to call a desire for power hunger. He had needed to learn the difference; sometimes, instead, he had merely learned to make them all wait alike.

Albin rose and moved his chair to clear the way. He did not retreat under a pretence of secrecy nobody had requested. The administrator's simple consideration prevented Edran from treating the girl as an interruption to manage.

“Is the book what you are looking for?” he asked.

“The one to take back. That's why I told Sabine we needed to go.”

Alexander looked at the slip between the pages. There must be a deadline, or perhaps simply a wish to choose something else. The library did not need to conceal a clue to justify the importance of the outing. His sister had asked for a piece of the day, and he could listen.

The girl glanced at the papers and stopped.

“I can come back.”

“You can also ask.”

She wanted to go to the library. Not the house library: the one in the town, associated in both their memories with a promise left unfulfilled. She had already arranged a time with Sabine, who had not yet approved the outing during such an uncertain convalescence.

Edran felt the impulse to get up and go with her. His body could cover the distance easily. The papers on the table were already irritating him. Then he noticed that Celine had not asked him to come.

“If Sabine can accompany you, that's all right.”

The governess nodded. Celine kept looking at him.

“What is it?”

“Nothing.”

“Would you prefer me to come too?”

She considered the offer long enough for her answer not to be mere politeness.

“Another time. I need to look for something.”

“Then good luck with it.”

Celine gave him a small, surprised smile and left with Sabine. Their conversation resumed almost immediately in the corridor. They were no longer talking about him.

Albin arranged the papers without comment. Edran was grateful.

“Has Yselle left any news?”

“She asks for an update, not a promise about the project.”

Alexander remembered his aunt with a technical drawing before her, the boy's hand pushing it aside to reach a letter he considered more urgent. The shame of the memory did not entirely belong to Edran. The rudeness, if he repeated it, would be his.

“Tell her I'm conscious and would be pleased to see her. Without making her travel here only to find me asleep.”

Albin noted that too.

Alone, Edran turned the statement over. The back was blank. For an instant he sought the other face of a far older tablet, crowded with names no accountant could have read. His five Effigies still answered him. Yet Albin, looking at him, had found a young man who needed to learn to have wages paid on time.

He turned the sheet back. The twenty-sixth was eight days away. In this household's calendar, Alexander's father would never have another appointment.

Edran marked the wages. Then he called Maurice: he wanted the letter to Yselle actually sent.
