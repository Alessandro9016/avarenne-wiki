# AVARENNE
## Volume I — The Waking House
### Chapter 60 — The Gift and the Work

Yselle crossed out the price of the second pump and realized she had been holding her breath.

The figure had remained in the estimate even after the first revision. Out of habit she had removed material they would not have to buy, then the operations replaced by the creation; she had left a total in the wrong column, as though the machine's presence were not enough to stop her ordering it a second time. She drew a firm line through it and added the figures again.

The new result was much lower.

She was almost afraid to look at it. Not because it made the miracle suspicious, but because it erased a threat that had occupied her nights so precisely it seemed a property of the future. On the bench beyond the papers, pump number two waited motionless. The third stood beside it. Both existed without having asked her for the money budgeted to build them.

Martin placed a ruler on the sheet before the draft could lift it.

“Did you count something twice this time?”

“This time I stopped.”

She showed him the column. Her husband followed it without pretending to recognize every item at first glance; when he did not understand the distinction between work completed and work no longer necessary, he asked her to indicate the two documents.

Yselle found the prototype invoice and placed it beside the order that would not be sent.

“This gets paid. They worked on it. We aren't ordering that anymore.”

Martin moved the ruler beneath the second line. “Then you don't need to leave the allowance for delivery here either.”

He was right. Yselle crossed out that item too and returned to the sum. She did not thank him in the special tone she might have used for a husband who had exceptionally managed to understand her accounts: she passed him the next sheet and trusted his reading.

Alexander joined them as the papers began occupying the side bench too. He had left Teren at the entrance. He stopped in front of pump two and tapped it with his knuckles as he might have knocked on a door.

Yselle looked up. “Does it answer you?”

“Only if you do it for it.”

“Then today it costs less than expected.”

He came to the table and looked at the new statement. Yselle did not immediately push it toward him. First she took the sheet she had prepared for something other than accounts.

“I want to put what you've given us in writing.”

Alexander read it. “Three pumps.”

“With the replacement parts specified here and those for yesterday's intervention. The pipe section, the supports, the shield that stays at the Mutual. There are descriptions that let people identify them without calling you or me.”

He followed the list. He did not try to add a noble value to the gesture. “That's fine.”

Yselle held the edge with two fingers. “Not in exchange for shares.”

“I haven't asked you for any.”

“I know. I'm writing it for people who weren't there too.”

Alexander watched her a moment and nodded. “Then write that there's no repayment to be demanded later.”

The line made her smile. “I already have.”

Martin showed him where. Alexander read it and sat down. The pen was on the other side of the table; Yselle passed it without ceremony. He asked only whether the description of the spare parts included the specimens already physically present. She indicated the inventory with its references to the crates. She was not asking him to sign for an infinite warehouse she could open whenever an estimate became inconvenient.

“The rest of the work stays ours,” she said.

“I see that.”

“Not just to keep you at a distance. After this morning, we know the first service works. We need to be able to maintain it when you go home.”

Alexander put down the sheet. “I want to go home without taking away what you've received.”

Yselle indicated the two motionless pumps. “These won't follow you.”

“Good. The carriage would be crowded.”

Martin drew the document toward him and checked that the references were legible. The joke had eased some of the tension, but had not replaced the words. Alexander signed the deed describing the donation, Yselle the acceptance. Martin witnessed the act and returned the pen; his hand had not become that of an owner of his wife's shares.

Yselle let the ink dry. She felt no urge to snatch the sheet back at once to stop her nephew reconsidering. He remained seated, looking at the statements beside it, and neither of them needed to ask again whether the gift had been accepted.

“The financing changes,” she said.

Alexander looked at the old total and the new one. “By a great deal.”

“By everything we no longer have to buy or build. Not the work already done. And not what we'll need for installation, support, ordinary stock.”

Martin touched an item with the ruler. “This is the part I misread. I thought it disappeared with the component.”

“The component arrives ready. The people who need to work on it don't.”

Yselle took the test report. Elias and Ruy had left concrete readings, with the water reached, the stops and restart. The replacement of the old pipe appeared as a separate intervention, not concealed among the pump's qualities. She placed it beside the donation deed. Now she had two things she could take to those financing the project: assets already present and a demonstrated service.

But the agreement in force still held her shares as collateral. No line crossed out in the estimate had released them by itself.

“Tomorrow I'm not asking for more time to do the same thing,” she said. “I'm asking them to finance what exists now.”

Martin nodded. “And release the collateral.”

Yselle felt how much it still cost her to say it directly. Fear suggested a weaker sentence: review the collateral, discuss it later, do not ask too much. She looked at the machines and discarded that weakness. She had risked the shares for a need that no longer existed on the same scale. She did not have to pretend the capital was unchanged to seem grateful to those who had believed in the prototype.

“Yes. Release of the shares held as collateral. This is the new capital.”

She wrote the proposal without turning to her nephew to borrow the weight of his title. The first paragraph described the changed supplies; the second listed expenses that remained, the third the replacement of conditions linked to the previous capital. She added no promise of the duke's personal support. His name stood beneath a defined gift, not beneath the future of her enterprise.

Alexander read only the passage concerning him. “This makes it sound as though I have to confirm the material's stability every time it's installed.”

Yselle took up the pen. In haste she had written a sentence that left an unnecessary obligation open. “That wasn't what I meant.”

“Then today's declaration is enough. The machines don't depend on where I am.”

She rewrote it. The matter remained stable; checks for use remained technical. The new sentence was not more cautious than the first; it was truer and offered her a stronger position.

Martin read the page from the beginning. He stopped at the ownership clause and asked her who would receive the Mutual's copy. Yselle marked the destination and added the delivery reference. Pump one was already on site; the second and third remained in the workshop inventory beside the crates of spare parts. She went to check the numbers on both and returned to the table with the pencil between her fingers.

When the messenger arrived, he found three sheets open and an envelope already addressed. Yselle gave him the packet with a request to get it to the representative before closing. She wanted them to discuss the proposal the following morning, not discover that things had changed.

The man checked the address and asked whether he should wait for an answer.

“Confirmation that they've received it. I'll discuss the rest in person.”

The door closed. Yselle stood a moment, her hand still where she had held the envelope. The deadline remained June eighth. The bank had not become a friend, and the contract had not dissolved because of a good test. But the question she would bring to the table was no longer whether the future would pay for what was missing. A decisive part of what had been missing stood behind her, ready and numbered.

Alexander rose. “Do you need anything else from me today?”

Yselle looked at the signed document. “Stay tomorrow until we know the result. I might need you to read a sentence, not pay a difference.”

“Departure is in the afternoon. Teren knows.”

His answer lifted a small weight. She could have pretended she had felt none, but he was her nephew as well as the man who had filled that floor with machines. She squeezed his arm and let him go.

She and Martin remained. Her husband gathered two copies and aligned them, tapping their edges on the table. He looked at her with a question that was not about handwriting.

“It isn't over yet,” Yselle said.

“I didn't ask you that.”

She sat. “I'm afraid they'll still say no.”

Martin did not assure her they would accept. He took the old estimate, placed it beside the new one, and left both totals visible.

“Then they'll have to explain which of these things they're saying no to.”

Yselle looked at the two sheets. The difference had cost her only an afternoon of corrections, after months spent pursuing a supply she could not afford. The rest had been done by a person sitting before her a little earlier. Admitting it did not diminish her pride; it made her want not to waste what that gift had opened.

The messenger returned before they closed the workshop. He had an acknowledgment of receipt and a smaller sheet in another hand. The representative wanted to see the machines remaining at the workshop and the results from the first installation. He asked for the revised statement to distinguish clearly between commitments already made and canceled expenses.

Yselle read it twice. There was no promise of acceptance. There was someone prepared to discuss the new capital rather than pretend the papers had never arrived.

“The distinction's already here,” Martin said, looking for the corrected sheet.

“I'll make him a more legible copy.”

Her husband took the pen. “I'll do that. You answer the question about the machines.”

Yselle indicated the clear bench where the representative could put his papers without turning the visit into a procession among workers. She needed to ask Elias to be there with the dimensions, and Ruy with the material checks already completed. The master could answer questions about the checks that bore his name. She wrote the two messages and left them ready for ordinary delivery.

Then she replied that pumps two and three were available at the workshop and that she would bring the test report together with the signed donation. Martin finished the copy and handed it to her. In the revised account she recognized his hand before even rereading the figures.

Outside, a cart stopped with a sharp bump. She turned abruptly, then recognized the sound of wheels against the step. Martin recognized it at the same instant and did not tease her.

Yselle returned to the paper. She removed one last duplicate from the final estimate, copied the total, and closed the folder for tomorrow.
