Chapter 68 — The Open Account

The glove was too small for Mael's hand and too large for the name assigned to it in the inventory. Operator's textile accessory, the card said. It did not mention the two fingers sewn inside because its owner had been missing the real ones.

He set it beside the goods ledger without putting it on. The hardened leather had preserved the curve of a grip. Whoever confiscated it had found it cheaper to leave the grease than pay for cleaning. Mael had bought the crate along with four unusable instruments, and the glove was the only thing that had yet to bring him an offer.

Sybille came in carrying a folded newspaper and a loose sheet. She did not sit. She looked at the courtyard door, the corridor door, and the passage between the shelves, then laid the newspaper over the glove.

“They're manufacturing miracles in Lethorne now.”

Mael moved the paper, irritated by the way she treated things without a price. “The newspaper or the workshop?”

“The newspaper sells the headline. The workshop apparently has the pumps.”

The article occupied little more than a column. The duke had visited Yselle Valcienne's project; three machines had been provided, and one was already serving the Mutual. The writer's name did not appear. One sentence promised benefits the rest of the text did not establish. Mael read the words attributed to a worker twice and those attributed to the financier only once.

“Who gave you the other sheet?”

“News bought along with the newspaper. The workers saw three machines. Nobody unloaded them from a wagon. That's the story.”

“Who saw it?”

“Not the person who sold the news.”

Mael pushed the sheet back. A complete lie would have been more agreeable: at least he would have known where the work began. The duke was young, rich, and close enough to Yselle to let her risk a piece of publicity. He might have paid for machines, brought them by a means the others did not know, or created them. The last possibility changed the value of many agreements. It did not tell Mael what abilities he possessed or how much he had chosen to show.

“Someone says he healed a woman,” Sybille added.

“The same source?”

“Another rumor. I don't have a statement from the woman.”

Mael drew a line beneath two lines in his own note. He wrote rumors, not facts, and hated the caution of the word. In the past he had seen men lose a warehouse because an implausible report proved correct. He had also seen entire investigations chase after a paper seller. The difference cost time.

The loose sheet also contained a drawing. A rectangle, a wheel, three parallel lines: the seller had called that a pump. Mael held it near the lamp and found a pencil impression on the back. The man had copied the drawing through another sheet, or written over it. It was not evidence of the machine. It was evidence of a desire to sell a drawing.

“How much did you give him?”

Sybille named the amount. Little enough to make an argument pointless, enough to ensure the seller would seek another piece of news by morning.

“Don't buy the next installment.”

“If he brings a name?”

“He'll bring the name he thinks you want to hear.”

She took the sheet away from the light. “Sometimes he brings the one he heard. We aren't the only clever people in the city.”

Mael could have reproduced the handwriting before him, remembered every irregularity, compared it with something authentic. He would not have turned that pencil stroke into a visit to the workshop. He let Sybille put the drawing back on the desk and pointed to the only useful line: the name of a street where the story circulated among people delivering materials. A place to check later, without showing up carrying an open newspaper and questions about the duke's wonders.

“That one,” he said.

“I know it.”

“That isn't the same as knowing who works there now.”

Sybille nodded, and he realized he had just repeated an objection she might have made to him. He let it pass without correction.

Sybille looked at the glove. “What is that supposed to do?”

“Nothing.”

“Then why keep it on the desk?”

“I'm looking for the right crate.”

It was not a lie. Nor was it the reason he had picked it up. The glove reminded him how quickly inventories freed objects from people. He had learned his trade in rooms where a desk could be sold before its owner finished protesting. He had been good. He had remained good when his sister was the one protesting.

He closed the ledger before reaching pages where he might have looked for a name. The past did not need his help to continue existing.

“Is Ilian coming back tomorrow?” he asked.

“He's supposed to contact someone who can answer about the collar. He hasn't promised to bring anyone here.”

“I didn't ask him to.”

She looked at him. Mael allowed her time to decide how much of the sentence was true. The returned sample and the rejected request had taught him at least this: pressing the same point of access increased its price without making it any more available. Ilian might still obtain an answer about an ordinary component. People willingly gave a small question the attention they denied a large one.

“His balance?” Sybille asked.

“When the job is complete.”

“The old one.”

Mael took a sheet from the pile on the left. The amount had been acknowledged; payment remained suspended. Each true word made actual money less necessary. Ilian had worked and wanted to be paid. As long as he came back to ask, there was a conversation to continue.

“I'll write that the account is under review.”

“He knows that already.”

“Then he'll be pleased to hear it hasn't been forgotten.”

Sybille did not smile. He had purchased her abilities, not her amusement. That boundary had always seemed generous to him.

He rose to fetch two shipping documents from the shelf. One concerned a disused press, the other a maintenance kit. They were ordinary forms, full of spaces meant to receive names no one would remember at the destination. The buyer of the access wanted a usable device; the latest letter asked whether a person capable of keeping it working was also available. It no longer said only device.

Mael left the letter in its folder. Sybille had read her own contract, the terms of payment and access to the premises. She had not read the sheet through which he had arranged for her possible delivery. He had no reason to show it to her that evening.

“If the duke really made the pumps,” she said, “he could make other things.”

“We aren't going to ask him.”

“It wasn't a suggestion.”

“Good.”

His answer came out too sharply. He turned the document toward her, showing only the section concerning the route. A shipment bound for the port did not have to appear bound for the port the entire way. An intermediate warehouse with a credible reason to receive it would suffice. That was work they both understood.

“That address doesn't receive machinery anymore,” Sybille said.

Mael went still. “Since when?”

“Last month. They've leased the yard. There are crates of fruit.”

He had an updated map in a drawer, a list of concession holders, and three genuine attestations; none had told him the courtyard smelled of pears. He crossed out the address with a single stroke.

“Another one.”

“I'll give you one after I've seen it.”

“I didn't ask you to go tonight.”

At last she sat. She picked a thread from her sleeve and wound it around a finger. “Varek wants to know how much longer she should keep asking about the same suppliers.”

“As long as the questions look like her work.”

“It is her work. That's how she knows when they start sounding strange.”

Mael accepted the correction with a nod. The distributor had a reason to be in the street, to know doors and shifts, to ask who had moved shop. A reason that could be used up. She was sixty-one and had no wish to spend the remainder of her life explaining why she sold newspapers beside the wrong person.

“Tell her to stop mentioning the duke. We need supplies and deliveries, not family gossip.”

Sybille tilted her head. “She'll like that order.”

Mael returned to the desk. From the drawer he took an old form that had already been canceled. The paper bore a destination that no longer existed and the stamp of an office that had moved. He could have copied the marks. He did not need a false stamp inviting the first conscientious clerk to ask a question. He needed a story ordinary enough not to require a visit.

He wrote grinding equipment, then crossed it out. Too specific for a device they did not possess. He wrote sampling equipment. Beneath it, accompanied technical kit. The second expression seemed better until Sybille read it upside down.

“Accompanied by whom?”

“Whoever knows how to set it up.”

“Then it isn't a kit. It's a work trip.”

“That depends on how you declare it.”

“It also depends on who gets off at the destination.”

The thread broke between her fingers. She had not raised her voice. Mael felt an unreasonable need to make her hold her contract again, to force her to look at the precision of her signature. Instead he folded the form in half.

“We have nothing to transport yet.”

That was the point on which they could agree. No hinge had arrived at the warehouse, no maintenance worker had accepted a job, no door to their objective had opened. The temptation to act simply to stop waiting was an expensive form of impatience. He could send them against the workshop, and perhaps obtain an enormous machine of no interest to the buyer and a duke interested in him. A very poor exchange.

“Tomorrow Ilian asks the commercial question,” he said. “A measurement, a part, a price. If they ask about the recipient, he names whoever is supposed to receive the work. No visits to the Mutual out of curiosity.”

“Can we pay for the part?”

“If it's worth what they ask.”

“Ilian is asking for something that's already worth it.”

Mael rested his palm on the closed ledger. She saw the gesture and rose before he answered. She was also good at choosing where to break off a conversation. She still had the advantage of knowing she could leave it through the door.

“Get me the new address,” he said.

“After I've seen it,” Sybille repeated.

She collected the newspaper but left the sheet of rumors. The glove lay uncovered on the desk. After she left, Mael listened to her footsteps as far as the courtyard; he did not attempt to follow them beyond what he could hear. The bolt returned to its place, and the warehouse resumed the small noises of badly settled crates.

He opened the buyer's folder. Sybille's second contract lay beneath the recent letter, protected from the light. He did not touch it. Instead he found the line recording the payment owed to Ilian and copied the date of the new contact above the old one without crossing it out. Even an open account needed to be kept in order.

For a few minutes he reread the news about the pumps. The paper offered nothing more. The duke remained distant, his power undefined; those who worked with him had schedules, creditors, hands that could grow tired. Mael would not try to buy the miracle. He could look for whoever brought it tools.

He picked up the glove to return it to the crate. A strip of cloth came out of the cuff, bearing a coarse seam: a repair made at home, not a function of the object. He carefully tucked it inside again. Then he changed the card. Under quantity he wrote one, not a pair; under condition he added incomplete. It was an accurate description. He extinguished the lamp, satisfied to have corrected at least that.
