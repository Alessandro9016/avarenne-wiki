Chapter 61 — The Shadow Side

They had lit the lantern above the gate that was supposed to stay dark.

Sybille saw it from across the street, standing beside a woman searching for her key at the bottom of her shopping. The cone of light reached the grate, cut through the passage beneath the cart, and ended against the wall. The day before, at this hour, the gate had offered a continuous stretch through to the yard. Tonight someone had decided to see the lock better.

The woman found the key. Sybille moved to give her room, then crossed without looking at the lantern again. She carried a light parcel, large enough to suggest a delivery and small enough not to hinder her movement. Inside were nothing but folded canvas and a piece of wood.

The man at the gate asked whom she wanted.

“The yard behind. I was told to come through here.”

“During the day.”

“Then they only told me half of it.”

He indicated the corner. The ordinary route added an alley and a gate; he had no interest in denying her that. Sybille thanked him and continued. She did not put out the lantern or stretch night over the whole street. The gatekeeper watched her as far as the first corner, then went back inside.

In the alley the cart's shadow joined the shadow of a low awning. A few meters of real darkness, from the side of a wheel to the wall beyond the axle. Sybille stopped where her body could reach it and checked the exit. An overturned basin, two crates, no feet beyond them. Footsteps in the yard came from farther away.

She invested the passage through the contiguous shadow.

The edge of the cart slid past her without forcing her to crawl beneath its bed. Her body changed position along that reached stretch; the parcel stayed tight against her hip, her sleeve touched the awning's wood. When she stopped, she was on the other side of the wheels, less than four meters from where she had begun. She left no other Sybille behind to bear the risk for her.

The lantern light interrupted the continuation. To reach the gate she would have to emerge, cross the visible section, and begin again from a different shadow. From here she could not even see inside the next passage.

A door opened. Sybille dimmed the contrast of her coat against the wall and stayed still while a man brought out a container. Her steps made no sound; the container did when he set it down. The man bent, letting his shoulders protrude into the band of light, and spat into the dirty water.

Sybille recognized the cloth knotted at his belt. He was one of the porters Mael had used at the warehouse, not someone she had been promised as an accomplice. She should have asked what he was doing there. Instead she waited for him to go inside and noted the detail. Being recognized would turn a route test into a conversation with too many destinations.

She returned along the stretch she had already reached, then left the alley on the side she had entered. The street's air brought the crowd's noise back around her. A man struck her elbow and swore as though she had sought his arm. Sybille answered in kind and let him go. Sometimes the simplest cover was an unpleasant temper.

The route through the gate took several minutes. In the yard she found a guard seated beside a lamp. The man had one foot on a stool; the other kept time to a song he was not singing. The light divided the passage in two and let him see anyone coming from either side.

Sybille could have tried breaking a call to him, if someone had called. That would not remove his eyes. She stopped before the visible point and chose another route.

The alley's second exit was no mystery. It had a squeaking hinge and led to the longer way around, where carts turned. Sybille passed with the parcel in her arms, endured a stretch of exposed paving, and returned to the warehouse street. The guard had no need to realize there might have been someone to stop.

When she reached Mael, she could still feel the fold of the wood hidden in the canvas against her palm. She set it on the desk and checked both doors before sitting. One led to the stairs, the other to the storeroom corridor. The window was not a convenient third exit: a narrow awning lay below, and tonight just enough rain was falling to make it slippery.

Mael looked at the parcel.

“You brought it back.”

“I wasn't supposed to deliver it.”

“The route?”

“The first stretch works. They've lit a lantern at the main gate. The connection ends before the inner gate.”

He took the little sketch he had given her and looked for the point. Sybille moved his pencil to the right corner. She had not traversed his entire trading network; she had checked a street and an alternative, which was the work she expected to be paid for.

“Can it be put out?” Mael asked.

“You can also ask the gatekeeper to let a cart through. Choose which thing you want people to remember.”

Mael looked up. A brief smile crossed his mouth. “I prefer the cart.”

“And there's a guard beyond. I went around him on foot. I won't promise to make a delivery vanish while he watches.”

“I was asking you for a route.”

“I'm giving you the one I found.”

Sybille left the pencil on the sketch. Mael did not insist that her power grow larger than the alley. He asked about the gate and the time the longer route took. She answered up to the point she had actually traveled; beyond it she left the paper blank.

He took out a second sheet. It was not another route. It contained a request worded to seem compatible with many things: delivery, availability, commissioning. Sybille read it standing because he would not bring it close enough.

“The device isn't enough for them,” she said.

Mael held a finger on the edge. “They want to know who maintains it.”

“The draftsman?”

“Or someone who knows how to make it work. That will depend on what they're shown.”

Sybille reread the word availability. She had concealed departures where someone's availability had been decided by somebody else. She knew the smell of closed carts and the too-quiet voices behind canvas. She did not need Mael to explain how far a well-worded delivery could go.

“Ilian still hasn't received his balance,” she said.

“We're clarifying the request.”

“Exactly. If you want an answer instead of a refusal, you'll have to clarify that too.”

Mael withdrew the sheet. He did not ask her to bring Ilian by force. He did not need to for her to understand the distinction in his head between a technician willing to travel and a service that would have to reach its destination regardless.

Sybille looked at her own copy of the assignment in her coat pocket. She did not take it out. First she mentioned the man she had recognized in the yard.

“One of your porters unloads there.”

“Which one?”

She described the cloth and the hand he had used to hold the container. Mael recognized the man, or pretended to; he gave her no new name to seek out. He said the yard received goods from many people.

“Then tell him not to greet me if he sees me.”

“Are you afraid of being recognized?”

“You pay me not to be.”

He liked the answer. She regretted making it so easy. She had done the work, found the obstacles, and brought a result; she did not want every piece of information to become proof of their resemblance.

“Let me see my part again,” she said.

Mael took the assignment file, the one he had shown her, and placed it before her. Sybille took out her own copy. She aligned the sheets, followed the lines with a nail, and checked the point they had discussed on the evening of June second. Leaving the assignment remained tied to the written terms; checking the route did not become agreement to any future destination.

“It's identical,” he said.

“I'm reading it.”

A cart struck the wall in the corridor. Sybille stopped her finger on the line and waited for the noise to pass. She did not want to be distracted precisely when the sheet seemed reassuring. Her name was where she had left it. The words around it were those agreed. Of other papers she could see only closed spines in Mael's case.

She returned the document. She knew an identical copy would not force a man to be honest in the rest of his life. But she could show it to someone if she needed to claim her pay or prove what work she had accepted. For the moment that was enough to keep her seated.

Mael asked whether she had found a better house.

“I didn't tell you I'd found one.”

“You look at it when you think you're looking at nothing.”

Sybille put away the copy slowly. “Then look less at me and more at what I've brought you.”

Mael took the route assignment and added the completed check. Sybille waited for him to finish, then read the note before allowing him to put it with the others. The longer detour was missing. She made him add it. No shadow would shorten a distance he wanted to forget on paper.

“This brings us closer,” Mael said.

“To what?”

“The delivery that pays for your house.”

She leaned against the chair back. “I pay for the house. You pay me.”

He opened his hand as though granting her a verbal nicety. Sybille let the gesture pass. In the house she had visited there was a small room beside the kitchen; the owner had called it a limitation, she had seen a table beneath the window. She could use a key and leave it in the lock without anyone having the right to come in and ask for work.

That wish did not erase what she was helping Mael prepare. She could see a technician between the words of the request and still straighten the road along which they would bring him. The thought troubled her enough to make her close the pocket on the house prospectus, not enough to make her get up and leave without agreeing on what came next.

“You handle the gate with an ordinary delivery,” she said. “If you want me to look at the yard side again, find me a reason to go in during the day.”

Mael wrote that down too. Sybille checked that the sentence concerned access, not transporting a person, then rose.

On the stairs she met a messenger coming up with a letter. She let him pass without trying to read it. She was tired of looking for her name on sheets she could not open, and she did not yet have proof she could act upon.

Outside, rain had wet the street, erasing the sharp edges of shadows beneath the wheels. Sybille reached the lit street and set off toward her lodging. At the first doorway a woman asked her to hold the door while she brought in her shopping.

An onion slipped from the net and rolled against her shoe. Sybille picked it up with her free hand and set it on the inner step, beyond the edge where rain was falling.

She kept holding the door open. She watched the key turn from inside and left before the woman turned to thank her.
