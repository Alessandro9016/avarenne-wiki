Chapter 66 — The Road to Rovenne

Yselle's letter arrived last, when Teren had already checked that nobody had left a bag inside the workshop.

Alexander picked it up from the table. There was a gray fingerprint on the flap and Celine's name on the front, written with care that ran out quickly at the final letter. He put it in his inner pocket, separate from papers he would not need to open during the journey.

His aunt's agreement was signed. She had shown him the result: capital reduced, share collateral released, work already owed still acknowledged. Alexander carried no title to the machines or stake in the enterprise. Behind him, on the workshop floor, pumps two and three remained whole.

Yselle also handed him the book he had left in the guest room.

“Is this yours?”

“Celine's.”

“Then take it back. If you forget it here, she'll write that I've confiscated it to stop her reading interesting things.”

He took it. The marker remained near the beginning; he had read less than he thought. Celine might ask what he had done with it in three days, and changing a workshop would not provide a good answer about that chapter.

Noemi arrived while Martin was telling him where he had placed the last bag. She did not run. She stopped at the gate to let a guard carrying a box pass, then approached with an envelope held flat against her side.

“Before you go.”

Alexander recognized the sheet she had begun writing after the failure, now concealed in the envelope. “The questions?”

“The ones I've managed to put in order. The rest, if you like, I'll ask aloud.”

She handed it to him. The paper's edge had been folded several times and smoothed again. Alexander did not look for a list of people already waiting for a promised healing. Noemi had brought him a way to begin discussing it.

“I'll read them on the way,” he said.

“I haven't written what you must do by tomorrow.”

“I understood.”

The woman looked at her wrist, almost annoyed by a habit she had not yet lost. The skin was whole. She had not needed to keep her arm still to preserve the result.

“There's a question of my own,” she said.

Alexander nodded without asking her to say it in front of Yselle and the men preparing to leave. Noemi lowered her voice anyway, though not to secrecy.

“I'd like to discuss it without coming to represent everyone else.”

“Then we'll talk about you.”

Relief did not erase her fear. It let her smile. “For the water, though, I can speak for the whole house. This morning I had to look for someone I used to find on the stairs with two buckets.”

Yselle asked where she had gone. Noemi described a different task, long waiting for someone with free hands. Martin laughed at how promptly work found the time released. That did not make the result smaller: there was no longer that weight to carry upstairs.

Alexander put the envelope of questions beside the letter for Celine. He would need to speak to Mirel when he had a way to arrange future meetings. He had not already examined the people Noemi knew, nor would their names grant access to their bodies. What he had was a real capacity to offer and an equally real wish not to leave it confined to a cut he happened to witness.

Teren approached. “We're ready.”

The two guards waited with their horses. The driver was in place; the carriage had regained its smell of warmed leather and road. Alexander said goodbye to Martin, then embraced his aunt. Yselle kept a hand on his arm an instant longer than the farewell needed.

“Write when you've arrived.”

“Celine will do it before me.”

“That wasn't permission to answer through your sister.”

He promised news of his own. Martin told him not to judge the whole household's cooking by one burned slice of bread; Noemi asked whether that bread was already in the letter. Yselle said it was now a documented fact.

Alexander climbed in with the book. Teren sat opposite. One guard moved ahead of the carriage, the other stayed behind; the driver guided the horses out of the yard and onto the road to Rovenne.

The city did not fall away all at once. First they passed gates Alexander had learned to recognize, then the shops grew less dense and spaces appeared between buildings. A cart loaded with barrels made everyone wait at a narrow point. Teren opened the door just enough to speak to the driver; the guard ahead returned to indicate where they would be able to pass.

Alexander watched the edges of the other cart's wheels. A few movements would have let the man clear the road, but a badly fastened barrel stopped him reversing as he wished. A second man climbed down, pushed the strap into the right position, and signaled. The cart moved. They passed without anyone needing to recognize who sat behind the glass.

The delay added a few minutes to a journey that would take many more. Twenty-three kilometers had room for a stop, a bend too tight, the horses' sound changing as the surface hardened. Alexander rested the book on his knee and opened Noemi's envelope.

The first question concerned the place for visits. The second asked whether people could talk without having to begin immediately. The third had been written with different pressure on the pen: what should a person who had worn a prosthesis for years tell him about herself?

Alexander reread the last sentence. It did not ask him to hide his power or pretend an old injury must be beyond reach. It asked how to enter the possibility of something she had stopped expecting. The sheet continued with other observations and the wish for a meeting without a flooded room around them.

He folded it without adding answers in the margin. He would write to Noemi after choosing how to arrange the conversation, with a real address and availability. Healing the arm remained a fact; the future of the other request was not yet a completed examination.

Teren watched him put away the envelope. “Will I need to plan another journey?”

“Perhaps. It isn't arranged.”

“Tell me when it is.”

“I will.”

The security chief went back to watching the road. He did not ask him to repeat the woman's questions. Alexander opened Celine's book and within a few lines found a passage he did not remember despite having read it before. He began from the previous paragraph and this time followed it to the end.

A jolt made him lose the line. Teren looked at the book and asked whether it was a document he had to finish before they arrived.

“Worse. My sister will want to know what I think.”

The security chief smiled without keeping his eyes from the window for long. Alexander showed him a diagram: a lever raised a load, resting on a fulcrum indicated by a triangle. Teren asked whether the ground beneath that support was hard.

“The drawing assumes it's fixed.”

“Then it chose a better road than ours.”

Alexander laughed and searched for the lost line. He knew the relationship between distances from the fulcrum; instead he returned to the legend, following how the manual made it readable to someone encountering it for the first time. Arrows distinguished weight from applied force. He showed Teren the point; Teren returned to the road without claiming to discuss the calculation. The carriage took a bend and the horses slowed on the rougher surface. Alexander held the page with one hand until the motion steadied. He thought of the questions Celine would ask looking at that diagram and kept reading even the explanations he could have summarized himself.

For a while the journey held only that: someone else's words, wheels, the sound of a bridle outside the window. Nobody waited for him to make an answer appear. Reaching the end of the page, he realized he had not looked at the road for several minutes.

The countryside offered fields, fences, distant smoke. The duke's memories could give that landscape familiarity; Edran also saw what he had not yet learned to know. Not every house was a story he owned because he recognized its roof. Behind some windows someone might be arguing about a delivery; behind others nobody knew a duke had passed.

He thought of Aurelie and the letter entrusted the evening before. It might already have arrived or still be making its ordinary way through deliveries. The road offered no news of her. He did not search passing faces as though the world must have arranged a sign for every question he had decided to ask.

Halfway along they stopped briefly for the horses. Teren got out and spoke to one guard; the other stayed where he could see the road. Alexander marked his place in the book, checked both envelopes in his pocket, and drank the water prepared for him. When the carriage moved again, the light had changed sides on the seats.

His body's strength did not need to diminish for him to feel the weight of a day. He had said goodbye to people he wanted to see again, received trust that required time, sent a question whose answer he did not know. He could hold metal without effort and still want a quiet chair without someone reading that moment as a measure of his powers.

Rovenne appeared after a little more than three and a half hours, when shadows had lengthened enough to change the pattern on the walls. The guard ahead slowed at the entrance, the driver recognized the route home. Alexander closed the book and looked at the window from which Celine had watched him leave.

Maurice waited when the carriage stopped. He did not rush to the step; he took the bag Alexander did not need to carry inside and asked whether they had met any difficulties.

“Only a cart that wouldn't move.”

Teren confirmed the journey had been uneventful, then attended to the men and horses. The two guards did not vanish as soon as the duke was safe through the door. Alexander saw them exchange a few words before one went away with the driver.

The house's air had a smell the memories recognized before he did. Maurice indicated where to leave his jacket and suggested he sit while something to drink was brought. Alexander agreed. A pale line of dust lay on his dark cuff; he looked at it and thought Celine would find the whole road in that stain too.

“Where is she?” he asked.

Maurice needed no name. “Near the library. She heard the carriage arrive.”

Alexander rose after drinking. He took the book and the letter with the gray fingerprint; he left Noemi's envelope in his own folder so the questions would not mix with family news.

Celine met him in the passage before he reached the library. She looked at the book, then at the pocket from which he was taking the letter.

“Did Aunt write back?”

Alexander offered her the envelope with the volume she had lent him. She took only the letter.
