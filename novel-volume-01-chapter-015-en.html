Chapter 15 — An Ordinary Piece

The sample arrived wrapped in a clean cloth.

Ilian set it on the bench and waited for Marcel to signal him to open it. Elias recognized the central curve from the drawing, but the actual proportions immediately explained the discrepancy.

It was another piece.

Not proof that he had imagined his own hinge incorrectly. Not an offer to repair the one he knew.

He felt disappointed before he felt cautious.

The sample had a weight the drawing could not convey. Ilian had set it down with both hands, first protecting the bench with a fold of cloth. Elias followed its curve and found the first plan's error again: a difference large enough to hide, within a resemblance, a materially different thing.

“Did you receive it like this?” Marcel asked.

“In this wrapping. I haven't taken anything apart.”

The draftsman showed where he had lifted the cloth. There was no untamperable package to display as an absolute guarantee. He had merely preserved what he could and remembered the hands that had passed over the piece before his eyes.

Elias brought a lamp closer. The metal changed color where light met the edge, and for an instant he thought he had found the property that would explain everything. It was only the cloth's reflection.

He put the lamp back. Ilian watched without comment. It could hardly be the first time someone had expected more from an object than the object had promised.

“I'm interested in comparing that part,” Elias said, indicating the joint.

“As part of this piece,” Marcel specified.

The boy nodded. The master had uttered no secret; he had prevented their interest being mistaken for an offer to show another. Elias realized how much more attention that distinction required when he was keen to be useful.

Ilian opened his notebook to find the request. A smaller sheet slipped toward the edge, and he stopped it with the back of his hand. Its fold stubbornly curled back on itself.

The sheet the draftsman had caught was his bill. Elias glimpsed an amount before the page slid beneath the request again. Ilian found the place where Marcel could begin; the young man kept looking at the smaller corner protruding from the notebook.

“Have they paid you?”

“Not all of it.”

“Does the comparison depend on the balance?”

Ilian put the sheet away.

“The balance depends on what work they recognize as finished.”

Marcel looked at the sample without touching it.

“Then let's also describe what you weren't given in order to finish it.”

Ilian nodded. He did not seem to want someone to be offended on his behalf. He seemed to want a sentence he could bring a client without losing his right to be paid.

“May I?” Marcel asked.

Ilian nodded. The written request concerned comparison and assessment, not a higher source given away for free. One note bore the name Xenia Helren, with observations about the sample's documentation. It did not certify all the properties another sheet attributed to it.

Marcel spread the two texts side by side.

“These don't say the same thing.”

“I noticed,” Ilian replied.

His relief had given way to a more concrete nervousness. He had already worked on the drawing, and someone expected a finished sheet, not new questions.

Elias showed him the enlarged copy of the lines.

“The dimension error is one thing. The shape is another.”

“That's why I wanted the piece.”

Marcel checked that the sheet identified the sample actually received. Ilian showed him the available correspondence, distinguishing his own contribution from the earlier copyist's. Being asked to redraw it had not automatically made him responsible for every line.

Elias thought of Gaston, the second set of readings, how easily one heading could be put over work done at different times.

“Can we speak to Helren?” he asked.

“I can pass on the request for a comparison.”

It was not a meeting already guaranteed. It was still more useful than a name to suspect without knowing.

Ilian set his fingers on the metal. His gaze stopped following what his eyes could see. There were no sparks or spectacular colors. When he spoke, he distinguished materials and joints with a confidence that did not come from merely looking at them.

Elias had seen demonstrations of Crucible before, but the stillness of that procedure seemed stranger than a flash of light. Ilian was not guessing at the substance. He reached it through a faculty Elias did not possess.

“That doesn't confirm an imbued passage,” Marcel said.

“No. I'm examining the components.”

Ilian withdrew his hand. The sample remained an object on the bench, opening nothing.

Marcel took the sample only after Ilian gave permission. He turned it on the cloth and held it still while Elias compared the side view. The paper's lines finally became a set of surfaces a hand could reach.

“The edge doesn't continue here,” Elias said.

Ilian brought the sheet beside the object. The break matched an area the copy had flattened. The discovery brought them no nearer a distant room or a hidden workshop; it simply explained why the drawing had lied without the copyist having decided to deceive them.

“A shadow mistaken for a boundary,” the draftsman said.

Elias remembered his irritation at the case that was too short. He had judged someone before seeing what the protruding part was for. He was tempted to do the same with this sample: attribute to it the ill intent that would make distrusting the client easier.

Marcel had him hold the sheet while he checked the opposite part with an ordinary instrument. Ilian had just completed a supernatural reading; the master still measured an edge. Neither seemed to consider the other's work useless.

“May I mark this break on my plan?” the draftsman asked.

“If you distinguish what you see now from what you received.”

Ilian took the time to mark the point with a fine line, leaving the previous stroke intact. Elias watched him choose where to place the note so it would not look like part of the object. There was a minute creativity in this work, different from the one with which he had imagined passages and connections. It consisted of giving a doubt a readable form without filling it out of embarrassment.

The draftsman lifted the sheet. Light passed through its margin, and for an instant the lines on the reverse overlapped the dimensions. He laid it back on the cloth, where it could actually be read.

The commercial request spoke of maintaining a connection and asked for comparison with a working specimen. The prospective client's name now appeared in full as the contact for further conditions: Mael Vaur.

Elias read it twice. It meant nothing to him.

“Do you know him?” he asked Marcel.

“Not well enough to hand him an object.”

Ilian sat down. The sample's edge was rough where the drawing made it smooth.

“They also asked whether I could correct this surface.”

“Can you?”

Ilian took a small scrap of material from his own supplies, separate from the specimen. He passed a finger over it. When he offered it to Elias, one area had changed texture. The boundary could be felt beneath a fingernail.

Elias ran his fingertip over the scrap, then tried his nail at the indicated point. The surface offered different resistance from one area to the next. He had seen no heat, abrasion, or tool to account for the change. The piece reached his hand already different.

He moved his finger over the boundary and felt how narrow it was. Ilian watched with the concentration of someone waiting for a client to stop looking for the trick and decide whether the service was useful.

“May I mark where I feel it?”

The draftsman nodded. Elias did not use the sample as a reference or place the scrap on it. He marked the point on a separate piece of paper, then compared it with the one Ilian had indicated.

It was a small demonstration, performed without claiming to open a passage. Perhaps that was why it seemed so convincing. A power could be real even when its possessor refused to promise the thing for which people hoped to pay him.

“One simple property. Not what they're claiming here.”

Marcel waited for Elias to return the scrap.

“And you don't want to do it to the sample before the comparison.”

“If I change it, how do I explain what it was like?”

Ilian put away the scrap he had transformed. He continued to touch the real sample only with his eyes.

“If they ask me to smooth this edge before Helren sees it, I won't,” he said.

“Have they asked?”

“They asked for a more convincing drawing. A clean edge would make it so.”

Elias looked at the irregularity. Having touched it, he knew how little it took to change the story told by paper. Ilian had the ability to erase the difference and the need to be paid. His thumb stayed still outside the wrapping.

“Write it down,” he said.

Ilian looked up.

“That I won't modify it?”

“That you don't want to transform the object before describing it.”

Marcel added that he would answer about the sample and the limits of the examination, without presenting an old piece's presence in his workshop as an offer to sell it.

Ilian nodded. He rolled the drawing more carefully than when he had arrived.

“Shall I leave the sample for comparison?”

“Only with a recorded handover and conditions for its return,” Marcel said.

Ilian spread the empty wrapping to check that no small element remained inside. Elias waited, keeping his hands away from the object. The piece required no secret preparation for safekeeping; it required someone to remember why it was there and when it must leave.

“What if Helren's answer takes longer?” Elias asked.

“We'll let the client know. I don't decide by myself to leave it with you for ever.”

Ilian checked the date in his notebook. “If I leave it, I have to return to the client without the sample.”

“With the receipt,” Marcel said.

“A receipt won't please him as much as an answer.”

Elias showed him where to record the next comparison. The man copied it in the space below the bill.

“I don't want to make things difficult for you.”

“Then let's write down properly what you received.”

The draftsman had not asked him to guess the solution to his entire life. He indicated the sample, the request's wording, and the date. Marcel added the terms of return. They reread the page long enough to remove a sentence that seemed to attest more than they had done.

When Ilian put his pencil away, Elias looked at the clear space on the bench. They had obtained an object to study, not the right to consider the person who brought it available too. The draftsman folded the cloth and put it in his bag with the care of someone who would need it again.

They filled out an ordinary receipt. No one signed that the piece was what the commercial sheet promised.

When Ilian left, Elias remained before the client's name.

For days he had searched for someone responsible who could explain a measurement that had become dangerous. Now he had a correct measurement, a man who had decided to move the bench, and, in another folder, a question asked by someone who wanted to see a working object.

He turned to Marcel.

“We aren't showing him ours.”

His mentor set the sample in its case.

“No.”

Elias felt none of the old resentment at being stopped. He looked at the motionless pin and finally wondered why that client was not satisfied with examining what he already had.
