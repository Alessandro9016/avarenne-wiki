Chapter 69 — The Part That Wears Out

The first gasket made in Lethorne had nothing miraculous about it. It was a dark ring, still damp where the cut had exposed the fiber, and it insisted on remaining oval. Elias laid it on the drawing. The man who had cut it turned it over with one finger.

“You can see the edge this way.”

The edge was not beautiful. Three spots retained marks from the shears, too small for Elias to feel without running a fingernail over them. Yselle had chosen a sheet of material available from a local supplier. If it held, they could order more parts without waiting for his return.

Pump number two waited on its base in the workshop. The white mark on its casing was almost dry, and they had set a basin beneath its feet. The machine was not connected to the Mutual's network. It would draw from the low container and send water through the test pipe, which returned to the container from the other side. Elias had followed the route with his hand before they began: none of their valves could interrupt the service provided by pump number one.

Number three remained covered a little farther away. That morning it seemed less impossible than number two, which they were about to open.

Marcel Ruy set a box for the fasteners on the bench. “Which one have you decided to sacrifice?”

“Only your good humor,” Yselle answered.

“That hasn't held water in years.”

Elias smiled without looking up. He had lined up the wrenches from smallest to largest, and now it seemed a foolish display. He moved two of them. Marcel noticed but chose to examine the ring instead. Before opening the machine, he handed Elias the workshop's safety goggles; Elias had to adjust the strap, which hung too loosely behind his head.

The maker said he had cut two from the same sheet, along with a scrap for identifying the material on the next order. Yselle asked him to leave the waste as well. He grimaced: waste was not something you paid for. She pointed to the drawing, and he understood that she wanted to preserve the shape around the cut, not buy his shop's rubbish.

Elias removed the cover with Marcel's help. The interior did not gleam as it had in his imaginings on the first evening; it bore a trace of water and the thin shadow of oil where there ought to be oil. The parts were real down to their smell. Ruy placed the fasteners in the box without counting them aloud. Elias counted them anyway.

The original gasket came out whole. The new ring was slightly stiffer and needed help settling into its seat. Elias held one side while the man who had cut it pushed the other. Their fingers covered part of the edge. Yselle asked for room to see; he moved aside, the ring seemed to settle, and Marcel brought the cover close.

Elias had prepared a sheet with three columns: assembly, test, inspection. He wrote successful in the first before he had even put down his pencil. He left the word there.

Noemi arrived while they were connecting the return pipe. She carried a folder under her arm and a small parcel wrapped in bread paper. She set it on the bench farthest from the water.

“I was told you were taking a new machine to pieces in here.”

“A malicious description,” Yselle said.

“The old one is still working at our place. Gaston threw me out because I kept watching his hands while he checked the pump in service.”

There was no old pump in service: she meant the first, already old merely because it was in its place. Elias felt an absurd pleasure at the usage. Noemi approached just close enough to examine the circuit, then sat on a stool. She did not ask about Alexander, and no one had to invent a reason to mention him.

Marcel indicated the drain valve. It was closed. Yselle checked the container, Elias the pipe, and the test began without a solemn declaration. Motion filled the workshop with a steady noise. The water rose, crossed the transparent inspection section, and returned to the tank. For a few moments Elias watched only that return.

The first drop landed on his wrist.

It might have come from the wet pipe. Then another arrived, and a dark line appeared beneath the cover. Marcel said “Enough” at the same moment Yselle reached the stop. The noise ceased; the thread of water did not. Elias shut off the test circuit's feed, keeping his hand away from the gap. The drain sent the remaining water into the basin with a much more modest sound.

No jet, no fall. Elias's heart still beat too hard. Beneath his sleeve, the memory of the wet railing mingled with his dry wrist. He glanced toward Noemi and found her with both feet on the floor, the folder on her knees.

“I like that noise better,” she said, indicating the drain.

The maker bent over the empty container. “It could be the sheet.”

“It could,” Yselle answered. “Let's open it first.”

Elias would have liked to nod along with her, but he had been the one holding the edge. He took off his glasses, wiped them with the wrong part of his sleeve, and had to start again. Marcel waited for the circuit to finish draining. He did not touch the cover until Elias showed him the level in the open section.

This time there seemed to be far more fasteners than before. He removed them without hurrying, irritated by his own desire to make them disappear. When they lifted the cover, the defect was there: a small section of the ring had folded inward. The material was not torn. It retained the flattened impression of the edge, like a bitten lip.

Elias pointed. “Here.”

The man reached for the part, but Yselle raised a hand. “First I want to see it where it is.”

The seat had accepted it badly, or they had put it in badly. With Near Coordinates, Elias reached the geometry of the exposed edge, the proximity between the bottom of the groove and the closing surface. Its arrangement became distinct in his perception, a relationship of distances made difficult to see by the reflection on the oil. It did not tell him how the material had folded. They could already see that.

He took the calipers and compared the accessible points. The dimensions agreed with those recorded. The groove had not narrowed. The maker breathed out; Elias felt that small acquittal and envied it.

“I let go when it looked as though it was in,” he said.

“So did I,” the man answered.

Marcel turned the cover over. On its underside, a trace of oil broke at the same spot. “It went in crooked, and we closed it.”

He did not say who had tightened it last. Elias remembered the wrench in his own hand. He would have preferred Marcel to say it, perhaps so he could answer something. Instead he had to erase successful and write leaks: folded edge. The pencil eraser spread a gray shadow across the first column.

Noemi opened the parcel: four pieces of bread with cheese. “Have one before you start blaming breakfast.”

The maker laughed with too much relief. Yselle took the bread and asked whether he could obtain a more regular cut by using a support rather than holding the sheet in his hand. The man answered at once, then slowed, thinking about his own bench. He could make a template. The first order would not pay for it by itself.

“I'm not ordering a hundred rings,” she said. “Tell me what it costs to make two we can fit.”

“These can be fitted.”

“Then let's fit the second.”

Elias ate standing up. His shame was beginning to retreat, leaving the more useful irritation of something that needed doing again. He washed his hands before touching the other ring. The folded one remained on the paper without becoming evidence that the material had failed.

They laid the second part on the clean surface. The maker trimmed a small ridge with a sharp tool, well away from the open machine. Marcel checked that no fibers remained in the seat. This time Elias guided the ring around its entire circumference and released it only after the other man had removed his fingers. Yselle looked from one side, then asked them to turn the lamp.

The cover came down slowly. To see the opposite edge, Elias had to kneel. The stone pressed through his trousers; that tiny discomfort had the virtue of restoring his whole body to him. When he stood again, he had found no hidden fold. He wrote nothing in the first column yet.

They tightened the fasteners evenly, without trying to compensate for the earlier leak with extra force. Marcel made a mark on the sheet when they were ready. Water returned to the circuit. Elias ran a dry cloth beneath the joint before the test, then laid it white on the bench where everyone could see whether it changed.

The machine started again. The first circuit of water raised a bubble in the return, the second carried it away. Elias waited for the drop on his wrist. It did not come. He bent without placing his fingers on the joint, looked from the basin side, and moved to make room for the other man. He found no water either.

Yselle prolonged the test. The noise that at first had seemed an answer became a noise again: they could talk without lowering it at every sentence. Noemi finished her bread and took a page of notes from the folder.

“When this part has to be changed at our place, what do we need to have?”

“The replacement,” Elias answered, and felt an idiot before she showed him the list.

“I'd guessed that much. I need the right name to order it by, who sells it, and the sheet that has to stay near the machine. I'd rather not ask for a gasket and get twenty different answers.”

Yselle handed her the scrap the maker had saved. He added his commercial description to the sheet, correcting one letter. Noemi asked whether he could recognize the material without seeing the original part. The man assured her he could, then asked to keep a sample. They divided the scrap.

Elias copied the seat and the sides of the ring onto his drawing. He did not write an explanation of every pump in the world. He drew the one they had opened, with the space needed to reach the fasteners. Noemi made him move the key: a dirty hand would land over the corner he had chosen. The erasure obscured part of the first line, and the drawing stopped looking ready for an exhibition.

When they stopped the machine, the joint was dry. They drained the circuit again and inspected the second installation. The ring remained seated, without the distortion of the first. Yselle accepted the outcome of that test; the material's long-term performance would only be answered after more work. Pump one at the Mutual had not lost a minute of service to give them that answer.

Elias wrote successful in the second row. This time the word seemed the right size. Beneath it he added the maker's name and the reference to the retained sheet. He returned the pencil without trying to clean away the gray stain from the first test.

Noemi gathered the documents and the last piece of bread. “I'll take this to Gaston. Otherwise he'll think I only came for lunch.”

“You also came to watch our hands,” Marcel said.

“Yours needed company.”

Elias laughed. As he accompanied her to the door, he heard an argument begin about the price of the template, concrete and unmusical. He turned to look at the open pump. Morning light crossed the high window and fell on the casing without distinguishing what had been created from what had been cut in a shop. On the bench, the two rings no longer looked enough alike to confuse. He picked up the folded one and placed it in the sample box with his sheet.
