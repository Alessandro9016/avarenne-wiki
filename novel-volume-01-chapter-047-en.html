Chapter 47 — Twenty-Three Kilometers

Celine slipped the letter into the wrong pocket of his coat and immediately accused him of losing it.

Alexander took it out. The envelope was smaller than Yselle's invitation, sealed with a care his sister had not devoted to handing it over. Their aunt's name ran diagonally across the front to avoid a slight crease.

“Here,” he said, putting it in the opposite inner pocket.

“That's where you put important papers.”

“Exactly.”

She watched him long enough to decide whether he was teasing. Then she turned toward Maurice, who waited at the door with something somebody else had forgotten. The duke was not the only person leaving or running late that morning.

“I'll be back after my lesson,” Celine said.

Alexander nodded. He wanted to ask whether there was an answer hidden in the name on the envelope, but had no wish to begin the journey by demanding an explanation of a letter that was not his. Teren signaled from the carriage. Two guards had already taken their places according to the head of security's arrangements; nobody looked as though they were accompanying a man unable to endure twenty-three kilometers of road.

Maurice reminded him where messages would reach them in Lethorne. Edran recognized the agreed address, confirmed it, and climbed in. The step sank beneath his weight. The duke's body held complete memories of that movement, yet the present sensation did not feel like a memory correctly performed. The spring yielded, the seat leather held the warmth of a hand that had rested there, and he was there.

The carriage set off on the morning of June fifth.

They crossed a street still occupied by deliveries. Teren had requested a route that did not need clearing before their arrival. A porter watched them wait while he finished carrying a package into a shop, then backed his handcart just far enough. The driver thanked him. Alexander saw the man return to the second package without seeking his eyes.

Rovenne spread beyond the window in pale walls, courtyards, and corners where washing water had not dried. Two children disputed a board resting on a wheel; a woman seized its end and made both children pass beneath her arm so she could reach the door. The smaller protested so loudly that Edran heard him despite the wheels.

Teren turned his head a little.

“An inheritance dispute,” Alexander said.

“At least they know what they're fighting over.”

The reply made him look up. The head of security had resumed watching ahead. Edran wondered how long the man had been making jokes Alexander's memories had not considered worth keeping.

Beyond the last houses, cart traffic had scored the ground. The road rose a little, then revealed a more distant line of roofs. The air carried smoke, crushed grass, and briefly the stink of a ditch nobody had hidden for their passage. Alexander shifted on the seat. The carriage made him feel the road through his back.

He had wanted to go out. Now that the house no longer filled his view, he realized he had chiefly imagined leaving as the absence of his own corridors. Here instead were people and tasks that had not been assigned to distract him. The field to the left ended in an incomplete fence; a man held up part of it while someone else brought an uncooperative animal underneath.

The sound that reached the window was laughter. He could not tell who had begun.

Ancient memory offered another road: dark earth, an iron ring around the ankle of the person walking ahead, a sound every step repeated without ever completing. Edran felt his hand close on the seat's edge. He did not try to make the two images alike. The man at the fence released the support, chased the animal a few steps, and called it a ridiculous name. Nobody dragged him away for laughing.

Alexander lowered his eyes to his glove. The seam ran between thumb and forefinger; it took a moment to stop pulling it tight.

“Shall we stop?” Teren asked.

“Not yet.”

The head of security nodded without assigning an explanation to the silence. Edran was grateful for the way he returned to his work. He had agreed to travel in company, and that company did not require turning every movement of his hand into an announcement.

They reached the bridge behind a loaded cart. A wheel had met a stone at the entrance, and the driver was trying to find the angle that would free it without shifting the load. The carriage stopped. At Teren's signal, one guard got down and went to speak with the man.

Alexander watched the water beneath the planks. It was the color of things carried downstream, not the sky. A branch turned near the pier and returned to the same little eddy. The cart driver pulled the reins, then slackened them. Someone on the opposite side made a gesture too broad to be mere advice.

“If you keep pushing there, you'll put it back in the rut,” Teren said quietly.

He spoke quietly enough that the man at the cart could not hear him. Alexander followed the wheel, the stone, and the available space, and saw the problem an instant before the driver changed his grip too.

The cart edged sideways. The guard returned, dusted his palm, and said the way was clear. Their journey resumed after a wait long enough for Alexander to reread the name on the envelope.

“You said three or four hours,” he observed.

“Yes.”

“I'm beginning to understand the breadth of the estimate.”

“The cart hadn't written to announce itself.”

Edran laughed. This time Teren did not hide his smile.

Along the next stretch, factories appeared first as smoke and then as walls. Movement came through high windows, too distant to distinguish; outside, a group of men sat with food wrapped in cloth. One had taken off a shoe and was massaging his foot. Another spoke, using bread to point at something beyond the roof.

Alexander's memories knew owners' names. Edran watched the men's hands, the shoe on the ground, the way one set aside part of his food. He could not derive what they earned, what they owed, or what they would say on seeing his face. The freedom he had recognized in the gesture at the fence did not answer every question of the world that followed. But it gave him something he wanted to keep looking at.

Farther on, repairs had narrowed a stretch of road. The driver brought the wheels slowly along the side still open. Alexander saw a young man bent over an empty box, trying to retrieve a nail fallen between the stones. He indicated where the metal had caught the light. The young man looked up, followed the gesture, and found the nail. He raised it to show Alexander, then stepped aside with his box.

The carriage gathered speed before Alexander could say anything else. A pale imprint from the tight glove remained on the hand that had pointed. Edran watched it while the young man returned to work behind them. He would remember the gesture without even knowing the name of the person it had helped.

The carriage passed a woman standing with a basket against her hip. She turned because of the dust, covered her face with a handkerchief, and resumed talking to a girl seated on the low wall. Alexander waited fruitlessly for the two figures to offer a greater meaning. They remained two people engaged in a conversation he had not heard.

He leaned back.

When Lethorne's first houses appeared, the light had changed its angle and the road had used up its morning. The time had not been empty. He felt he had passed through more gestures than he could bring home without betraying them in a sentence.

The driver slowed in a narrow street. A wheelbarrow with a shrieking axle emerged from a courtyard. Teren sent word to the house without dispatching a guard to impose silence on the workshop. Their accommodation had been agreed; the rest of the stay did not need to become a procession before Alexander stepped down.

Yselle stood in the doorway when the carriage stopped.

The duke recognized her before she offered a word: the way she put her weight on one leg, the escaping lock of hair, the gaze that went from his face to his hand on the door. Memory held affection together with impatience; now both had a body moving toward his.

“You really came.”

“The road tried to discourage me.”

Yselle took his arm, looked at him closely, and slipped a hand behind his shoulder to embrace him. Edran smelled soap and some remnant of workshop work on her sleeve. She was shorter than her voice suggested. She did not restrain her embrace because a duke occupied the coat.

“You've recovered,” she said as she released him.

“People often point that out.”

“You must have looked dreadful before.”

Martin appeared behind her with a pale stain on his cuff. He greeted Alexander, hesitated for a breath, and offered his hand. His grip had the care of someone who remembered a recent illness and did not yet know how much of it he could forget.

“The journey?”

“It taught me how springs work.”

“If you've understood why that carriage transmits every pothole, I have a problem to show you.”

Yselle tapped his arm. “Let him come in first.”

Alexander produced Celine's letter before his coat changed hands. His aunt looked at the name on the envelope and smiled differently. He liked the thought that he had only been entrusted with carrying it more than he had expected.

“She told me not to lose it among the important things.”

“She's wise enough to know what a man would lose.”

Teren spoke to the person responsible for the carriage; the two guards went to the place where they could wait without obstructing the passage. Alexander followed Yselle inside. The house and the work met in a smell of bread, water, and metal he could not entirely separate.

Food lay on the table beside a stack of papers kept away from the plates. Martin moved a book to make room. Yselle opened the letter only after showing Alexander where to sit; she read it standing, smiled at one line, and folded it without explaining.

They ate something before the visit. Martin told them about a pupil who had believed he could lighten a machine by changing the scale of its drawing. Alexander laughed and asked whether the error had lasted long enough to save transporting it. Yselle's husband began answering seriously, then stopped and laughed too.

His aunt set down her cup and looked at him. “People are coming to show you the work. You needn't stay here listening to us until evening.”

“That was part of the invitation too.”

“What I left out of the invitation was the amount of paper.”

A blow against metal sounded beyond the door, followed by two voices. Yselle rose. Alexander followed her to the workshop entrance. Beneath the windows he saw the base, the prototype's body, and a bench where several hands had already prepared something to examine.

His aunt pointed out a hook for his coat. He hung it without covering the drawing fixed to the wall. Then he turned toward the voices, ready to meet whoever was arriving.
