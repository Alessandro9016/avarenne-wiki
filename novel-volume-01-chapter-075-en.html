Chapter 75 — The Stolen Testimony

The mark was hers. Livia recognised it before reading the sentence it was supposed to authenticate, which was why it took her a few seconds too long to understand what was wrong.

Edith had brought the sheet from the entrance desk. A delivery boy had handed it over, asking for confirmation of an appointment to inspect a collar. He was waiting outside on the step and had made her promise a short answer.

Beneath Elias's name were the words availability confirmed. The date was that same tenth of June. Neither a fee nor a full address was given; one line said an escort would arrange access.

“Have you told them something without me?” Livia asked.

Edith looked at her. “No.”

She did not take offence. She waited for Livia to stop reading her own name as though it belonged to a stranger.

The copyist ran a finger beside the attestation. The invested relationship answered her touch: she had seen two versions of a request on the fourth of June, with Ilian Uren sitting opposite her. That precedent was true. She had not seen Elias agree to a visit. She had not promised one on his behalf.

The mark had not been persuaded to lie. Someone had made it appear to support a sentence it did not answer.

“Is it authentic?” Edith asked.

“It's mine. It doesn't authenticate this.”

Livia held the sheet towards the window. Light shone through a darker band around the mark. A clipping had been pasted onto another sheet; its upper edge ran beneath the words witness to comparison. On the new page, those words looked like the beginning of a commercial confirmation.

They had preserved what worked and changed what a reader would think it meant. Anyone recognising only the pull of Archive might have found that sufficient.

With Held Moment, Livia recalled the passage she had recorded while reading Ilian's request. His voice, the light on the table, the line she had refused to interpret as a commission. The invested memory remained sharp while ordinary thought searched for an excuse: perhaps a bad copy, perhaps someone had misunderstood.

Availability confirmed. The tenth. Her name beneath. This was not a lost comma.

“Who sent this?” she asked the delivery boy.

He pointed at his bag. “I don't know, ma'am. They gave it to me at the delivery counter.”

“A man or a woman?”

“My employer took the parcel. I do the round.”

He showed her the address strip. The reply was to go to a business box, not a home. There was no name that could turn the boy into an accomplice. Livia copied its number, told him she would return an answer to the counter and let him resume his round. He seemed chiefly disappointed that he could not take an envelope away immediately.

Edith closed the door. “We have to tell Elias.”

“First I want someone else to see how it arrived.”

She called the supervisor of the copying room. She showed him the pasted edge without detaching it and had him read the sentence. He knew her handwriting; he did not know the faculty. Livia had to explain the distinction and saw the point at which his professional patience became unease.

“Can you identify who did it?”

“No.”

“Not even by touching it?”

“I can look for traces of how it was worked. Not a name that wasn't left.”

She placed two fingers on the back and allowed Residual Sense to surface. Beneath the glue lay pressure and dragging, repeated passes along the same edge. More recently, a compressed impression left by a flat object. Not a vision of hands, not a room reopening before her. The paper remained paper, carrying its small injuries.

Livia stopped before confusing fatigue with discovery. She noted what she could actually report and added that it did not identify the maker. The supervisor wanted a copy to keep. While she prepared it, Edith picked up her coat.

“I'm going to the workshop.”

“Ilian first,” Livia said. “His address is on the old request. If he sent something by mistake, he needs to stop the rest.”

“And if it isn't a mistake?”

Livia looked at the clock. “Then he'd better know before the escort arrives.”

Ilian worked in a room overlooking a yard with a single exit. When they found him, he was securing a batten over a long table. He did not seem pleased to receive two visitors at once. He looked at the paper Livia held flat and put down his hammer.

“I didn't write that.”

He said it before greeting them. Then he moved so close to the sheet that his shadow covered the signature.

“Who brought it?”

Livia described the delivery. Ilian searched his folder for his copy of Elias's questions from the ninth. The location remained blank. The terms remained unsettled. He placed the pages together and pressed his palm against the table's edge.

“Yesterday I told him I'd arranged nothing.”

“Vaur?”

“Yes.”

Edith looked towards the yard door. Ilian noticed and took his hand off the table.

“I haven't brought you into a trap. This is where I work.”

“I know,” she replied. “I'd like it to stay that way.”

Livia showed him where her attestation had been cut out. Ilian recognised the old sheet: he had left it at the depot with the comparison, as a document to be returned. He had given it to nobody else. That narrowed its possible route; it did not make everyone at the depot responsible for the alteration.

Ilian seemed uninterested in that caution. He seized his hat. “I'm going to ask for an explanation.”

Edith stood in front of the door without touching him. “Martin was injured yesterday. A man took his papers about the pumps.”

Livia turned sharply towards her. Edith produced a note from Yselle, delivered to her contact address that morning because Yselle knew she would see her friends. The news took only a few lines: theft, cut hand, report made. Below was the name Uren, copied from a label found in the classroom. Yselle asked them to warn the draughtsman and have him speak to the guard.

“I was about to tell you when the other envelope arrived,” Edith said.

Ilian read to the end. His hat remained in his hand. For the first time since they entered, he looked at the yard as though its single exit were no convenience.

“My name?”

Edith nodded. She did not add that it probably meant one thing or another. The news hurt enough without an invented explanation.

Ilian returned to the table. He moved the batten, pulled accounts from beneath a case and began searching too quickly. Livia saw the tip of the pencil he held between his fingers tremble; she waited for him to offer her the sheet.

“This is yesterday's signed request. This is the old invoice. Nothing else exists.”

“Let's make copies,” she said.

Ilian opened his mouth, then closed it. He sat. Edith fetched clean paper; neither woman promised to recover his balance. While Livia copied, he described the depot entrance, who had received his first drawings and which payments had failed to arrive. He knew the small facts, not the whole organisation.

He hesitated when she asked for the address. Livia stopped writing. Something harder than shame crossed his face: the conviction that he was destroying his last client who still owed him money. If the guard closed the depot, his account would remain inside with the others. He said so without asking the women to agree.

Edith asked how much he had already spent on the work. Ilian named the materials and the time, then began again with the previous month's costs. He stopped halfway through. The sum would not make Elias's purported agreement true.

He dictated the address. Livia copied it without asking him to repeat himself. He insisted on checking it, moved the entrance directions down a line and returned the sheet.

His anger returned when he reached Elias's name. “If they wanted another technician, why write him in particular?”

Livia did not say Hinge. Ilian knew a request concerning a device and an earlier sample, but not where they kept their own object. Nothing on the altered page proved anyone else knew either.

“That's what I want to understand.”

Ilian looked at his receipt. “I thought it was meant to get me paid.”

Nobody answered quickly enough to make it hurt less.

They took the paper and the copies to the guard post. Ilian insisted on handing them over himself. Livia gave each statement its limit: the authentic mark, the altered text, the sheet's known route. The guard noted the connection to the school report and sent a man to check the business address. She did not declare Mael Vaur guilty before them.

Ilian asked whether they could immediately recover the drawings he had left at the depot. The guard asked whether they were the same ones of which he had just supplied copies. He nodded and pointed to the signature, then to the unpaid debt. Livia understood that, for him, those two lines still held the possibility of getting paid.

The guard wrote a request to check the documents and entrusted it to a colleague with the depot address. First they would establish that the address matched the recipient; the copies would remain on file even if an original could not be found. Ilian wanted to know whether he could keep his invoice. The guard returned it after recording its particulars. He folded it with a care Livia had not seen him use for any other sheet.

She asked where Ilian could be found that afternoon. He gave his workroom and said he would close early to call on Yselle. The guard advised him against going to argue at the depot alone and gave him the statement number. Ilian repeated it twice before putting the receipt away.

Outside, a damp wind bent the paper against its case. Livia had retained the composite sheet, leaving the guard a copy and the address at which to request the original when a specialist examination was needed. She briefly invested Held Surface in the pasted margin, shielding it from a cart's spray. She only needed to bring it dry to Elias.

They found him at the workshop, back from the Mutual. He had an unnumbered medical request and the expression of someone who had spoken too soon to too many people. Livia set the other request before him, the one assigning him a decision he had never made.

Elias read his name. “I wrote that the details were missing.”

“We know.”

“I didn't agree.”

“You're not the one who has to convince us,” Edith said.

Livia watched his hand resting still on the page and felt something change. Until then, they had believed they could choose what to promise. Someone had discovered they could promise the people themselves instead.

They agreed to warn Ruy and Yselle, to send nothing to the box without keeping a copy of the denial, and to meet that evening using the ordinary entrance. Livia would not take the suspect document into Room Six. She left it with Yselle in a case, recording the handover. Their address appeared on none of the papers for that work, and it had to stay that way.

Before they parted, Edith returned Yselle's note to her. On its back she had written the report number. The handwriting was not elegant. Livia kept it in her notebook beside the memory of the fourth of June, and this time found nothing to correct.
