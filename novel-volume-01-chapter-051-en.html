Chapter 51 — The Hand on the Drawing

Yselle kept passing the second pump on her way to the same cupboard.

The first two times she had looked for something: a clean cloth, then the folder of estimates. On the third she stopped with her hand already raised toward the door and looked at the sheet covering the machine. The fabric described an angle where there had been empty space. She lifted a corner and touched the metal.

Cold. Solid. Beneath her fingertip, a worked surface caught the light like the other worked surfaces in her shop. It was June fifth, three days remained until the deadline, and the thing that had kept her from sleeping stood beneath an ordinary cloth.

Martin found her there.

“Have you forgotten what you were looking for?”

“The folder.”

He showed it to her. He held it beneath his arm with the smaller drawing. “You put it on the table and asked me not to let you lose it.”

Yselle let the sheet fall. She stepped back to see the whole row. The first machine was ready to load the next day; the second and third had numbers she would recognize even beneath a strip of fabric. A little farther away, the prototype still bore the marks of the work that had led them to the correct configuration.

“How long have we been here since the others left?” she asked.

“Long enough to need a lamp if we want to read.”

She had ignored the changing light. Martin took the lamp from the shelf and carried it to the table. Yselle followed with the folder. From the courtyard came Teren's voice speaking with a guard; Alexander had left the workshop for his lodgings, close enough to return for their agreed evening. The pumps had received no news of his departure.

The engineer opened the estimate. The first line she sought concerned the missing liners. She placed the point over the total but stopped before drawing a stroke.

“What do I cross out?” she said.

Martin sat opposite. “What you no longer have to buy.”

“Yes. But knowing I no longer have to buy it isn't enough.”

Her husband passed her the order book. Yselle's fingers found a page she had opened often enough to wear its edge. The intended material, the machining, a delivery promised but not received. They had to distinguish what remained payable from what would no longer be requested. The created metal did not retroactively cancel work someone else had already performed.

She prepared a separate sheet. She did not touch the old total until she had found the corresponding entry in every document. Then she removed from the new requirements the components already in the workshop, leaving the earlier figures legible in the preserved estimate.

The red line changed position.

For weeks that line had added up things the project knew how to ask for and her time could not obtain. Now the same words described goods present. Yselle lifted the point from the paper and had to lower it again because her hand had ceased to find a useful pressure.

“Are you dizzy?” Martin asked.

“No.”

“Would you like to sit more comfortably?”

“I'm sitting.”

He looked at the chair, of which she occupied barely the edge. Yselle moved back until she felt the backrest. Laughter rose in her throat and stopped before emerging; it had the unpleasant taste of something that might become a sob without asking permission.

Martin put a cup of water beside her.

“I don't know what to do with this evening,” she said.

Her husband did not look at the list. “I can see that.”

“I had prepared what I'd say if we still lacked the parts. I had prepared how to defend the work already done. Now I keep starting there.”

Martin turned the smaller drawing. The wrench mark beside the locking ring remained visible the right way up. “This hasn't vanished. You have another possibility, even if nobody uses it tomorrow.”

Yselle laid her hand on the drawing. She knew every correction. Her nephew had given her the machines, but he had not erased the afternoon when Elias found the narrowing, or her husband's stubbornness in front of the catch. The three pumps had that form because they had reached that form.

“I don't need capital to buy what I possess,” she said.

“And you don't have to say everything else is free.”

She nodded. Transport remained, installation, tests on site, the people who would work, and the checks that would allow them to declare the service complete. The estimate could not remain inflated merely because nobody yet knew how to describe in one line the gift that had changed it.

She began a second column. Martin counted the work already performed with her, then let her find the point where remaining requirements separated from the previous exposure. It was less. Much less at the decisive point, though she did not yet want to pronounce a figure before discussing it with those who had to recognize it.

The security over her shares remained written in the contract. Yselle reread it without trying to negotiate with the sheet that evening. The change lay in the facts she could bring to the table, not a stamp appearing by itself.

“We test it at the Mutual tomorrow,” Martin said.

“Yes.”

“And tonight?”

Yselle took writing paper. “Tonight I tell them the requirements have changed.”

She wrote that they now had three complete machines and the specified spares, that the first had passed workshop tests under the recorded conditions, and that the intended installation would allow checking on site. She requested a review of the financial requirements on the basis of the new statement, keeping expenses already incurred distinct. She did not write that the security had been released. That was why she was preparing the communication, not news she had received.

In the first draft she had left a sentence about the miraculous availability of the machines. She reread it, put down the pen, and sought the test sheet. The addressee's name at the top reminded her of a room where she would have to answer questions less easy than her own relief.

She took Marcel's note and replaced the sentence with a reference to the conditions observed. The machines' presence did not require an adjective capable of making them appear. It required that the recipient know where to find them and who had checked the first one's operation. She added the workshop address and left the test sheet beside the attachment. Then she placed the discarded draft beneath the folder, where she would not send it by mistake.

Martin read the page when she passed it to him. He rested a finger over the phrase describing the machines' availability.

“Do you want to say how?”

The question made her look at the door. “First I want to ask him.”

Alexander's voice came from the passage a little later, preceded by a greeting to whoever had opened. Yselle gathered the two pages, then left them on the table instead of holding them against her chest. Her nephew entered with his coat over one arm. The small dust mark he had not quite managed to remove still marked his knee.

He looked at the lamp, the papers, the covered row. “I left you working.”

“You brought us a great deal of new work,” Martin said.

Alexander stopped. Yselle heard how different the words could sound from her husband's intention and intervened before they became a misunderstanding.

“Good work. But I need to redo an estimate, and I can't do that merely by admiring how lovely it is not to search for more liners.”

Her nephew nodded and put his coat on the same hook. “Do you need me to describe what I've given?”

“That too. But first I want to understand who's answering me.”

Martin remained seated. Alexander did not seem surprised by the question, and that struck her more than an attempt to evade it would have. The boy had grown up with an important name and people who had to choose their words carefully before him; Yselle had known his whims and his courtesies. This man was young enough to retain the same face and different enough not to hide from her that he knew it.

“What do you want to know?”

“Where did what we saw come from?”

Alexander looked at the drawing's edge beneath her hand. “It's Cradle. I can create matter, already in the configuration I understand.”

The word gave Yselle a reference and left much of the distance intact. She had known Bearers, heard descriptions, and seen things a workshop could not obtain with a good lever. But three complete pumps had taken the place of absences in a fraction of an afternoon.

“And do you always understand it?”

“No. I understood your machine because you showed it to me. You showed me the materials, the passages, what needed to remain accessible.”

Martin shifted on his chair. “If the drawing had been wrong?”

“I would have had a faulty design to reproduce. That's why I asked questions.”

Yselle looked at her thumb on the sheet. The metal had arrived whole. The knowledge she had put into the machine had not been unnecessary to him. She breathed out and discovered she had held part of that breath since the first pump appeared.

“How long?” she asked.

Alexander remained silent long enough for her to understand that the answer would not be a simple date. When he spoke, his voice had not become ceremonial.

“I can't tell you everything tonight. I don't want to give you a short explanation I'll have to correct later.”

Yselle felt irritation and fear touch. She could insist. She had kept information from Martin to make her own day easier; she had no wish to receive a comfortable version from her nephew now. But the face before her was not asking her to consider the question closed.

“Then don't call it a complete explanation,” she said.

“It isn't.”

Martin looked at Alexander. “Are you still the one who tried to convince me a longer lever would make the weight disappear?”

Her nephew smiled. “I remember arguing that the problem was the floor.”

Yselle remembered too. The boy had possessed an exasperating certainty and a piece of wood much longer than his patience. The shared memory did not answer everything she lacked; it let her see Alexander's hand relax on the edge of the chair.

“We can test their use before we know how to tell your whole life,” she said. “But if a machine needs something from you, you tell me.”

“These don't. The machines are yours. Stable materials, ordinary mechanisms. You can repair them with components made by anyone capable of making them.”

Yselle drew the communication for the financier toward him. Alexander read it, asked where to specify the offer, and wrote a confirmation of the goods given without any demand for shares or control of the project. She had him leave enough space for an addition about the technical inspections. The duke did not hesitate when Marcel was named as the contact for the checks performed.

The letter left that evening with the revised statement and the attached confirmation. Yselle followed its bearer to the threshold, then returned to the workshop. Martin had put the smaller drawing back in the folder. Alexander was studying the number three through a fold in the sheet, as though the chalk tail were a detail he had not expected.

She extinguished one of the two lamps still burning and beckoned them both outside. Before closing, she looked at the first pump ready for transport.

Tomorrow they would take it where the water was needed. At last it was a project for which she could prepare the morning, instead of preparing only what she would say if it failed.
