Chapter 57 — Glue on the Cuff

The message had reached home before Elias, but it had said nothing about the noise his shoes made.

On the first flight he tried climbing on the edges of his feet. On the second he gave up. Water trapped beneath the insoles came out at every step with a small sucking sound; anyone hearing him from the landing would have thought him a man too tired to conceal that he had been foolish.

Adrien opened the door before he knocked.

He looked at the jacket, the pants dark above the knee, the shoes. Then he took Elias's bag and set it inside, beyond the table where he had spread a newspaper.

“There.”

Elias stepped onto the newspaper. The ink darkened beneath his heels.

“Did someone tell you?”

“That something had broken and you weren't hurt.”

“It's true.”

“Take off your shoes.”

That was not the sentence Elias had prepared on the way home. He had imagined explaining the valve, the joint, the moment the key had been too far away. Instead, sitting on the chair by the entrance, he pulled a water-swollen lace and managed to tighten the knot.

Adrien crouched and loosened it with a blunt point he carried in his pocket. Elias let him. His father's hands were suited to that sort of merciful humiliation.

Lise appeared with two cloths. She tossed one to her brother; the other went beneath his first shoe.

“We asked you to take us to see a pump,” she said. “Not bring it here.”

Elias laughed, and it pulled at a part of his chest he had kept rigid without knowing.

“The pump is dry.”

“Of course. You've got all the water.”

His foot came free of the shoe with difficulty. The sock was cold and the skin beneath it pale. Nothing hurt. He checked once again, annoyed by the need.

“Go and change,” Adrien said.

Lise handed him the second cloth as he passed. She did not let go immediately.

“Is the bag dry?”

“Inside, yes. I think.”

“Were there papers?”

“The drawings stayed at the workshop. I'd left the notebook outside.”

She nodded. It was a question about the notebook. About that too.

In his room Elias took off his shirt. There was a pale, hardened stain on the cuff, older than the water. Glue: that morning he had fastened a paper tab to the folded drawing and rested his elbow on it. He rubbed the stain with his thumb. It did not come off.

The absurdity of that remnant made him close his eyes. The glue came from before. When he turned to look for the key, he had stopped thinking about the morning, what he had eaten, or the steps it had taken to reach the Mutual. Now the fabric gave it all back at once, down to Ruy's voice scolding him for putting his elbow on a drawing he had just tidied.

He dressed quickly. He did not want anyone knocking to check whether he had sat down on the bed and been unable to get up again.

Dinner waited on the cleared table. Adrien had moved his work to the shelf, and that unsettled Elias more than a sharp question would have. Lise put his plate in front of him and told him to eat while it was hot.

“It isn't soup,” she added.

“I noticed.”

“In that state, you might have got confused.”

Elias broke the bread. He was hungry, with a concrete, almost unpleasant hunger. For a few mouthfuls nobody spoke about the water. Lise described a man at the market who insisted on paying less for some fabric because it was the wrong color; Adrien asked what color he wanted, and she said he wanted that one, but cheaper.

Elias laughed again. This time he managed to finish the mouthful without stopping.

It was his father who asked what had happened. He had not picked up his bread yet. Both hands rested beside his plate.

“An old pipe. Not the pump.”

“That's what the message said.”

Elias put down his knife. He began with the machine's arrival and the connection. He tried to be precise without recounting the entire morning; his father asked a question about the circuit, then another about the location of the shutoff. Elias showed him the positions with the cup and breadbasket. Lise moved her own glass before it became another part of the installation.

“Yselle had stopped the pump. But water was returning from the old section. The joint gave way before we could shut that off too.”

“And where were you?”

He indicated a spot between the cup and the edge of the table.

Adrien looked at that empty space. “That's where the jet hit.”

“Yes. The duke created a shield. Curved, with an opening toward the drain. Noemi had to reach the valve.”

“And you?”

“I helped her stay on her feet.”

Lise had stopped eating. Elias realized he was describing everything in the voice he used to explain a successful assembly. He changed it, and for an instant could not find another.

“The key was on the work surface. Close, but with the water in between. I brought it into my palm. I gave it to her and she shut the valve.”

Adrien nodded slowly. He knew enough of his repertoire not to ask him for a different marvel. “You transferred it from somewhere within your reach.”

“Yes.”

“While you were holding her up.”

“With my other hand.”

His father looked at him. Elias understood too late that the clarification did not help.

Lise lowered her spoon. “Were you frightened?”

Elias thought of the noise behind the metal, the railing he had reached, Noemi's body that had to remain in a precise position. He did not remember telling himself he was afraid.

“I am a little now.”

His sister watched him another moment, then picked up her bread. Adrien pushed his chair back and rubbed a hand over his mouth.

“You could ask Ruy to keep you on measurements in the workshop.”

Elias felt the food stop in his throat. “That's not all I do.”

“I know.”

“Then you know I can't decide to measure only where everything's already easy.”

“I know a failure can leave a girl and a father waiting for a message.”

The sentence fell quietly. Elias looked at Lise; she did not come to his aid, but neither did she nod in support of their father. She held her plate with one hand, thumb on its rim, as if waiting for the point at which she would have to speak for herself.

“I didn't go in there to be a hero,” Elias said.

“That wouldn't have made you any harder to kill.”

He wanted to answer that nobody had died. He looked at the cup in the middle of the table and felt ashamed of the sentence before using it. His father saw it coming too; he did not need to hear it.

Lise picked up the basket and put it back where it belonged.

“I don't want him to stop working,” she said. “I want him not to tell me the worst part as though he's explaining why he's late.”

Elias opened his mouth and closed it again.

“Before holding her up, I'd reached the railing,” he said at last. “I knew where it was. I thought if I lost that reference I wouldn't be able to hold her too. When the key arrived, I realized I hadn't been breathing.”

The silence changed. It did not become easier, but his father drew his chair back toward the table.

“Noemi got cut,” Elias continued. “The duke healed her. He closed the wound in front of us.”

Lise raised her eyes, and this time wonder did not erase fear but settled beside it. “Really?”

Elias nodded. He described the skin, the movement of the hand, the blood left on the cloth when the cut it had come from was gone. He did not know how to name that precision without reaching for the name of a power he did not possess. He said what he had seen.

Adrien listened to the end. “The pipe?”

“Repaired. New section, new supports. The test is tomorrow.”

“And you're going.”

It was not a question. Elias said yes anyway.

His father nodded once. “Send the message first. When you know you'll be late.”

“We sent it as soon as we could.”

“I know. I'm telling you for the next time it isn't a breakdown.”

Elias accepted that part. He did not promise nothing would ever happen again; his father did not ask him to. His food had grown lukewarm, and he finished it anyway.

Later Lise brought his shirt while he opened the bag to check its bottom. The papers left inside were dry. He set out only what needed air, leaving the space for her papers clear.

“This is glue,” Lise said, indicating the cuff.

“I know.”

“If you rub it like that, you'll tear the fabric before you get it off.”

She took his sleeve and began working the edge of the stain with a little water. Elias let her teach him something he would have no reason to remember in a technical report.

“You haven't asked whether I can see the machines yet,” she said.

“Not today.”

“I thought so.”

“I'll ask when the work settles down. I'm not taking you into tomorrow's test.”

Lise looked up. “You can ask when people can visit without already deciding I wouldn't know how to stay out of the way.”

She was right. Elias realized it with an irritation he was glad to recognize before speaking. “I'll ask what visits they can accept.”

“Thank you.”

She returned the shirt with the stain still there, but less hard. Not every result had to be instantaneous to be real. Elias folded the cuff outward to dry.

Adrien had gone back to his work on the shelf. The lamp lit one temple and the glass of his spectacles. Elias went over, waited for him to finish the motion he was making, and asked to see the tool.

His father put it in his hand without asking why. An ordinary repair, a grip that needed to become firm again. Elias found the play and reached for the right implement. His fingers obeyed him. The afternoon's faint tremor had not returned.

He wished he could hold more things together: a point he had reached, a person, a tool. He wished he understood how much of that desire came from the training he lacked and how much from fear that he had not been enough. There was no answer he could buy that evening. There was a piece in his hand and his father waiting to see it aligned.

He seated it on the second attempt. Adrien checked, then passed him the screw.

Once the tool was closed again, his father had him test its grip against a piece of wood. It held, then slipped a little. Elias was already looking for the point to adjust when Adrien stopped his hand.

“Look underneath.”

A wood shaving had caught between the surfaces. Elias removed it, tried again, and this time the grip held. He handed the tool to his father, who turned it over in his hands without adding anything. For years Elias would have taken that silence as judgment on his mistake. That evening he watched him carefully put away the little piece of wood too, because it would serve for another test, and understood that Adrien was simply working with him. He sat closer to the light.

On the other side of the room Lise asked whether anyone had seen her folded sheet. Elias pointed to the place he had left clear. She found it without having to move anything and sat down to work.

The shirt stayed hanging beside the window. The white of the glue was still visible on its cuff.
