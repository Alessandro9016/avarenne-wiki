Chapter 62 — Footnote's Question

Elias began with the full tank. Livia let him talk until he said the joint had stayed dry.

“Stayed?”

The word changed his face. He had placed his jacket on the chair back with the air of someone bringing good news who deserved at least time to tell it; now he looked at the sleeve as though it held the point where he should begin again.

“The old section broke open yesterday.”

Livia laid her pen across the inkwell. It was the only place she could put it without staining the papers of the work she had not yet finished. “How open?”

Elias told her about the water. Not all at once: first the fitting, then the shield created by the duke, finally Noemi and the key. Livia waited for the moment when he could say nothing had happened. He did not. He showed her with a hand how small a space he had kept clear.

“And you tell me after the successful test.”

“I wanted to tell you today had gone well.”

“I understood that.”

Livia pushed a folder toward the dry side of the table. One sheet slid over the edge and fell; Elias bent to pick it up, she did the same, and their fingers collided. It was not a pleasant or significant touch. They were simply getting in each other's way.

“I got home late yesterday,” he said.

“I don't expect a messenger every time you get your shoes wet.”

“It was more than that.”

“Exactly.”

She took back the sheet. A word was missing from the heading that she had already added on one copy and forgotten on the other. The evening before she had spent almost an hour determining which was correct; only a few hours earlier he had looked for a valve key inside a jet of water. The comparison came against her will and made her angrier still. Her work did not become ridiculous because Elias's could hurt him.

“I thought you'd worry,” he said.

“Yes. That's what happens when someone I know risks getting hurt. It isn't a disease to prevent by withholding the news.”

Elias leaned against the edge of the chair. Livia wished he would defend himself enough to let her stop being right. He looked at the papers and asked whether she was finishing something urgent.

“Yes.”

“I can wait.”

The answer removed a second easy accusation. Livia took up her pen, added the missing word, and reread the sentence. When she had put the copy right, she found Elias in the same position, the fallen sheet aligned with the others and his hands empty.

“Was Noemi hurt?” she asked.

“A cut. Alexander healed her.”

Livia looked at him. Elias began explaining the gesture before she could ask: hand on wrist, blood stopping, whole skin. He had not used Cradle like glue on an arm. Elias had not known how to name the exercise and did not pretend he had recognized it.

“So there's that too,” she said.

“Yes.”

The urge to ask more questions swept over her with unpleasant speed. She had just scolded a friend for wanting only the reassuring result, and now she wanted to extract the largest secret from his account. She returned to the detail she could actually know.

“The cut is healed. He stayed at the Mutual. And you went home.”

Elias nodded. “My father would like me never to leave the workshop again.”

“Not me.”

She said it too quickly. It was true, but not simple. She wanted him to keep finding things to measure in the world; she also wanted their friendship not to become the place where he brought the already dried version of accidents. She would have to learn to ask for the second without sounding like the voice of the first.

Edith arrived as Livia closed the folder. She had held a folded sheet between her fingers, wearing the ink off the crease. Seeing their faces, she asked whether she should wait outside.

“No,” Livia said.

“Did you lose?” Edith asked Elias.

“It wasn't a contest.”

“Then you lost badly.”

Livia laughed before she could stop herself. Edith removed her coat, sat, and listened to the part of the story she had not heard. She did not take his wrist to check for an injury he did not have. She merely asked whether he had eaten; when he said yes, she said at least this time she would not be talking to a hungry man.

They entered Room Six through the ordinary door. Livia lit the lamp they would use and left the others dark, because the long table did not need light at either end to welcome three people. The empty chairs stayed where they were. Marcel did not enter with them; helping the project had not made him a fourth member.

Livia put the folder outside the space they had set aside for the exchange. She opened a new page in the notebook and wrote the date. Footnote appeared on the previous page beside a hasty correction; Northbound was beneath it, neater. Edith took the chair that let her see the door.

“Now tell the good part,” Livia said.

Elias did. The water rose, people learned the controls, the shield had been put away. When he mentioned the woman who had stopped carrying buckets, Edith asked where the outlet was. He drew a short route. The question brought out a detail Livia had not heard the first time: they had not changed only the machine, they had changed part of someone's day.

“And after the duke left?” Edith asked.

“It kept going.”

Elias smiled. Repeating it did him good. Livia saw that and let his satisfaction arrive whole before making notes.

“I'd like to talk to him,” she said.

The pen stopped above the page. She had not prepared the sentence; she realized that from the way the other two looked at her.

“About the cut?” Elias asked.

“That too. But not to have it told like a fairground act.”

Edith rested an elbow on the table. “What would you ask him?”

Livia looked at the notebook. She could have listed wishes large enough to fill the room: records that would not be lost, copies that did not depend on a tired hand, people who did not reach the office too late because a word had ended up in the wrong heading. Not all those wishes belonged to a power whose effects they had only seen.

“How he thinks about what he creates. Whether he needs someone to check the forms, whether he has to know them. Elias showed him a pump. I know how to make a document precise enough for people who have never met to work together.”

“Do you want to offer him work?” Edith asked.

“I don't want to hand him a request for protection.”

“I've heard what you don't want. What do you give him?”

Livia tightened her grip on the pen. The question annoyed her because it was exactly what she would ask someone arriving with a great name and a vague project.

“A discussion,” she replied. “One where someone doesn't immediately say he's right because he can fill a workshop.”

“He can already get that from his aunt,” Elias said.

Livia looked at him, then laughed again. The correction deserved to remain. She wrote one word on the sheet, exchange, and left the next line blank.

Edith unfolded the sheet she had brought. It was an ordinary reminder about a delivery of materials and a time to change. She had rewritten it twice because the person meant to carry it out kept confusing recipient and address.

“A pump doesn't solve this,” she said. “Neither does a closed wound. I know how to notice what's missing after someone has said everything's ready. It might be useful. But I don't want to be invited to thank a man alongside you two.”

Livia took the sheet and returned it after following the wrong line. “You're missing an arrow.”

“I'm missing you reading it without already knowing the answer.”

It was the second time in a few minutes that her skill had stopped short of her confidence. Livia covered the marginal note with her palm and began again. Without the clue, the sentence sent the delivery to the wrong place. An arrow was not enough. She rewrote the sentence, then passed it to Elias. He said aloud where he would go. This time it was the right place.

Edith nodded and folded the sheet. “I can offer this. A test by the person who has to understand.”

The proposed meeting remained on the table without becoming a letter. Elias said Alexander was still in Lethorne, but the following day he had his aunt's financial matter and then the return to Rovenne. Livia did not want to squeeze into the deadline as though their curiosity should take precedence over Yselle's work.

“We'll think more about it,” she said. “First about what we have to exchange. Then about how to ask him to meet us.”

“Outside here,” Edith said.

Livia nodded. They had decided no place, date, or way to present themselves. Nobody looked for an envelope. The page with the word exchange stayed in the notebook, between their names and a space still white.

Edith was about to close her folder when a printed page slipped out. Livia recognized it by the stain on the edge: an installment of the novel they had left unfinished. Elias saw it and asked whether at least the character they were waiting for had decided to come back.

“He came back,” Edith said. “With a terrible explanation.”

“I'll believe him before I believe you,” Livia replied.

She took the page and read the line indicated. The man had been missing a week and expected to explain everything with a lost suitcase. That could not be enough: the previous chapter had left the suitcase in his hand. Livia looked for the passage on the old sheet she had kept in her notebook. It was there. She read it aloud, ready to declare the error.

Edith took the sheet before she reached the end.

“Keep reading the new one.”

The next line revealed that the suitcase was not his. Livia stayed a moment with her finger halfway down the page; Elias laughed enough to need to move his chair. She tried to maintain an offended expression but failed.

“My correction will arrive after dinner,” she said.

Edith took back the page and folded it at the right place. They had not solved anyone's work or found anything precise enough to offer a duke. For a few minutes they had been able to want the rest of a story without needing to be the most competent people in the room. Livia realized it was also for that pleasure that she wanted to return.

When they left, Elias took her folder without trying to conceal how much it weighed. Livia let him carry it as far as the door, then asked for it back.

“Tomorrow tell me even if it goes badly,” she said.

“Yes.”

He added no justification. They parted on the landing; she went back to put away the finished copies and found the pen still resting across the inkwell. The ink on its tip had dried. She cleaned it, put the table in order, and left open the page of work she would resume in the morning.

She marked the exact point in the margin so she would not have to search for it among the papers still waiting to be sorted.
