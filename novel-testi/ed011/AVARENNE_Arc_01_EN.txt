Chapter 1 — The Wrong Measure

The wall lay eleven centimeters beyond the back of the cabinet.

Elias kept his eyes closed.

He could feel the chipped edge of the workbench beneath his fingers. Beyond the wood, beyond the floor and the cabinet's legs, the room continued to yield its distances. These were not images. He could not see the screws lost in the dust or the color of the wallpaper. He knew where things ended.

And something back there ended too far away.

He opened his eyes. The back of the cabinet was dark, flat, perfectly ordinary. A cobweb joined its frame to the wall.

“Eleven centimeters,” he said.

Marcel Ruy stopped filing.

“You wrote down nine.”

“I know.”

“Then write eleven.”

Elias looked at his notebook. He had drawn the nine with a confidence he now found irritating.

“There could be a false wall.”

“There could.”

“Or a sealed recess.”

“That too.”

“Or the cabinet has a double back.”

Marcel put down the file.

“You could also move it.”

They moved the cabinet.

Behind it was a low, disused recess, a length of pipe sealed into the masonry, and enough dust to make Elias regret his curiosity. Marcel handed him the measuring rule without comment. The recess was eleven centimeters deep.

In his notebook, Elias crossed out the nine with a single stroke, leaving it legible. He wrote the correct value beside it.

His mentor returned to the vise. His gray hair was flattened on one side, with filings caught in a curl. Elias considered telling him, then decided that a man who could deliver a lesson without leaving his stool could put up with a little brass in his hair.

The sound of a cart came in from the alley. The window rattled, and two replacement glass panes clinked against a box on the shelf. The workshop smelled of oil, damp wood, and freshly worked metal. Someone called a name in the courtyard. No one answered.

It was May eighteenth. Thirteen days until the hearing.

Elias turned the page before he could count again.

On the next sheet he had drawn the warehouse where the test had taken place. The lines were tidier than the real room: no crates, no people, no sleeve caught in the wrong place. The portable test bench occupied a rectangle in the center. He had copied the distances twice, once in ink and once in pencil.

“You don't have to work on that now,” Marcel said.

“I'm taking a break.”

“From work?”

“From the cabinet.”

His mentor resumed filing. Three strokes, then another pause.

“Has a letter come?”

Elias shook his head.

“Did you go to the office?”

“Yesterday.”

“And?”

“My permit is still restricted to work within the workshop.”

“You knew that already.”

“So did they. It was a very evenly matched conversation.”

Marcel blew away a shaving. He did not smile, but the corners of his eyes tightened.

The warehouse drawing had not changed in four days. Elias had checked the subtraction until he began to doubt he knew how to subtract. The measurements recorded before the test were correct. He could have sworn to that, and not because he was especially fond of his reputation. He had repeated the checks.

The problem was what had happened afterward.

The sound always returned before the image. A sharp blow; then Etienne's arm where it should not have been. Elias had reached out and stopped a finger's breadth from the skin. He did not know whether pulling would free the man or finish what the field had begun. He remembered the sleeve dragged sideways, the cry that never became a word. Everything else arranged itself badly around that suspended finger.

Etienne could hold a glass now. The bench, though, was still there. Two sets of correct measurements, with an injured man between them. Elias had checked the figures until the paper wore thin: nowhere could he find the moment the world had stopped obeying him.

“Elias.”

Marcel's voice brought him back to the vise. He was holding the file motionless above the metal, unable to remember raising it.

Someone knocked on the open door.

Etienne Brac stood on the threshold, cap in hand. His jacket was too warm for the morning, and he held a folded envelope beneath his thumb.

Elias stood so quickly that his stool struck the bench.

“How's the arm?”

Etienne bent it.

“It bends.”

“I meant—”

“I know.”

Marcel indicated the chair by the window. Etienne sat down. Before setting down his cap, he ran his palm over the surface to make sure there was no oil.

Elias took a cloth and wiped the spot Etienne had already wiped.

“Edith says I need to start using it normally again,” Etienne said. “So you can both stop looking at it.”

They both looked at the envelope.

Etienne handed it to Marcel.

“They gave me this.”

His mentor unfolded the sheet. He read without moving his lips, then passed it to Elias.

The offer took up half a page. An amount, a date, a space for a signature. The rest was written in sentences that seemed designed to make a man read them twice and feel ashamed of the first attempt.

Elias reached the waiver of further claims. He went back. Read the sentence about the test again.

“This makes it sound as though you accept their account of the accident.”

“Does it sound like that, or does it say that?” Etienne asked.

Elias was already searching for another way to put it when he realized it was exactly the right question.

“I don't know.”

The workman nodded, without pleasure.

“That's what I said.”

Marcel wiped his fingers on his apron.

“Livia needs to see it.”

“That's why I came. You told me she understood those sentences.” Etienne looked at Elias. “I'm not asking you whether I should sign. I want to know what I'm signing.”

Elias already had an answer ready, one too big for him: don't sign, wait, we'll find the document, we'll prove everything.

He kept it behind his teeth.

“I can take it to her,” he said instead.

Etienne took back the sheet.

“I'll come too.”

He folded the letter along its existing creases, with a care Elias found difficult to watch.

“My wife says if I wait much longer, the shops will be deciding what happened. The baker's first.”

Elias lowered his eyes to his notebook.

He could not remember how much unpaid wages Etienne was owed without looking it up. He knew every distance in the warehouse.

“When did it arrive?” Marcel asked.

“Last night.”

“The envelope.”

Etienne handed it over. Marcel held it to the light, then gave that back as well.

He had not found a confession hidden among the fibers. Elias was grateful he did not pretend otherwise.

They were deciding where to meet Livia when she appeared in the alley.

Elias recognized her by her step before she reached the door: quick, but without the final burst of speed most people used to pretend they were not late. She carried a document case under one arm. Seeing Etienne, she stopped at the threshold.

“Good morning. I was looking for you.”

“Me or him?” Elias asked.

“Today, finding both of you suits me.”

Livia came in and set her case on a clear part of the bench. Marcel spread another cloth beneath it. She waited for him to finish.

Etienne showed her the letter.

Livia read it all the way through, then a second time, her finger held beneath one sentence. She did not speak at once. Elias realized he was holding his breath and let it out before anyone could notice.

“This sentence makes payment conditional on your acceptance of the attached account,” she said at last.

Etienne looked at the empty envelope.

“There wasn't anything else.”

“Then something's missing.”

“Can they put it in afterward?”

“You shouldn't sign a reference to a text you haven't received.”

“Shouldn't.” Etienne rubbed his thumb. “And if I don't sign?”

Livia handed the sheet back.

“I can help you ask for the attachment. I can't promise when they'll pay you.”

Elias wished at least one of them had a better answer. For a moment he resented her, irrationally, for having spoken so precisely that she had left no room for a comforting illusion.

Livia opened her case.

“I've brought the copy of the original position too.”

A drawing appeared on the bench, one Elias knew almost as well as his own notebook. The first time he had seen it, the relief had been so strong he had felt foolish. It was authentic. It was incomplete. Those two things still seemed incompatible.

Livia held down one corner while he moved the lamp.

“What is it supposed to show us?” Etienne asked.

“Where the test bench was when I took the measurements,” Elias said.

“Before I got hurt.”

“Yes.”

Etienne studied the lines. He pointed to a rectangle along the wall.

“Are these the crates?”

“The ones that were there at the time.”

“And this is the bench.”

Elias nodded.

The workman stayed silent long enough to make Elias want to explain everything: the reference point, the deviations, the values no one had questioned until someone had been injured. He bit the inside of his cheek.

Etienne raised his head.

“I remember where I was working. I can't tell you whether it was two steps farther to the right.”

“You don't have to,” Elias said.

He had said it too quickly.

Livia glanced at him. No obvious reproach. It was enough.

Etienne returned to the drawing.

“Who brought the bench in?”

“I don't know,” Elias admitted. “I arrived afterward.”

“Then perhaps you need someone who brought it.”

Marcel leaned against the vise. The curl full of filings fell across his forehead.

“I'd say so.”

Elias looked at the notebook lying open beside the drawing. For four days he had been asking the same figures for an answer they did not contain.

He picked up his pencil.

“The warehouse should have a record of the transport.”

“Perhaps,” Livia said.

“Or someone paid the men.”

“Perhaps.”

This time Elias smiled despite himself.

“Are you always this encouraging?”

“When you're trying to write down a certainty before you've found one.”

He did not write who had moved the bench. He wrote who might have seen it being moved, leaving the rest of the line blank.

Etienne picked up his cap.

“I can ask the others. But first I want the attachment.”

“The attachment first,” Elias agreed.

The workman stood, thanked Marcel, and left with Livia, who was explaining how to word the request. Elias watched until they turned out of sight. He had not offered to go with them. His permit did not prevent him from taking a walk. It prevented him from turning up at the warehouse to conduct another inspection. The distinction weighed on him more than he had expected.

Marcel took up the file again.

“You can finish the regulator,” he said.

Elias sat down. On the vise lay the small piece of brass with an initial engraved along its edge. Ruy marked even the pieces he would discard.

He set the piece back in the vise with the engraved side facing him. The jaw marked the metal if he tightened it too far; if he did not tighten it enough, the piece shifted at the first stroke. Before the accident, that balance had seemed something he had learned once and for all.

Now he hesitated even here. He opened the vise and started again.

Marcel turned the lamp. The line emerged from shadow and Elias's file finally found the edge. Neither mentioned the hesitation. In the silence came the scraping of metal and the wheel of a cart passing too close to the window.

“Can you see better like that?”

Elias tilted his head. The brass reflected a bright strip of light.

“Yes.”

The first stroke of the file was short. He was not measuring the warehouse or preparing an answer for a man who had not questioned him yet. A shaving lodged between the tool's teeth. He cleared it with the brush and tried to find the same angle a second time.

For a while they worked without speaking.

Then Elias looked up at the cabinet, still out of place. Behind it, the recess stood open, ugly and full of dust.

“Shall we put it back?” he asked.

“After you've cleaned.”

Elias looked at his mentor.

“You'd already found it, hadn't you?”

Marcel kept filing.

“It's my workshop.”

Elias laughed. It did him good, and for a moment he felt guilty about that too.

He took up the regulator again but left his pencil between two pages of the notebook. On one side were the recess's eleven centimeters; on the other, the bench he remembered measuring correctly. He had found the first error by moving a cabinet.

For the second, he would have to find whoever had moved something after him.

Chapter 2 — The Hand on the Sheet

The hand on the sheet had no scars.

He turned it slowly, examining the back, then the palm. The fingers were long, the nails clean. There was no pain beneath the skin. He tried closing it, expecting a resistance that never came.

Footsteps reached him from the corridor, a rustle of cloth, the faint knock of something against a door.

He breathed.

The air smelled of water left in a jug and dry linen. Above the bed, a fine crack in the ceiling had been poorly concealed with paint. He knew it. He had been fifteen when he first noticed it, one morning when his mother had forbidden him to get up.

No.

Alexander had been fifteen.

He remembered the woman bending over that bed, the way she touched his forehead with the back of her fingers. He also remembered his own mother, another woman, her hands worn by work, in a place where that ceiling would have seemed an obscene luxury.

There was no one else inside him to share those memories.

The boy's life ended with a sweet taste, a half-finished letter, the sickness that kept him from calling loudly enough. His own continued here, inside a hand he had never used.

Edran Vael sat up.

The sheet slipped to his waist. A glass stood on the bedside table, beside the bell. He reached for it and drank. The water had taken on a little of the room's warmth.

Only as he drank did he realize he was thirsty.

That small delay seemed wonderful.

He put the glass down. Beneath the body's stillness he recognized what remained his: five distinct workings, a familiar depth that had never ceased to belong to him. He reached farther and stopped at the limit. The vastness he remembered no longer answered as it once had.

Perhaps he ought to have felt fear. Anger.

For now, he listened to his breathing.

The handle turned.

The woman who came in was carrying a small tray. She stopped so abruptly that the water in its bowl almost reached the rim.

Nela Bress. Her name came with the memory of her voice. Thirty-one, a nurse. The boy had met her. The man in the bed had never heard her footsteps.

“Your Grace.”

Edran looked at the tray, then at her.

“You can put that down.”

Nela did, with a care that seemed intended for something far more fragile than porcelain. She came closer.

“Can you hear me clearly?”

“Yes.”

“Do you know where you are?”

It was a reasonable question. The complete answer would have ruined the morning for both of them.

“In my room, at Rovenne.”

“And do you recognize me?”

“Nela.”

For a moment, her shoulders lowered. She asked him to follow her finger with his eyes, and he obeyed. It had been a long time since anyone had told him to do something so simple with such easy authority.

“Any pain?”

“None.”

“Nausea? Dizziness?”

He shook his head.

Nela brought the tray closer, then stopped halfway through the movement.

“Before you drink any more, I should like to check that you can swallow properly.”

“I have already had some.”

He looked at the empty glass. Only then did she notice it, and the small change in her expression told him how tired she must be. For four days she had watched over an unresponsive body; now she had to catch up with each action after he had already performed it.

Edran leaned against the pillows.

“What would you like me to do?”

Nela poured him a mouthful from the jug without filling the glass. She waited until he had swallowed. He also obeyed her request to speak afterward, stating his title and the city's name. His voice had a new depth. He felt it in his chest before he heard it in the room, and caught himself wanting to try it again, like a door newly restored to its hinges.

He could have spoken his own name. His real one. Seeing it reach the woman's face without the least recognition might have made the time that had passed more tangible than a calendar could.

“Alexander Valcienne,” he said instead.

Nela nodded. The name was correct; none of her professional questions required her to ask who had spoken it.

He was glad of that, then felt a resentment as brief as it was unfair. He had left things in the world greater than this house, yet being recognized by a single person had become impossible. The body before her provided a sufficient answer. Edran would have to learn to live in the distance between those two truths.

“Does the light bother you?”

He shook his head. Even so, Nela moved away the fold of curtain swaying beside the bed, so that its edge would not brush his face.

The gesture surprised him more than the question. In Alexander's life, people constantly did small things like that: moving an object, lowering a voice, leaving a place free. He did not remember allowing the boy time to notice them when he had passed through all those memories in a flash. Now the fabric really was farther from his cheek. Someone had moved it for him.

“Were you here last night as well?”

“For part of it.”

“Did I speak?”

Nela straightened the cloth on the tray, then said that she had not heard him utter any intelligible words. She did not turn her own shift into the entire duration of the crisis. She asked whether he remembered a dream.

Edran searched. He found the sweet taste in the boy's mouth, then this bed. No corridor to travel between his own death and the morning; no voice arriving to announce a task.

“No.”

“If anything comes back to you, tell Mirel.”

The woman began folding the damp cloth. A drop ran along her thumb to the small patch of reddened skin. She did not seem to feel it.

Edran followed the redness to where the cloth rubbed the skin. He could have closed it with Heart. Nela's fingers were already on his wrist: counting, pausing, resuming. He waited until she removed her hand. An inexplicable healing would have given her a second body to observe.

Nela took his wrist. Her hands were warm, with a small red patch near one thumb. She counted silently, then began again.

“Am I counting wrong?” he asked.

She looked up.

“I'm the one doing the checking.”

Edran smiled.

“You're right.”

The nurse released his wrist and straightened the edge of the sheet for no apparent reason.

“I'll call Mirel. Don't get up yet.”

“How long?”

“Just a moment. She's—”

“How long have I been like this?”

Nela followed his gaze to the bed.

“Four days. You were very ill.”

Four days.

May eighteenth, the year eight hundred and twelve. The duke's calendar recognized those numbers; Edran's memory could find nowhere to put them. He sought a kingdom's name, a capital, a war to place between his death and this morning. Ruined cities returned to him, and people who should have been dust. Nela was watching, waiting for an answer to her question about the light.

“It doesn't bother me,” he said.

Beneath the sheet he closed his hand. The First Testament was still accessible within his existence; the depth of The Origin answered too. Neither told him why his return had coincided with this boy's death. The dead man whose face he wore could no longer answer him.

“My sister?”

The nurse's expression changed. A different sort of attention, more personal.

“She's in the house.”

“Is she well?”

“She's been very worried.”

Edran remembered Celine on a bench, her knees dirty, asking him not to tell the governess where she had fallen. Twelve, then. He remembered another, more recent conversation: a promise to take her to the library, postponed before the shipwreck and then swallowed by grief.

The images were new to him and already familiar. The pain within them had a different shape from his own.

“Tell her I'm awake.”

Nela hesitated.

“I'd rather the doctor—”

“Don't promise her anything more than that.”

The nurse nodded and left.

Edran listened to her footsteps recede. From the courtyard came the sound of a wheel on gravel. Someone laughed, far enough away not to feel guilty.

He ran a hand through his hair. Short. Black. He touched his face, finding features he remembered from Alexander's mirrors and would not have recognized without those memories.

On the table, his damp fingerprints marked the empty glass.

He was back.

At last he allowed himself the whole thought, and joy came without ceremony: a fierce impulse, almost a laugh. There was still air, water, a room to cross. He could open a door. He could choose.

Then he saw the dressing gown folded at the foot of the bed, ready for a man who would never wear it again.

He stayed still.

The door opened a second time.

Celine came in alone. She stopped just inside, one hand still on the handle. Edran recognized her immediately. The light from the window left half her face in shadow.

She was smaller than the memories had led him to expect.

“Nela said I could,” she said.

“Come in.”

She left the door open and approached. She looked at the glass, the bed, his feet beneath the sheet. When she reached the chair, she seemed no longer to know what to do with her hands.

“You're sitting up.”

“Yes.”

“By yourself?”

Edran nodded.

Celine took the chair by its back and pulled it closer. A wooden leg scraped across the floor. She sat much too properly.

From this spot on the bed, Alexander had been used to seeing her drop into the chair and start talking before she had settled. Edran remembered the boy's impatience when he was in a hurry. He could have reproduced his smile, the teasing reply, even a nickname.

He did not.

“Have you slept?” he asked.

She looked at him as though he had asked something insulting.

“You have.”

For a moment he had nothing to say. Then a breath of laughter escaped him. Celine lowered her eyes, but her lips moved.

“Four days seems a long time,” Edran said.

“It is.”

The silence that followed was not peaceful. He could feel everything he might do to break it: a remembered joke, reassurance, a quiet command that would take away her distress. The ease of that last possibility disgusted him.

He let her have the silence.

“Yselle kept saying to wait,” Celine murmured. “Everyone said to wait. Then when I asked what we were waiting for, they changed the subject.”

Edran looked at her hand resting on the edge of the bed. Her fingers gripped the fabric.

“They haven't told you much.”

“They'd tell you.”

There was no accusation in her voice. Worse: she considered it so obvious a fact that it needed no defense.

“I'll ask Mirel to speak to you,” he said. “If you'd like.”

“I don't want her to use that gentle voice.”

“You can tell her.”

Celine looked up. She studied his face, searching for something he had no right to assume he had given back to her.

“Are you really all right?”

“I'm not in pain now.”

“That isn't what I asked.”

Edran accepted the rebuke with a slight nod. Proud. Alexander had known that. He was discovering it.

“I've only just woken up. I still need to understand what happened.”

It was true, and not enough.

Celine released the sheet. For a few moments she stared at the crease she had made. Then she set her hand near his, without touching him.

He moved his fingers closer. Waited.

She was the one who took his hand.

Her grip was fierce, almost angry. Edran felt the trembling she had hidden as she came in, and held her hand without trying to stop it.

When Mirel appeared in the doorway, the girl let go and turned abruptly, wiping her cheek with an awkward gesture.

The doctor stopped.

“May I?”

Celine nodded, then looked at her brother.

“I'll come back later.”

“All right.”

She took two steps, then turned again.

“You don't have to answer letters today.”

He remembered the desk, the papers, the boy who for weeks had mistaken a delayed reply for a failing.

“Not today,” he said.

Celine went out. Mirel waited until her footsteps had faded before approaching the bed.

Edran looked at his free hand on the sheet.

He had the strength to tear that house from its foundations. A moment earlier, he had not known what to promise a child.

Chapter 3 — A Place at the Table

When Elias got home, his father was arguing with a lamp.

It was not an evenly matched contest. The lamp occupied almost the entire table, Adrien what little space remained, and between them lay three screwdrivers, a dirty plate, and a cup no one should ever drink from again.

“You're early,” his father said.

“The kitchen clock says otherwise.”

“The kitchen clock is slow.”

“Shall we fix it?”

“As soon as someone pays me to.”

Elias hung up his jacket. He had spent the whole walk imagining his arrival: he would say that work was progressing, that Livia had found a way to ask for the missing document, that Etienne was well. He would mention nothing that might make Adrien lower his screwdriver and really look at him.

The lamp offered a way in.

“What's wrong with it?”

“An owner convinced he can repair it.”

“He opened it?”

Adrien pointed to a screw with a mangled slot.

Elias sat down. He looked at the connection, the bent wire, the tiny crack along the edge of the insulation.

“Here.”

“I've seen it.”

“I thought—”

“You can pass me the small box.”

The small box contained at least three things Elias remembered breaking as a child. His father kept them without ever deciding whether they were spare parts or reproaches.

He found the piece Adrien needed and handed it over.

Adrien took off his glasses, cleaned one lens with the edge of his shirt, and began again. He only worked slowly when someone else had already tried to go too fast.

Elias recognized that movement of his hands. It had been the first trade he wanted and the first he tried to leave behind.

“Did Marcel let you finish anything?” his father asked.

“A regulator.”

“Did you finish it?”

“Yes.”

Adrien nodded.

Elias waited for the question about the warehouse. It did not come.

He felt relieved and, immediately afterward, vaguely offended.

The kitchen door opened. Lise came in carrying a pot wrapped in a cloth and stopped in front of the table.

“No.”

Adrien did not look up.

“One moment.”

“The soup isn't waiting for you to finish an installation.”

“It isn't an installation.”

“Then it'll be easier to move.”

Elias picked up the lamp before his father could object. He carried it to the shelf by the window, gathering up a small dish of screws. Lise set down the pot and began laying out bowls.

A breadbasket occupied his mother's place.

For years Elias had continued to think of that chair as an empty seat. Lise had stopped before he did. She put books on it, laundry to fold, a basket that got in the way of anyone trying to pass. At first he had found it unbearable. Then he had begun moving things without protesting.

That evening he left the breadbasket where it was.

“I saw Etienne,” he said as they sat down.

Lise ladled out the soup.

“How is he?”

“Well. He's using his arm.”

“Good.”

Elias nodded. He picked up his spoon. He was hungry, but the heat of the soup brought the folded letter back to mind.

“They've offered him a payment.”

Adrien broke the bread.

“A good one?”

“I don't know.”

His father passed him half the loaf.

“Do you know how much he's short?”

Elias lowered his spoon.

“Not the full amount.”

“How much are we short?”

There it was. The question he had prepared for on his walk home.

“The workshop's still paying me.”

“I know.”

“My permit's restricted, not revoked. And if the hearing—”

“Elias.”

Adrien had set his bread beside the bowl. His fingers were still stained black despite washing them.

“I asked whether you know what money we'll need this month.”

Lise kept eating. Elias wished she would look up and give him something to argue with.

“The usual expenses.”

“And mine,” she said.

“What expenses?”

“The journey for my interview.”

Elias looked at her.

Lise rested her spoon in the bowl.

“I told you I'd applied.”

He remembered an evening, her standing by the door while he copied a drawing. He had asked her to give him a moment. Later he had eaten alone, from a plate kept warm for him.

“Have they replied?”

“Three days ago.”

“And you didn't—”

He stopped.

Lise waited with an expression very like their father's when confronted with a damaged screw.

“When?” Elias asked.

“Next week. I need to confirm the appointment.”

She did not announce the school's name as though it were an important revelation. To her it was already something real, something she had discussed with someone, made calculations for, waited to hear from. Elias was arriving late to a part of her life.

“Is it far?”

“We can walk there, too. There is no need for that expression.”

“What expression?”

“The one you make when you're about to say you're coming with me.”

Elias closed his mouth.

Adrien returned to his bread. He looked like a man very busy not interfering.

“I can go on my own,” Lise said. “I need the fare and one morning when I don't have to tidy your papers before I leave.”

“You don't have to tidy my papers.”

“Then tonight you can start with the windowsill.”

His notebook about the test was on the windowsill. Beside it lay three sheets, a caliper, and the cup Elias used for rinsing his pens. He had not noticed them when he came in.

“I'll move them.”

“Thank you.”

They ate for a few minutes. A voice in the courtyard announced that a child had lost a shoe. Another replied that they knew exactly where it was, and that this was precisely why the child would not be getting it back just yet.

Lise smiled into her bowl.

Elias realized he wanted to ask what it had been like, waiting for that reply. Instead he asked how much she needed.

She told him. It was a small sum, small enough to make him ashamed of his relief.

“I can contribute.”

“You already put in your share,” Adrien said. “First we decide what's needed. Then who pays.”

His father had not raised his voice. Elias would have found a scolding easier to defend himself against.

“I don't want this business costing you.”

“It already does,” Lise said.

He looked up.

She met his eyes frankly.

“Not necessarily money. We talk to you, and sometimes you don't hear us.”

Elias looked at the basket on the chair. The bread had left a pale dusting on the wood.

He could have told her it was unfair. That he was afraid. That when he closed his eyes he saw a wrong distance and the arm of a man who had trusted him.

Lise had been there that night. She was the one who had brought him a clean shirt. She did not need him to explain it.

“I'm sorry,” he said.

She nodded without immediately setting aside her irritation.

Adrien gathered up the bowls. Lise told him to leave them. He pretended not to hear and took them into the kitchen.

Elias got up to collect his papers from the windowsill.

Beneath his notebook lay a page of Lise's exercises, folded to make room for it. He smoothed it with his hand. Some figures had tiny, orderly notes beside them. He could not follow all the steps. Not understanding them did him good.

“Are you taking these to the interview?”

She came over.

“Some of them.”

“Will you show me?”

Lise looked at him, then at the kitchen clock.

“Now?”

Elias put his own notebook on the clear shelf.

“Now.”

Lise returned from the kitchen with a dry cloth. She wiped the table once, gathering crumbs into her palm, and took a bowl to hold the few objects in danger of falling.

Elias lifted the calipers off her notebook.

“This doesn't need to be here.”

“I've been saying that for two evenings.”

His sister retrieved a thin board from beneath a stack of papers. She had attached a base with four pencil marks and a small bracket. Nothing one could show a client; something that could be altered without regretting the wasted material. Elias reached toward the bracket, then stopped his hand.

“What am I supposed to look at?”

Lise sat down. That question seemed to amuse her more than all the jokes over dinner.

“How the load shifts when you change where you hang it.”

She showed him the first exercise on the page. Elias understood the problem before she finished explaining and felt the temptation to solve it for her. He looked at his sister's hands: a red mark still showed on one finger where the cloth she had used for the saucepan had been too thin.

She had been doing things all day while he prepared to explain his accident. She had not stopped studying because of it. She had simply found the time between one thing and the next, and Elias did not know when that had been.

“Did you make this mark?”

“All of them.”

“I meant the point the string starts from.”

“Then ask that.”

She smiled, without sharpness this time. She handed him one end of the cord. Elias held it between two fingers while she moved the bracket one notch. The base tilted toward him.

“You're pulling.”

“I'm holding it still.”

“Exactly. You need to let it slide.”

He did. The board tapped against the bowl, and a screw from the lamp clinked against its rim.

From the kitchen, Adrien asked what they had broken.

“His pride,” Lise said.

“I only mend that if I'm paid.”

Elias laughed. He had not imagined his father was still listening. When he took the cord again, he let it run over his fingertip rather than gripping it. The base settled into a different position, clear enough to explain his previous mistake without a single remark about the talent of the person who had made it.

Instinctively, he reached for the distance with Astrolabe. He did not activate the ability. Just then he wanted to understand what Lise had done to produce that movement, and the answer was not hidden in the space beneath the board.

“How long have you been working on it?”

“I don't know. In bits.”

“You could have asked me.”

Lise put the bracket back where it had been.

“I wanted to manage it when you weren't at home, too.”

There was no grand declaration in the words. She said much the same shortly afterward, when he offered to find her better materials: these were enough for the exercise. She would ask for something else when she knew what she lacked.

Elias looked around for somewhere to put the testing notebook. He knew this room down to the stains on its walls, yet for days he had chiefly seen obstacles between himself and a page to check again. He moved his papers to the shelf beside his jacket. He left the middle of the table clear.

“If I go with you, I can carry your box. Then I'll wait wherever you like.”

“First I need to know which morning it'll be.”

“All right.”

“And don't tell whoever receives me that I can already do everything.”

“I've never done that.”

Lise raised an eyebrow. Elias remembered at least two conversations in which he had described his sister's ability to people who had not yet asked her a question.

“I won't,” he corrected himself.

She passed him the page. An arrow in the margin linked two distant steps. Elias did not understand why, and before asking, he took the time to follow it to its point.

She picked up a pencil. She pointed to the first step with an air that reminded him painfully of himself.

“Don't start by saying you know this part.”

“I don't.”

“Good.”

When Adrien returned, he found them bent over the page. He put the repaired lamp between them. Lise covered the solution with her hand before her brother could peek.

“This time I'll wait,” Elias said.

“Until I get it?”

“Until you throw me out.”

Their father sat with his piece of bread. A few minutes later, it was Lise who asked for the light to be brought closer.

Chapter 4 — What Remains

Mirel Osta asked him to walk to the window.

Edran pushed back the sheet and set his feet on the floor. It was cool. He stood without leaning on the bed.

The doctor had already reached out a hand. She left it suspended for a moment, then lowered it.

“Slowly.”

He slowed. Not because he needed to. He remembered enough about the woman's profession to understand that he was not making this easy for her.

At the window he found the courtyard Alexander knew. A man was checking a wheel; two people stood talking by a service entrance. Farther away, beyond the wall, trees and rooftops hid the road.

His last view of the world had been different.

He did not pursue it.

“Are you dizzy?”

“No.”

“Come back here.”

He sat where she indicated. Mirel examined him, asked questions, listened to his breathing. The metal of the instrument was cold. He answered patiently until she returned to the same note for the third time.

“Something doesn't match,” he said.

Mirel closed the notebook with a finger between the pages.

“A great many things.”

“Your diagnosis?”

“The course of your illness.”

Mirel asked him to spread his fingers, then close them around two of her own. Edran gauged his grip to suit the boy's body, with a care he could not have explained without frightening the doctor. She checked both hands and made him repeat the movement.

“Do you find any difference?”

“Between the two?”

“Between what I ask and what you can do.”

He liked the question. It was far more exact than asking whether he had recovered. He felt a healthy body, immense possibilities still gathered beneath its skin, and a previous life ready to lend him habits nobody would find strange. None of that told the doctor how he had reacted four days earlier.

“Not so far.”

Mirel repositioned the instrument. She asked him to cough. He did, feeling ridiculous for having expected a more important reason.

Part of him wanted to declare the examination over. For a very long time, he had had people ready to accept his assessment of reality before their own. The woman in front of him still had a finger on the previous page and another question to ask. Edran waited.

“You can tell me what you have found without trying to phrase it reassuringly.”

“That is what I am trying to do.”

“With little help from me.”

Mirel looked up from her notebook for a moment. A small concession to humor, promptly returned to the work.

Mirel searched for a page, holding another down with her wrist. Edran saw only a date before the sheet turned toward him.

“This part is yours,” the doctor said. “I need to put the other observations in order first.”

He looked at the finger marking the line. This woman had written while Alexander's heart ceased to cooperate. He wanted every sheet, wanted to find the exact moment an empty body had remained. Instead he asked which drink had been kept. Mirel stopped turning the pages.

She sat down. She had moved the chair so she could look at him without tilting her head. Edran approved.

“Do you remember when you first felt unwell?”

He remembered the taste of the cordial. The letter Alexander had been writing. He remembered the first attempt to stand, the armrest that seemed to draw away from his hand.

“In the evening. I was working.”

“The fourteenth.”

He nodded.

“Did you take anything?”

“Some cordial from the tray. I didn't think it was medicine.”

Mirel wrote a few words.

“I treated you for a toxic reaction. We addressed the symptoms and what we could establish. I haven't adequately identified the substance yet.”

The words settled between them.

Edran's expression did not change. Inside him, the boy's memory arranged itself more clearly: the drink, the pain, the hand that could not reach the bell. No decision to die. No farewell.

“So it wasn't simply an illness.”

“A substance harmed you. I don't yet know how it reached you.”

The doctor watched him closely, perhaps expecting the thought to frighten him. Edran remembered poisons, clumsy attempts, other patients. His first response was an old, practical anger: someone had used access to the household.

Then he thought of Celine in the chair.

“The container?”

“Kept.”

“Where is it?”

Mirel indicated a case on the cabinet.

“I'd like to continue the tests.”

“You will.”

She did not stand at once.

“I ask that you don't throw it away or have it washed.”

He looked at her. She must have dealt with some remarkably stubborn patients.

“I don't intend to.”

Mirel opened the case. She took out the closed vial and set it on the bedside table, on a clean cloth.

There was nothing threatening about the object. Small enough for one hand. A residue remained at the bottom of the glass. Edran could have erased it with an ease he already felt in his fingers.

He did not touch it.

“Who brought it?”

“I wasn't the one who received it.”

“Can you ask for the delivery notes from those days to be kept?”

Mirel nodded.

“The staff should be able to help you trace it. I have no reason to say that whoever prepared your tray knew anything.”

“Neither do I.”

He said it firmly enough to make her look up.

The boy knew the names of the people in the kitchen. He knew who would lower their eyes before a question from the duke even if they had done nothing wrong. Edran knew how efficiently a frightened household could produce an innocent person to blame.

“Ask that no one be accused simply for handling a bottle.”

She began writing again. This time the pen moved faster.

As she put the vial away, she shifted a separate small box. The lid slipped a little, revealing two dull fragments.

Edran looked at them.

Mirel noticed.

“An instrument I used during the crisis.”

That moment was absent from Alexander's memories. His return did not give him memories of the hands that had worked on the body before it became his.

“Did it break?”

Mirel carefully replaced the lid.

“It was used up. I'd like to discuss it with you when I have the notes in front of me.”

Edran nodded. He had recognized enough to want the rest immediately, but not enough to know what the doctor had actually observed.

“Keep those too.”

“I am keeping them.”

A smile escaped him.

“I'm beginning to understand.”

Mirel gathered her instruments. Before leaving, she paused beside the bed.

“Is there anyone you'd like to see?”

The question went through his chest with unexpected force.

There were people he needed to find. Names that for centuries had meant a voice, a hand, a particular way of remaining silent. He did not yet know whether they existed in this world, whether they had buried him, cursed him, forgotten him. The missing stretch of time was too vast to fit into a question addressed to a woman holding a notebook.

“Albin,” he answered. “Later. First I'd like to get dressed.”

Mirel said she would have his clothes brought and urged him to eat something. Then she left him alone.

Edran waited until the door was closed.

The glass remained on the table. He picked it up, felt its weight, turned it slowly. A simple object: matter arranged in a shape that someone had once needed to imagine for the first time.

He put it back.

He opened his free hand.

A small goblet of clear glass appeared in his palm, half full of water. Its weight settled on his fingers all at once. A drop slipped over the rim and wet his wrist.

Edran held his breath.

He had not lost that gesture.

Memory brought back a distant hunger, a first working infinitely less certain. Back then, a little matter had been a chance wrested from the following day. Time had turned that need into power. Even the memory of his greatest heights could not rob him of the absurd delight of once again holding something he had created.

He drank.

The water was cool.

For a moment he wanted to laugh. Then he set the goblet down and watched it remain, intact, while he thought of something else. The world did not need his attention to preserve it.

He picked it up again. He let destruction reach the glass beneath his fingers and spread only through the object.

The goblet ceased to exist.

No sound, no shards on the floor. His fingers closed around a vanished weight. Edran watched them until he felt the earlier drop cooling on his wrist.

That had remained.

He could do much more. He remembered how much, and had no need to endanger a house to prove he was alive.

He passed his thumb over the water.

He could have created a face identical to the boy whose life he remembered. A body, a breath. It would not have given Celine back her brother.

He lowered his hand.

The drop on his wrist spread beneath his thumb. Edran wiped his skin with a corner of the sheet, then sat looking at the small damp mark. Had he told someone about his joy, he would have needed a grander image: a return, a sun, a shattered door. Instead, he only wanted to touch that vanished cup again.

He opened and closed his hand without creating anything.

It was all too easy to use power to fill the time in which he did not know what to do. He had known that temptation before. A person could build something splendid to avoid a conversation, and even find people willing to thank him for it.

The room held the young duke's belongings. A worn edge on the little table, a fold in the cloth, the spot in the mirror where the light made a face difficult to see. None of these things asked him to become less than he had been. They asked for attention, and attention was work he could not delegate to his strength.

He reached for the dressing gown. Its belt fell to the floor as he lifted it. He bent to retrieve it and felt the length of his legs, the altered distance between his hands and the ground. The memory of the gesture was precise; the experience was arriving now. Had he possessed a hundred bodies like this, none could have made that first movement for him.

He put it on. The fabric was soft in an almost irritating way, made for a man who had never needed to justify his right to be warm.

He thought of his mother's hands, then left them in their past. He did not want to use ancient misery to despise every good thing he found. This house might yet prove hostile; for now it had offered water, a bed, and a woman checking whether he could stand.

He realized he was hungry. He had discovered it as he had thirst: later than the body, and with a gratitude that left him defenseless.

He moved the cloth off the little table so that whoever came in could set something down. It was a tiny gesture. After so many impossible things, it cost him only the time it took.

By the time someone knocked, he had picked up the dressing gown. The man bringing his clothes asked whether he wanted help, and Edran recognized the face, the name, an entire domestic routine that, for the other man, had never been interrupted.

“Put them here. Thank you.”

The man obeyed. The relief he tried to hide was almost painful.

Alone again, Edran chose a shirt. His fingers knew where to find the buttonholes before he had grown used to the length of his arms. He dressed without haste.

A mirror stood near the window. The face he found in it was young, handsome, with golden eyes that now answered his gaze. He tried to imagine Celine's expression when she looked for reassurance and he failed to give her the right answer.

On the little table, the wet mark left by the cup was drying. He could make the glass again, identical down to the last irregularity of its rim. The boy's hand that had preceded his, though, would not return to seek it.

Edran looked at the bell. Celine knew its sound; she would wait for her brother's voice.

He left the bell where it was and went to open the door.

Chapter 5 — The Request

The man carried a rolled drawing in a case that was too short.

Elias saw him from the bench and immediately felt a flicker of professional irritation. The edge of the paper stuck out, rubbing against the bag's stitching. A little rain would be enough to ruin it.

It was his first mistaken judgment of the morning. When the man unrolled the sheet, Elias discovered that the exposed portion was a blank strip pasted on to protect the drawing.

“Ruy?” the visitor asked.

Marcel looked up from the vise.

“That's me.”

“Ilian Uren. I've been asked to redraw this to scale. Before I finish, I thought I'd show it to you.”

He laid the paper on the bench and held down the corners with two fingers. It showed two plates, a central joint, and several markings Elias recognized too quickly.

Not the hinge. The family of shapes to which it belonged.

He leaned closer.

Ilian turned the sheet toward Marcel without drawing it away from Elias. He did not look like someone who enjoyed making other people ask.

“It came to me as part of an appraisal. The client says a similar piece passed through your hands.”

“How similar?” Marcel asked.

Ilian indicated a proportion.

“That's one of the problems.”

The drawing had been copied at least once. Some lines had inconsistent thicknesses. One broke off where the copyist had probably mistaken a scratch for the edge of a fitting. Beneath it, a note gave a dimension that could not be reconciled with the others.

Ilian took two small weights from his bag to hold down the corners. He used none of the tools scattered across the bench. Elias noticed because he had already been looking for something clean enough to lend him.

“The paper tends to curl back up,” the draftsman said.

“You leave it rolled up too long.”

“I carry it rolled up. When I work, I lay it flat.”

Elias lowered his eyes to the drawing. The wrong dimension was written in a clear stroke, while the lines around the joint overlapped. Whoever had prepared the copy had wanted the number, at least, to be easy to read.

“Does the request mention repairs?”

“Comparison,” Ilian replied.

Elias held the ruler without correcting the value. He liked that word less than he had a moment earlier.

Elias drew his hands back from the sheet.

“May I look from this side?”

The draftsman moved his notebook a little. His broad hand rested motionless beside the paper, and a graphite stain on his cuff had merely spread in the wash. When he indicated the inconsistent dimension, he made no gesture of exposing someone else's incompetence. He brought it into the light, allowing Marcel to follow its course.

Elias knew that restraint. Whenever he had to tell a client that the drawing provided was insufficient, the client seemed to hear a demand for more money. It was simpler to change the line quietly and hope the result was what they wanted. Ilian had preferred to bring the error with him, still visible.

The morning began to interest Elias for a reason unconnected to the component stored elsewhere.

Elias picked up a ruler.

“Who gave you the dimensions?”

“They were already there.”

“This one's wrong.”

“I know.”

Ilian opened his notebook. He had copied the calculation twice, openly crossing out the earlier conversion.

“That's why I'm here.”

Marcel wiped his hands and left the cloth on the bench.

“What have they asked you to do?”

“Produce a readable drawing and confirm the components. Compare it with an actual specimen, if possible.”

Elias immediately imagined a workshop where someone knew how to recharge the hinge. A Bearer who could recognize what Marcel could not restore. He pictured the room available for more than two openings, meetings that did not have to end when the reserve ran out.

He realized he had already moved his ruler to the point he wanted to correct.

Marcel set a hand on his open notebook.

“Do you have a written request?”

Ilian took out an envelope.

His mentor read the sheet flat on the bench so Elias could see. The examination was proposed for the following day, at a tool depot. The request was to bring the component so the test bench would not have to be moved. The owner would be compensated for his time.

“Does your client own a test bench?” Marcel asked.

“That's what I've been told.”

“Have you worked there?”

“No.”

The tip of the ruler went still beneath Elias's fingers.

Marcel read the request again.

“I don't take an object to a client who hasn't made it clear what he intends to examine.”

Ilian did not object. He almost seemed relieved.

“I can pass that on.”

“You can ask the examiner to come here. With a name, qualifications, and a proposed procedure.”

“Even if it's only to say you don't have the piece?”

Marcel looked up.

“To say I don't work that way.”

A sharp crack came from the courtyard. Someone had dropped a crate. Elias turned his head, then looked back at the drawing. Ilian was waiting for him to finish, making no attempt to fill the silence.

“Have they told you which part is supposed to be imbued with power?” Elias asked.

Ilian leafed through his notebook.

“The assembly. That's the word they used.”

He showed him the line.

There was no diagnosis, no description of how the power worked. Just a word broad enough to make anyone feel they had been precise.

“And what is it supposed to do?”

“Maintain a passage.”

Elias felt his excitement return, along with a more cautious unease.

“Where?”

“It doesn't say.”

“For how long?”

Ilian turned the notebook around. The page was blank.

Marcel went to the shelf and took down an ordinary hinge. A small one, with a plainly visible pin. He set it beside the drawing.

“This maintains a passage,” he said. “If the door's hung properly.”

Ilian smiled for the first time.

Elias wanted to laugh with them. Instead he was looking at the note about compensation. This was not an offer of maintenance. He was the one who had turned it into that possibility.

“May I copy the discrepancies?” he asked.

Ilian looked at Marcel. His mentor did not answer for him.

“The ones you find on this sheet, yes. I'm not asking you for an appraisal.”

Elias nodded. He took a fresh page. He copied the incompatible dimension and marked the break in the line. He did not add the shape of the part he knew.

It was harder than he wanted to admit. He knew something useful, and the desire to show it made his hands almost itch.

Ilian watched him work.

“Have you seen something like this before?”

“Yes,” Marcel said.

Elias looked up.

“Salvaged objects often resemble one another,” his mentor continued. “I won't certify a provenance from an incomplete copy.”

Ilian wrote that down too. He did not try to turn the yes into a larger statement.

When they had finished, he took back the sheet. Before rolling it up, he noticed the pencil mark Elias had made beside one of the dimensions.

“You can leave that. I'll report it as a doubt you raised, not an approved correction.”

“I haven't signed it.”

“That's why I'm asking.”

Elias left the mark.

Ilian read the note aloud. Elias heard how his uncertainty changed in another voice. It had seemed obvious with the sheet before his eyes; away from the line, it could sound like an agreed correction.

“Add that I worked it out from this copy.”

The draftsman lifted his pencil.

“You haven't seen the piece?”

“Not the one shown here.”

Ilian added the words, then returned to the previous sentence without a sigh. Elias wondered how often he had to redo a drawing because someone had treated information they had never given him as common knowledge.

“When did they give you the job?”

Marcel did not intervene, but his silence immediately made Elias hear the question. He corrected himself before the other man had to decide how much to answer.

“I meant to ask whether you have a deadline. We have to go out today.”

“I have to bring back an answer. The drawing can wait longer than the answer.”

“And if the client doesn't like it?”

Ilian put the cap back on his pencil.

“I prefer him to dislike something I actually said.”

Marcel cleared the edge of the bench. An old cut showed on the surface, filled with oil and dark dust. Elias ran a finger over it without thinking. Until the day before, he would have described this meeting as a chance to meet someone more competent. Now the visitor was simply trying to finish his work without claiming someone else's expertise.

“I've asked you a lot of questions,” he said.

“Some of them were useful to me.”

Ilian smiled. He offered no reassurance about the others.

Elias gathered his own sheet of discrepancies and turned it so that the visitor could check it. This time he was in no hurry to reach the secret part of the conversation. First he wanted to give an accurate account of the one they had held in front of everyone.

Marcel returned the written request and dictated his conditions for a possible examination in the workshop. Ilian noted them, then read back the address and the time by which he would bring a reply. His mentor corrected only the time. The afternoon was already taken.

The visitor put everything in his bag. At the threshold, he turned.

“For the comparison, may I say you'd like to speak directly to whoever prepared the request?”

“Yes.”

“And that you won't hand over components?”

“Until I know to whom, and why.”

Ilian nodded and left.

Elias waited until the footsteps faded down the alley.

“They might know someone.”

Marcel picked up the ordinary hinge.

“A great many people know someone.”

“I mean someone who could recharge it.”

The word hung between them. Elias looked toward the open door. No one stood on the threshold.

Marcel did not rebuke him. He let him notice for himself that he had lowered his voice afterward, not before.

“Perhaps,” he said. “If they do, they'll know how to introduce themselves.”

Elias looked at his page of discrepancies.

“I almost corrected the drawing.”

“I saw.”

“You could have stopped me.”

“I did stop you.”

There was no harshness in his voice. Elias felt it all the same.

In a matter of days, the room had become something Elias could lose. To Marcel, it was also an apparatus he did not understand well enough to leave in anyone's hands, including his own apprentice's.

“I don't want to take it away,” Elias said.

“You haven't asked me to.”

His mentor returned the hinge to the shelf. The pin struck the wood with a small, final sound.

Elias picked up the ruler. He ought to have returned to the regulator, but part of the drawing still troubled him.

He copied it at a larger scale, without completing it. Two lines, an edge, the angle where the first copyist had lifted the pen.

“What is it?” Marcel asked.

“The dimension is wrong. But the plan and side view are wrong in the same way.”

Marcel came back to the bench.

Elias pointed.

“These aren't two conversion errors. They copied a shape that was already different.”

His mentor took the sheet and turned it toward the window. He used no power. He followed the line with a fingernail, then reread Elias's note.

“It could be another piece.”

“Yes.”

The answer disappointed him. He wanted a discrepancy that could mean only one thing.

Marcel handed the page back.

“Keep it.”

Elias slipped the page into his notebook. At the top he wrote a question: why ask someone to bring a working example when they could not even describe what they wanted to compare?

Below it he left the incompatible dimension. If a real piece arrived, they could check it.

Marcel had already resumed work. The little ordinary hinge remained beside the drawing, open on the bench like a toothless mouth.

Chapter 6 — The Attachment

Etienne had brought a new envelope for the missing sheet.

He held it against his chest, protecting it with the hand he had been unable to close only a few weeks earlier. Elias watched his thumb bend over the edge and forced himself to concentrate on the office door.

“Keep that up and I'll want to put it back in the bandage,” Etienne said.

“What?”

The workman raised his hand. Sitting beside him, Livia turned her face to hide a smile.

The door opened for a man who was not the one they expected. He carried a board covered in numbers beneath his arm and crossed the corridor without looking at anyone. Etienne shifted to let him pass; Elias had already begun to stand.

He sat down again. The bench was too short to stop minding other people's elbows.

“If you want to go, we can stay,” Livia said.

“I want to be here.”

“Then stop looking at the door as though you have to open it with your forehead.”

Etienne laughed. Elias looked at him, surprised by that sound in a place where he had decided to suffer with dignity. The worker's sleeve had a fresh seam; the thread was lighter than the fabric and ran straight to the wrist. Beneath it was an arm he could use. For a moment Elias managed to see the rest of him too: a man who had dressed, discussed something before leaving, and would return to a day not entirely occupied by their file.

“What will you do afterward?”

Etienne showed him his cap.

“Try not to get it wet.”

“I meant after this.”

“I know. I'm not keeping anything important from you. I haven't decided yet.”

Elias would have found it easier to have a plan in which to place his help. A visit, an assignment, an address to reach. Instead, this was a morning the worker would have to salvage however he could, without knowing beforehand how much of it would remain.

Livia took out a sheet to write down the request. A draft from the door lifted it, and Etienne set his cap on it before she could catch it. The gesture earned him thanks and left a dusty mark on the margin.

“Now you can't say I didn't contribute,” he said.

She moved the cap away from the ink. Elias let them laugh. He had come prepared to dispute an accusation, not to discover that the others could still endure the morning without thinking about it constantly.

A cough came from the next room. The man without boots asked whether it was his turn. Nobody answered at once; then a voice called him by a surname Elias could not catch. Someone took the empty place on the bench before the first man had disappeared through the door.

Livia looked at the pencil he was turning between his fingers.

“That wears the point down, too.”

Elias put it back in his notebook. He realized he had written nothing since their arrival. The waiting had seemed too full to let anything out of his head.

The clerk returned with two pages and a register. Ink dust marked his cuff, and he looked like a man who considered the request resolved the moment he had found the right folder.

“It was left in the file.”

Etienne looked at the sheet.

“I spent quite a while looking for it in the envelope.”

“It must have been an oversight.”

Livia asked him to record that day's delivery. He looked at her, then wrote. He did not seem offended. He seemed to wish the rest of the world had fewer margins.

They stepped out beneath the portico. The air smelled of rain, though none had fallen yet. Etienne opened the new envelope at once; Livia helped him hold the sheet against a column.

Other people waited along the wall. A man had taken off his boots to rest his feet; a woman read aloud to someone holding a document upside down. No one looked at them as the victims of an exceptional wrong. For a moment, Elias felt almost offended by how easily the world accommodated other people's waiting too.

Etienne asked him to move so the man without boots could lean against the column. They stepped aside, still holding the papers between them.

“Have you lost the morning coming here?” Elias asked.

“Part of it. Not all.”

“We can try to finish sooner.”

The workman looked at the door the clerk had just come through.

“I don't think it depends on how you say it.”

Elias lowered his eyes. He had treated urgency as an argument that would persuade any reasonable person, provided he worded it well enough. The man without boots was telling the woman reading that he would return after lunch, when someone could cover for him at work.

Livia slipped a blank sheet beneath the printed one to protect it from the damp stone.

“Let's read it before we decide how much time we've lost.”

Elias recognized his name on the third line.

The account did not say he had moved the bench. It said the accident was consistent with an error in reading the reference points during preparation. It did not deny that a measurement was correct. It moved that measurement into a sentence where it no longer mattered.

“That's false.”

Livia raised a finger, without touching him.

“Which part?”

He pointed it out. Only then did he notice how much his fingertip was shaking.

“This. I used the reference points I was given.”

“Then that's what we write.”

Etienne lowered the page.

“If I don't sign, the money doesn't come.”

The sound of carriages filled the silence. Elias wanted to offer an immediate solution, even a sum he did not possess. The thirteen crowns and forty cents he remembered among his belongings were not thirteen crowns and forty cents he could give away. Some still had to become food and journeys.

“We can ask them to separate the payment from this declaration,” Livia said.

“They can say no.”

“Yes.”

“And will you come with me another time?”

The question was for Livia. She did not answer immediately. She consulted her small appointment book, then indicated a time when she could accompany him without abandoning another job.

Etienne agreed. Elias saw relief pass through his shoulders: no guarantee of the outcome, but the name of someone who would actually turn up.

“I can do that too,” he said.

“Then don't both promise me the same morning,” the workman replied. “One person who knows why they've come is enough.”

Etienne studied the place where he was supposed to sign.

“Then let's ask.”

Elias looked at him. Relief came too quickly, and shamed him. The workman noticed.

“I'm not doing it to save your name.”

“I know.”

“I want to be paid for the time I lost. Not for remembering it wrong.”

Livia took out her pen. Etienne dictated a request shorter than the ones she wrote for a living. When she tried to improve a phrase, he told her to leave it as it was. Livia did.

The first drops struck the edge of the portico. Elias took his hat and held it above the sheet, discovering that his most useful contribution to the morning was a small roof.

Before they went back inside, Etienne asked him about the transport.

“Marcel has sent the request to inspect the warehouse. He wants to compare the mounting points with the original plan.”

“Will you be there?”

Elias hesitated.

“Not as an inspector. My permit hasn't changed.”

Etienne nodded without pitying him.

“But you can say where you were.”

“That I can do.”

Etienne reread the request against the light beneath the portico. He held the sheet in both hands, far enough from his eyes not to have to admit that Livia's handwriting was smaller than he would have liked.

“Can I change this?”

Livia offered him the pen. He did not take it.

“I mean, can I say it another way?”

“Of course.”

Etienne pointed to the passage where they had stopped. He wanted to ask for the days owed without making it sound as though he had never doubted how the accident had happened. Elias again felt the urge to explain that this was precisely why they must avoid any hesitation. The sentence would have sounded good. It might have persuaded the worker to sign something that spoke better than he did.

Livia waited. Elias saw rain reaching the toe of his shoe and shifted his hat to protect the edge of the page.

“You can say you want to receive payment without accepting that account,” he said at last. “You don't have to say you already know the whole correct version.”

Etienne looked at him to see whether he meant it. Then he dictated a simpler sentence. Livia crossed out the previous one with two lines and wrote in the space remaining.

The sheet grew less handsome with every correction. Elias would have recopied it at once, but Etienne first asked whether he should sign beside the deletion too. His interest in that tiny precaution differed from theirs: he wanted to be able to recognize the same request tomorrow if someone put it before him again.

A gust carried the smell of horses and wet stone beneath the portico. The woman reading for the other applicant gathered the pages to her chest. For an instant everyone protected their paper with the same gesture, without that making them a community.

“I can help you read the answer when it comes,” Elias told Etienne.

“You can also tell me if you've got work.”

The request surprised him.

“Why?”

“Because I don't want to ask you to come here every time as though you had nothing else to do.”

Elias could have said that his own future also depended on this matter. It was true, but it did not answer the question. He explained when he had to be at the workshop and which hours remained to be agreed. Etienne nodded as he would to a colleague describing a shift.

The hat was now wet enough to sag under its own weight. Elias changed hands. The rain had stopped seeming a personal insult; it was a problem for the paper, and that at least he could address without arguing with the sky.

They went back inside and waited again. The clerk read the request, accepted a copy, and promised nothing that depended on someone else. This time Etienne obtained a receipt before the folder disappeared.

The clerk looked for the place to enter the request's reference. Etienne watched the hand holding the register. When the man indicated the margin, the worker wrote his name there slowly, forming the first letter more carefully than usual.

Elias realized he was waiting for a comment from the official. None came. The request went away with the others and the man moved on to his next task.

Etienne remained at the counter a moment, as though he had forgotten something. Then he reopened the envelope and slipped the receipt inside with the writing facing inwards. Livia waited without tugging his sleeve.

“Can the wages arrive before the hearing?” Elias asked at the counter.

The clerk indicated the request just registered. It would be forwarded; it was not an order for payment. Etienne took the receipt and put it in the envelope before Elias could ask a second time.

“If I wait until the thirty-first, the baker doesn't wait with me,” he said as they left.

Livia tightened the folder beneath her arm. “Then we ask them to answer that too.”

When they left, it was raining properly. Livia gathered the pages into her case. Etienne slipped the receipt into his new envelope and checked twice that it was there.

“Edith is expecting me,” he said. “I need to tell her whether I want another check tomorrow.”

Elias opened his mouth to ask him to remember who had been near the bench that day. He closed it.

“See you tomorrow.”

Etienne nodded and crossed the street.

Livia stayed beneath the portico with Elias. She handed him his wet hat.

“I can copy the sentence about the payment,” he said. “Without the rest.”

“For whom?”

“Marcel. He needs to know they've answered.”

She nodded. She let him copy only that part.

Elias wrote the date on the notebook page. The workman's name stayed on the letter that belonged to him. For once, it did not feel like losing information.

Chapter 7 — The Things That Continue

Albin Sere had waited until the following day to bring the accounts. The act of consideration had clearly cost him more than the climb to the study.

Alexander indicated a chair. The administrator arranged his case, checked that the ink was dry, and began with the balance.

“Seventy-seven thousand five hundred crowns. Seventeen thousand three hundred already committed.”

The figures in the duke's memory were different. Edran followed the transactions one by one, rediscovering the logic of a world that had not stopped beside his bed.

“So sixty thousand two hundred available.”

“Before any new decisions.”

“Have you ever had an owner capable of forgetting that part?”

Albin looked up.

“Rarely before his first purchase.”

Alexander smiled. The other man did not appear to regret his answer.

Wages were due on the twenty-sixth. The summary did not include names, but Edran remembered many: people the boy had met at the mills, in the courtyard, outside a door as they waited to be received.

“Continue with the commitments already approved. I don't want anyone waiting in order to prove they're indispensable.”

Albin made a note. He did not thank him for authorizing what was already owed. Edran liked that too.

The next folder concerned a road. Alexander remembered a maintenance request and looked for the point at which the work would fall to his private estate. Albin showed him that this matter belonged instead to the territorial administration.

“The money doesn't come from this balance.”

Edran looked at the two statements. Wealth and authority overlapped enough to create a confusion useful to anyone inclined to exploit it. The young duke had received enough instruction to recognize it, when someone gave him time to read.

“And my signature here doesn't replace a decision there.”

“Exactly.”

“Did you prepare two folders because I was likely to confuse them?”

Albin touched the edge of the one about the road.

“I prepare two because they are two.”

For a moment Edran saw another table, impatient hands around a map, people convinced that a victory would make every later decision simple. Albin's precision seemed an uncelebrated form of courage.

Alexander followed the edge of the map with his finger without touching the ink. In the duke's memories, that route belonged to a landscape seen from a carriage; in Albin's folder, it belonged to jurisdictions, requests, and people who would have to work on it.

“What if I left this house and had the people who work for me repair that stretch?”

The administrator waited to discover whether this was an order.

“I am asking you what would happen.”

Albin moved the private-property sheet aside, leaving the territorial map alone. He explained that commissioning work did not transfer to the landowner the right to decide for everyone who used the road. They needed to know who had authorized the access points, where the area to repair ended, what obligations would remain afterward. None of this made the road more complicated than it already was; it merely revealed people a line on a map had concealed.

Edran felt an old impatience rise between his ribs. He could have done something immense without asking for a load of stone. He could have prevented a body breaking in a pothole. He knew that, and had no intention of turning complexity into an elegant excuse to remain seated.

“In an emergency, I can act.”

“You can. Afterwards, someone needs to know what you have done.”

Albin was not disputing the power to prevent harm. He was asking not to discover one day that a direct order had shifted another cost, closed an access route, or promised maintenance in the name of an office that knew nothing about it.

Edran leaned over the map. The stretch he had indicated crossed more than one boundary. He returned to Albin's first word, access, and asked who used it today. The administrator turned the folder toward him. The mark on the paper stopped being a line to straighten.

“I want a question that can receive an answer,” he said. “Not a folder that circulates until someone forgets why it was opened.”

“Then let us begin with which stretch and which problem.”

Alexander looked at the map once more. The question brought him back to the present better than a recommendation to rest. If he wanted to be useful here, he would first have to learn about the place where he wished to exercise that usefulness.

He left the pen beside the statement. There was no signature to put at the foot of that thought.

He returned the file without signing it.

“For new requests, I'd like to see you rather than simply provide a signature.”

“I can arrange short meetings.”

“And if I say something foolish?”

The administrator met his eyes cautiously.

“I'll explain it to you.”

“In front of someone else too?”

He considered the question with a new attentiveness.

“If waiting would cause harm, yes.”

“Good.”

Albin did not smile. But he moved his pen, as though he had finally decided this conversation was more than a test of a convalescent's faculties.

Alexander could have made him more confident with a word. He found he preferred the slight discomfort of still being judged.

In the duke's memory, that answer would have stung a pride still young. Edran found it restful.

He asked about the household documents next. Albin immediately distinguished the estate registers from the receipt of gifts: Maurice was better placed for those. He did not try to become competent at everything in order to preserve his position.

“Ask him to come when he's free. There's no need to interrupt the service.”

The folder remained open between them. Alexander noticed a minute correction in the margin, a figure rewritten by a tired hand.

“You dealt with all this during the crisis.”

“The parts that fall to me.”

“It's still a great deal.”

Albin ran his thumb along the fold of the case. For the first time he had no answer ready.

Albin's silence lasted long enough for the clock's ticking to come to the foreground. Edran watched the administrator's knuckles, still on the folder. The boy whose memories he possessed might have sought a polite phrase to end the awkwardness. He found it more useful to give it some space.

“You were accustomed to working with my father.”

“Yes.”

“And now you have to explain everything to me again.”

“Not everything. Some things you remember better than you think.”

Edran did remember. Phrases heard without interest returned to him, the location of a folder, Lucien's voice when a decision could have seemed easier if a name were omitted. He had not lost Alexander's education. He had to choose what to do with it now that the house no longer expected a son standing behind the duke.

Albin reopened the statement listing commitments. He showed how they were distinguished from payments already made. Alexander let him finish explaining. Halfway through, he caught himself wanting to interrupt to prove he already knew how to read that part; precisely the sort of gesture that forced those working with a powerful man to guess how much more than him they were permitted to know.

Instead, he asked about a condensed entry. Albin explained it without offering a new expense to approve. The total remained the same. Their way of looking at it had changed: not a reserve of favors, but money someone would soon need for work already done.

“If you find me impatient,” Edran said, “that is no reason to skip an explanation.”

“I may find you impatient with many explanations.”

Alexander laughed. The administrator lifted his chin slightly, as though testing the strength of a support before putting more weight on it.

That caution brought an unexpected melancholy. He had known people who could contradict him without first looking at his hands. With some, he had shared hunger. He did not know what remained of them in the world, and would not force Albin into a dead person's place to spare himself the time of getting to know him.

“The statement is enough for today. We shall address the rest one thing at a time.”

Albin closed the road folder and left the wages folder open. He showed him where to find the deadline again. Edran looked at the number, then the hand indicating it. He had obtained something rare in an impossible awakening: a commitment that existed even when he stopped thinking about it.

Two voices came from the corridor. One belonged to Celine; the other was trying to convince her that waiting would be more appropriate. Alexander recognized Sabine Varel before seeing her at the door.

“You said I could ask,” Celine said.

“I did.”

Sabine carried a book with a sheet tucked between its pages. Celine reached to take it, then gave up, as though she had to prove before her brother and the administrator that she had not come in for something too small.

Edran remembered the weight of an audience. People with real hunger had waited for him, alongside others ready to call a desire for power hunger. He had needed to learn the difference; sometimes, instead, he had merely learned to make them all wait alike.

Albin rose and moved his chair to clear the way. He did not retreat under a pretence of secrecy nobody had requested. The administrator's simple consideration prevented Edran from treating the girl as an interruption to manage.

“Is the book what you are looking for?” he asked.

“The one to take back. That's why I told Sabine we needed to go.”

Alexander looked at the slip between the pages. There must be a deadline, or perhaps simply a wish to choose something else. The library did not need to conceal a clue to justify the importance of the outing. His sister had asked for a piece of the day, and he could listen.

The girl glanced at the papers and stopped.

“I can come back.”

“You can also ask.”

She wanted to go to the library. Not the house library: the one in the town, associated in both their memories with a promise left unfulfilled. She had already arranged a time with Sabine, who had not yet approved the outing during such an uncertain convalescence.

Edran felt the impulse to get up and go with her. His body could cover the distance easily. The papers on the table were already irritating him. Then he noticed that Celine had not asked him to come.

“If Sabine can accompany you, that's all right.”

The governess nodded. Celine kept looking at him.

“What is it?”

“Nothing.”

“Would you prefer me to come too?”

She considered the offer long enough for her answer not to be mere politeness.

“Another time. I need to look for something.”

“Then good luck with it.”

Celine gave him a small, surprised smile and left with Sabine. Their conversation resumed almost immediately in the corridor. They were no longer talking about him.

Albin arranged the papers without comment. Edran was grateful.

“Has Yselle left any news?”

“She asks for an update, not a promise about the project.”

Alexander remembered his aunt with a technical drawing before her, the boy's hand pushing it aside to reach a letter he considered more urgent. The shame of the memory did not entirely belong to Edran. The rudeness, if he repeated it, would be his.

“Tell her I'm conscious and would be pleased to see her. Without making her travel here only to find me asleep.”

Albin noted that too.

Alone, Edran turned the statement over. The back was blank. For an instant he sought the other face of a far older tablet, crowded with names no accountant could have read. His five Effigies still answered him. Yet Albin, looking at him, had found a young man who needed to learn to have wages paid on time.

He turned the sheet back. The twenty-sixth was eight days away. In this household's calendar, Alexander's father would never have another appointment.

Edran marked the wages. Then he called Maurice: he wanted the letter to Yselle actually sent.

Chapter 8 — The Weight of the Toolbox

The step gave way while both Gaston Mery's hands were full.

Elias saw the change of angle first: the toolbox lifted from his right palm, turning toward the railing of the service stairs. Below lay the courtyard, where a woman was dragging a basket of laundry.

“Stop!”

Gaston tried to recover his grip. The box slipped.

Elias reached it with his power while it was still close. Its weight yielded to his will with a familiar resistance, like a lever suddenly meeting a lighter load. It did not stop falling. Its edge struck the railing, and that lighter impact gave him time to catch it.

The handle tore the skin between two fingers.

He held on.

Gaston clung to the upright. The woman below was looking at them, motionless beside her basket. A screwdriver had fallen separately and was still spinning on the paving stones.

“Don't move,” Elias said.

“I'd gathered.”

Gaston did not sound angry with him. He sounded angry with the wood.

The handle pressed against the wound. Elias could not change his grip without letting the bottom swing beyond the railing again. The iron had become lighter, but the toolbox retained its cumbersome shape, and his wrist could find no angle at which to stop trembling.

“Can I pass it to you?”

Gaston looked first at the object, then at the wood beneath his feet. One shoe rested on the sound edge of the step; the other was still searching for the end of the broken section.

“Wait.”

Elias listened. It was the hardest effort of those few seconds. He had done the impossible; now someone was asking him not to do the next thing at once.

The woman in the courtyard gathered her basket and pulled it beneath the covered section. She did not run away screaming. She stared upwards with a fixity that made him understand where the load would fall if his hand gave way. Elias kept his elbow close to his body. He felt the railing's wood against his side and a sudden strip of sweat beneath his shirt.

A tool shifted inside the box with a small metallic sound. Gaston stopped searching for the lower step and gripped the upright in both hands. The stairs creaked beneath the new distribution of weight.

“Don't push toward me,” Elias said.

“I'm trying not to end up on the other side.”

The young man nodded. He could not offer the man's body the same rescue he was offering the metal. The difference was present, tangible as the gap between two rungs, and for once he had no time to hate it.

He called briefly toward the door. After that, he saved his breath for maintaining the ability. The courtyard had not stopped with them: a window opened, someone asked what had fallen, a cart continued beyond the wall. The world kept producing sounds that could not help him hold on.

When he heard Marcel approaching, Elias felt gratitude so strong that he almost lost concentration. He did not need another miracle. He needed someone to look at the stairs from the place toward which he could not turn.

Marcel came out of the workshop. He brought Elias down first, then Gaston, indicating where to put their weight. No one tried to save the step.

When the toolbox touched the floor, Elias released the working. Its full weight returned at once. It almost felt like a rebuke.

Only then did he notice he was breathing too fast.

“Your hand,” Marcel said.

“It's nothing.”

“Your hand.”

The cut was small. It hurt a great deal for its size. Elias washed it while Gaston explained that the wood had looked sound the day before, that the toolbox was not heavy enough to justify a break, that he had used those stairs a hundred times.

Marcel asked him to stop talking long enough to check his ankle. Gaston protested, then allowed the dust to be brushed from his shoe. There was no obvious injury. He stayed seated anyway.

Elias looked up at the step. Its upper surface had been dry; the fracture exposed dark wood beneath the edge. No malicious hand was needed to make a passage used every day dangerous.

“Had we reported it?” he asked.

“The handrail,” Marcel answered. “Now we'll have the entire stairway checked.”

Elias sought where sound wood became breakage. Near Coordinates could give him accessible geometry; the step, though, had held until the previous footfall. The cut in his hand throbbed as he tried to press. Marcel told him to leave the railing and looked at the stairs from below.

He dried his hand on the clean cloth.

“That's enough for today,” Marcel said.

He closed access to the stairs. The woman retrieved the screwdriver and set it on the toolbox without smiling.

“That could have hit my head.”

Gaston thanked her for stopping, then apologized for the box. He did not explain how unlikely the accident had been.

The woman did not leave at once. She had picked up her basket but was watching the screwdriver as though still expecting it to fall. Elias searched for a suitable remark and found only an explanation about probabilities that he was ashamed even to have thought of.

“I saw you were underneath,” he said.

“I was underneath, too.”

She did not say it cruelly. It helped him stop preparing a version of his gesture that made everything less unpleasant. She settled the basket against her hip and asked which way she could return without using the stairs. Marcel indicated the ordinary route. She left with one more look toward the parapet.

Elias put his hands in the basin's water. The first pressure on the cut produced a hot sting, sharper than he had expected. Instinctively, he brought his fingers toward his mouth, then stopped before touching the dirty skin.

Gaston had sat on the lowest stool. He was looking at his boot. When he tried to stand again, Marcel stopped him with a question about the pain rather than an absolute order to remain still.

“I haven't broken anything.”

“I'll know after you've answered.”

Gaston described what he felt without making it a diagnosis. The master checked that he could put his shoe down, then let him decide whether to remain seated a little longer. Elias wanted to laugh at the tone. It was exactly the one Marcel used when asking whether a dimension had truly been checked.

The workshop was the same as that morning: the file, the glass on the shelf, the light in the screw box. Elias found it absurd that nearly losing his grip could make it different. He had gained no ability. He had discovered how narrow the time was in which to choose the one he possessed.

He rubbed his sound hand over his mouth. Gaston was watching him.

“You don't have to stand to make me believe everything's all right.”

Elias sat beside the basin. His knees trembled much less now that he was no longer trying to use them.

Marcel went to block the access with a visible obstacle. The sound of wood being dragged made both turn. When he returned, he looked as ordinary as a man who would carry on working once he had made the passage safe.

“Next time, one person handles the box at a time,” Gaston said.

The master nodded. He did not promise this would prevent every accident. Elias looked at the lid's fastening and understood why it would help nonetheless. A solution could be worth the work of putting it into practice without deserving to be called salvation.

The small cut had barely stopped bleeding. Elias kept the cloth in place and asked where the clean gauze was.

Elias was searching for gauze when Gaston noticed the warehouse drawing open on the bench.

“Still working on this?”

“Do you have better news?” Marcel asked.

“Confirmation of the inspection. Tomorrow morning.”

Elias turned too quickly and caught the edge of the basin.

Gaston took the sheet from his case. The request was addressed to Marcel, as the person responsible for the examination. Etienne could indicate his place of work. Elias would describe what he had done before the test, without conducting a new appraisal.

“It doesn't change your permit,” Marcel said.

“I know.”

“Tomorrow I don't want to have to remind you in front of someone.”

Elias nodded. The gauze was sticking to his wet skin. Gaston handed him the loose end so he could wrap it without dirtying it again.

For a while they discussed the route and the available records. Gaston kept looking at the apprentice's hands.

“How did you do that earlier?”

“Catch the toolbox?”

“Make it fall like that.”

Elias hesitated. On the stairs the miracle had felt large. Beside the basin it became small again: an object made lighter long enough to catch, a hand that had been cut anyway.

“I changed its weight. Not enough to make it float.”

“Could you have stopped me?”

“Not like that. Not at that moment.”

Gaston sat down. Dust stained one knee, and he wore the expression of a man finally understanding a fall that had not happened.

“Better to know.”

He took the screwdriver the woman had returned and slid it into its place. One hand was trembling slightly.

“I thought becoming a Bearer made these things simpler.”

Elias thought of his working on the stairs: the nearby target, attention gathered in time, the body that still had to catch the handle.

“Some of them.”

“Not all.”

“No.”

Gaston nodded. He did not ask why Elias lacked the power to heal his own hand as well. He passed him the gauze, and that ordinary gesture did not seem a lesser solution.

Marcel picked up the toolbox and checked its clasp. Elias waited for his heart to slow. He did not feel the shining satisfaction he had imagined when he acquired Astrolabe. He felt alive with unpleasant precision.

Gaston looked back at the drawing.

“The second set of readings was taken after the move,” he said.

Elias raised his head.

“You know that?”

“I know which sheets reached me. Not who carried the bench or what the men were told.”

“Why didn't—”

“Elias,” Marcel said.

He looked at the toolbox. He had just seen how easily an explanation could become a defense before anyone even asked for one.

“Will you help us distinguish the two sets tomorrow?”

“That's why I came.”

Before leaving, Gaston wanted to know which door to take the box through. Marcel showed him the longer way round. The man took the handle, felt its full weight, then let go to put two tools in a separate bundle.

Elias offered to carry it. Gaston looked at the gauze on the young man's hand and entrusted him only with the light bundle.

“This won't open between your fingers.”

They could have argued about who had the greater right to pretend the accident did not concern him. Instead, they used the ordinary door, one after the other. The basket was gone from the courtyard. A damp streak remained on the paving where a cloth had been dragged hastily away.

Elias accompanied Gaston to where work could continue without using the stairs. Returning, he looked up. From the ground, the gap did not seem large enough to have contained all that fear.

At the bench, he needed two attempts to pick up his pencil without pressing on the cut. He stopped, repositioned his hand, and wrote down the inspection time. He would not forget that pain. He hoped he could remember just as precisely who had needed the passage kept clear.

Before returning to the bench, Elias looked at the recovered screwdriver. When he had lightened the toolbox, the tool had kept falling. With his eyes closed he could still reconstruct the distance between its point and the woman in the courtyard.

In his notebook, below the inspection time, he added: second series after the move.

Gaston had said it in passing. Elias could no longer read it as a passing remark.

Chapter 9 — The Note with the Gift

Marta Sorel would not sit until she had put down the box.

“The papers are inside. Not the bottles.”

Alexander indicated the clear space on the table. Maurice Verr followed her with a register under his arm. Neither looked as though they were facing a patient's recovery. They looked like employees summoned to explain a mistake.

“I haven't asked you here to accuse you,” he said.

Marta drew her hand back from the box.

“I'd prefer to know what you want checked.”

He nodded. A general reassurance had not helped her.

“A small vial of cordial. It was on the tray on the fourteenth. Mirel kept it.”

The woman had carried the box against her body, and its edge had left an impression on her sleeve. Edran saw her conceal it with a hand over the fabric. The calm he had prepared was of no use to her yet.

“Marta, sit down.”

“I would rather stay like this until I open it.”

He did not insist. Maurice moved a chair without offering it a second time. They had worked together long enough to spare themselves a conversation about the small refusal.

In Alexander's memories, Marta often appeared at the end of conversations the boy considered finished. She brought practical information, corrected a guest count, remembered something nobody had taken responsibility for telling her. Her voice lingered more easily than her face. Edran recognized it as she asked which exact day she should check.

He repeated the date. Marta repeated it in turn, without adding that it had been a difficult day for everyone. The duke had just returned from a death the others called illness; the household could have attributed any previous disorder to that circumstance.

“Have you already had to look for these papers?”

“I checked that they were there.”

“On someone's orders?”

The question made her stop.

Edran understood his mistake before Maurice had to intervene. He had spoken as though preserving a document during a crisis required an instigator to identify. For her, it was her job.

“I did not mean that you did anything wrong,” he said. “Mirel asked me to preserve what might help us understand the illness. I should like to know what you saw yourself.”

Marta looked at Maurice; he did not answer for her. Then she removed the lid and showed the divisions between days. The arrangement was not elegant. It had the simplicity of something another person needed to find without waiting for whoever had put it away to return.

Edran thought of how often he had looked at such order and seen only obedience. It took will, hours, and some faith that someone would need the work even after one's absence.

“Thank you for keeping them together.”

“I didn't keep them all together. The pantry papers are separate.”

“Then thank you for separating them.”

Marta looked at him to see whether he was mocking her. Edran let her see a smile, and her hand finally stopped covering the mark on her sleeve.

Maurice opened the register. Marta waited for him to find the week, then took a thin file from the box.

The gift had arrived on May twelfth. The receipt recorded its delivery to the household. A courtesy note preserved the sender's name. The vial had been separated from its wrapping before being served.

Edran read the name.

Marcel Ravelle.

Alexander's memory came with a drawing room, an older man fond of speaking about continuity, and his son Etienne waiting to go out so he would not have to hear another piece of advice.

An acquaintance. A baron. A friend's father.

The handwriting was familiar. Alexander had seen it on invitations, replies, courtesies that needed no close reading. Edran lingered on that very familiarity. It was what had allowed the object to reach this room without seeming something that needed examining.

He did not yet know whether the sender had betrayed that trust or someone along the route had exploited it. He knew how little it sometimes took to confuse recognizing a signature with knowing the person who had written it.

Marta waited. He realized he had let the silence last long enough for her to clasp her hands again.

“Go on,” he said, moving the card away from the part of the table she was watching.

“Is that it?” Maurice asked.

“The description of the gift matches.”

“Were there others?”

The question was for Marta. She indicated two different entries: common supplies, a delivery to the pantry, quite another destination. She did not try to fill the silence with a person to suspect.

Alexander asked her to describe the route. She did, stopping where her own observations ended. Maurice added what the receiving records showed. Ordinary gaps remained between their accounts, handovers by people who might or might not remember a small vial among many objects.

Edran recognized the impulse to make everything immediately clear. Imbued words would suffice, questions leaving no avenue of escape. He could do it. The power did not order him to begin with the two innocent people unfortunate enough to have kept the papers.

“These documents stay together,” he said. “Mirel can compare them with the sample.”

Marta stared at the card.

“Was the content bad?”

“The crisis was toxic. We don't yet know enough to say who altered anything.”

Maurice straightened.

“Should I stop the receipt of gifts?”

“Keep them separate from anything served until we've established a procedure. Don't have the staff taste anything to check it.”

Marta looked at him with sudden hardness.

“I wouldn't have.”

“Good.”

The answer seemed to surprise her more than a reprimand would have. She closed the box, leaving out the file he had requested.

Marta closed the lid with one hand and sought the lower grip with the other. The box was awkward to lift; Alexander moved the chair so that she could pass without turning sideways.

“The file you need stays here,” she said. “I'll put the rest back where it belongs.”

“If they ask why it is missing?”

“I'll say where it is.”

There was nothing servile in the answer. It was the assurance of someone who knew how to make her work traceable. Edran held open the part of the door Maurice had left ajar. Marta lowered her head as she passed, but the box did not strike the wood.

The butler stopped before following her. He wanted to know whether the duke needed anything from the household staff. The question might encompass a meal, fresh water, something to be moved. Edran also heard an attempt to learn whether the household's habits still had somewhere to function.

“Nothing for now. I shall call if I need you.”

Maurice nodded. The gesture seemed almost identical to one preserved in dozens of memories; only his fingers, tight around the register, remained stiffer. Edran let him leave without asking for a demonstration of trust.

Alone, Edran read the card again. The name was not a confession. A gift could have been altered before delivery or afterward. Knowing the first point in the chain did not mean he had already reached the person responsible.

In the boy's memories there was warm trust toward Etienne. No particular love for his father, but no reason to look for poison behind every courtesy either.

He rang the bell. When Maurice returned, he asked him to tell Teren the papers must be preserved and the questions kept focused.

“No arrests.”

Maurice asked whether he wished to speak to the officer directly. Alexander agreed. Teren arrived shortly afterward, listened, and asked which arrangements for access should change.

“Preservation first. And the staff must know a question isn't an accusation.”

“If someone asks why we're keeping those papers?”

“To reconstruct a delivery during my illness.”

Teren asked how he should deal with anyone who remembered something different from the register's entry. Edran looked at the note on the table. Its presence was seductive: a readable name, an origin, the beginning of an explanation.

“Let them describe it. Do not correct them before they finish.”

“We may have many versions.”

“We have them already. We do not know them.”

The officer nodded. It was an answer he could put to work; it did not require him to promise that the household would stop being afraid.

Edran noticed the branch still tapping the glass. The sound irritated him. It reminded him how much time he was allowing a series of questions when he could have forced every person to surrender their answers.

The adulterated bottle, the bed, Celine in the next room. Edran arranged those three thoughts as he would targets. The fourth eluded him: the hand that prepared the drink. Until he found it, he had a house full of people within reach of his anger.

“Do you want a guard at every entrance?”

Teren had phrased the question cautiously. The duke need only say yes for people who had worked here for years to find someone watching as they carried a plate.

“I want gifts kept from the household service until they are distinguished from the other deliveries. For the rest, explain what you are changing and why.”

The officer described the task in terms the household could carry out. He did not promise to see through packaging or recognize poison through suspicion alone. He would make sure people knew whom to approach when something did not match the expected delivery.

Maurice listened in silence. Asked whether it was practicable, he pointed out a passage whose phrasing confused those who received packages with those who distributed them. Teren corrected it without defending his words merely because he had spoken them in uniform.

Edran let them finish. He noticed that one hand had tightened on the edge of the table and opened it. The wood was intact. Possessing destruction sometimes made it necessary to remember that he could simply stop gripping.

“If someone asks what I said about him?”

Maurice seemed surprised by the question.

“That you named no names.”

“That is true. Let us start with that.”

Teren looked at the box without touching it.

“That's precise enough.”

He did not ask to parade the staff before the duke one by one. Edran understood that the officer had already had to defend someone from confusion between an order and a suspicion. He did not know the story and did not try to extract it while they discussed something else.

When Teren left, Maurice paused before returning to household matters. The next piece of news no longer seemed an innocent note.

The butler nodded.

“Etienne Ravelle asked for news this morning. We told him you were awake, without clinical details.”

Edran looked again at the name on the table.

“If he'd like to come, I can see him tomorrow morning.”

Maurice repeated the young Ravelle's name to confirm the invitation. Edran recognized the surname twice: on the butler's lips and on the gift's note still before him.

“The twentieth. Write that I wish to see him.”

When Maurice left, the branch tapped the glass again. Edran opened the window and moved it. In the duke's memories, Etienne flung himself forward to embrace him, without a reason to suspect his own father.

The note retained its perfect courtesy. Edran put it where he could find it before receiving the father.

Chapter 10 — The Ordinary Door

Edith Torrel looked at the key in Marcel's hand.

“You told me about a special room.”

“Not the lock,” Elias said.

Livia glanced at him, and he abandoned the second joke.

They had arrived separately. The warehouse still smelled of packaging, rope, and rain brought in on shoes. Marcel had checked the route. No one had summoned them across a city or torn them from another place. Edith had come because Livia proposed a limited discussion of Etienne's documents.

Marcel opened the door.

The antechamber held a different coolness from the warehouse. Beyond it, the dark walnut walls seemed to absorb their first words. An oval table occupied the center of the hall, long enough to make a chair on the far side seem distant. Old brass fittings reflected subdued light.

Edith stopped.

“Do you really intend to discuss my shifts in here?”

“We can do it outside too.”

“I was asking about the taste in furniture.”

Elias smiled. He realized he had prepared to defend the place against an insult that had never come.

Marcel showed her the ordinary door and the way back to the warehouse. The prepared connection remained closed. They did not spend one of the two remaining openings to save a few steps.

The hall was too large for the number of their voices. Elias had grown used to it alone, but Edith's first hesitation restored the scale of the place: the section of wall left dark, the distance between table and door, the chairs nobody had yet pulled out.

The nurse looked at the brass fittings without approaching.

“If I touch something, will I set a machine going?”

Marcel explained which parts could be left alone and where the clear route lay. He invented no story about the table's origins to make it more interesting. The room had a material past they knew only partly; they need only avoid confusing it with their own project.

Edith walked a few steps along the table. Her bag nudged a chair and she brought it round in front of her hip.

“Can people outside hear what we say in here?”

Elias opened his mouth. Marcel deprived him of an overconfident answer simply by indicating the still-open door. They could hear rope being dragged across the warehouse floor. Livia stood near the threshold and spoke a sentence in her normal voice; Elias stayed where he was, trying to distinguish every word.

They did not turn it into a test of perfect privacy. They merely realized how unserious it was to promise secrecy because a room looked fine enough to deserve it.

Edith let a cart pass in the noise of the street before asking again:

“What if I'm left behind?”

“The door is there. It doesn't only open for whoever brought us in,” Elias replied.

“I'm not coming here to have every minute counted,” Edith said.

Elias already had an answer ready. He saw her check the clock and stopped.

“How long do you have tonight?”

She told him. Livia wrote the time at the top of the sheet, large enough for Marcel to read from his place. Edith finally set down her bag. The table began to look less like something Elias had to fill and more like something the others could get up from.

“If we need to finish at a particular time, we'll say so beforehand,” he added.

Edith checked the clock. She told him how much time she had that evening. Elias did not round it up to gain a little more attention. He noticed Marcel watching him and was grateful the man had not offered the small nod of approval he would have found natural in a workshop.

Edith ran a hand along a chair's back, then chose one near the side she had entered from. Her nursing bag sat at her feet, its handle turned toward her.

Elias sat within conversational distance. Twelve chairs did not mean twelve people hidden behind a project. At that moment there were only four of them, and one had not decided whether she wanted to return.

Edith noticed the sheet on which Livia had written Footnote for an earlier trial.

“Am I supposed to call you that?”

“Only if we decide to use our meeting names.”

“And yours?” she asked Elias.

“Northbound.”

The nurse looked at him, then at the door.

“You know you could be followed here even if you change your name?”

“Yes.”

Elias still felt the steps taken to arrive: the alley, the warehouse door, Marcel's key. Things no mask would erase. Livia left their two pseudonyms on the table; Edith looked past them, straight at the door through which she would leave.

“One thing first,” Edith said. “What has Etienne authorized you to ask for?”

Livia set down a copy of the offer and the request for its attachment.

“A comparison concerning these. Not his whole medical file.”

Elias had already opened his notebook to the page where he had written examination. He closed it.

Edith saw.

“You can ask. I can say no too.”

“I'd like to know whether he can manage the waiting tomorrow.”

“I can discuss that with him. A healed hand doesn't take away his right to be tired.”

Marcel sat without choosing the head of the table. For a few moments the four listened to a distant noise from the warehouse. The size of the hall did not isolate them from the world.

Livia explained what they hoped to build. Elias added the services they lacked: a check, a competent contact, someone able to say when a problem had been framed badly. Edith let them finish.

“Who pays?”

“The person asking for the work, when we agree on a fee,” Elias said.

“And when they can't?”

Edith opened her bag to check the time on her small watch, then closed it without offering its contents to the table. Elias followed the gesture and realized how easily he had imagined a skill without imagining a day: visits, journeys back, a meal to find between shifts.

“We won't ask you to bring the clinic's medicines here,” he said.

She stared at him.

“Good. They aren't mine.”

The sentence took an ease out of the discussion he could not have named. If the group wanted to help someone, it first needed to understand what its guests had the right to offer.

He looked at Livia. They had no answer to sell as an established rule.

“Then someone must choose to bear the cost,” she said. “We can't promise other people's time.”

Edith nodded slowly.

“I like that sentence more than the table.”

“Let's take an example,” Edith said.

Elias set down the pen. This was the moment he had awaited: a concrete request, finally something to organize rather than another limit to list.

“Someone is ill. I can come, but I can't leave my shift. What do you tell them?”

“That we'll find someone else.”

“Who?”

He had no name to give her. Acquaintances of acquaintances came to mind, half-heard rumours, the promise that it would be easier when there were more of them. The future could not replace a person available that evening.

“If we can't find anyone, we can't announce that the problem is solved.”

Edith nodded. Livia asked who was authorized to know the sick person's name. The conversation became less brilliant and more useful. Elias stopped recording only answers. He also wrote down the questions they had failed to answer.

Marcel took the jug and filled a glass. The sound of water restored a detail he had forgotten: they had been sitting for some time without offering their guest anything.

Elias pushed the glass toward Edith. She took it with thanks, without treating it as proof of an alliance.

“And what would you want from me, besides understanding Etienne's case better?”

The young man looked at Livia. They had discussed information that could not easily be bought, skills a person did not possess merely by becoming a Bearer. Now he had to explain that desire without making the nurse sound like the missing part of a machine they had built for themselves.

“I'd like to be able to ask a question of someone who has the right to tell me I'm asking the wrong thing.”

“I'm already doing that.”

“Yes.”

They laughed. The table grew no shorter, but Elias stopped measuring all the distances between empty places as though they were a deficiency to remedy before evening ended.

Livia moved the sheet bearing the pseudonym. She put it beneath her papers, leaving on top a page on which any of the four could write. Edith asked whether they would keep an account. The copyist replied that first they must agree what to preserve and whom to show it to.

“Even when someone says something foolish?”

“Especially then, I'd want to know whether they're trying something out or making a commitment,” Livia said.

Elias thought how much he would hate rereading some of his remarks that evening. Even so, he left the pen in the middle of the table. If he wanted somewhere to learn from others, he would have to bear the possibility of finding his starting point in writing.

She asked who owned the keys, who could enter, and what stopped a participant from reporting everything elsewhere. None of the answers was an unbreakable oath. Elias felt the hall's allure diminish with every limit they listed. At the same time, he saw Edith relax her shoulders.

When she asked to see the exit a second time, he stood and went with her. They crossed the antechamber, reached the warehouse, came back. The door opened both ways with a real handle.

“You aren't missing an opportunity,” Edith said as they returned.

“What do you mean?”

“You look as though you'd like to show me something more spectacular.”

Elias looked at the marks he had not activated.

“A little.”

“For tonight, knowing how to leave is enough.”

At the table, Edith agreed to help Etienne distinguish a useful check from a visit requested only for the dispute. She did not join the circle. She did not promise every evening she had.

Edith shouldered her bag again. Its handle had marked her skirt where she had held it throughout the conversation. Elias rose too soon to accompany her and struck a chair; he put it right without making a joke of the noise.

Livia gathered her pages. She did not pass their guest a copy to make something not yet agreed look concluded. She merely reminded her of the channel through which they would arrange their next relevant discussion.

“If my shift changes, I'll let you know,” Edith said.

Elias nodded. He wanted to say the hall would always be available, but it was not in his sole keeping, and Marcel had a life long enough not to deserve that sort of promise made on his behalf.

They headed toward the antechamber. The chairs left in place no longer looked like missing guests. They were furniture: they would wait without judging how long it took to find people.

The warehouse light seemed brighter on their return. Edith slowed to let Livia pass with her folder, then pointed out a badly placed crate beside the door. Elias saw the obstruction. That, at least, was a question he could answer with his hands.

Marcel locked the door when they left. Elias waited for the click, then helped move a crate obstructing the way.

Before leaving, Elias turned toward the brass fittings. Livia waited on the threshold.

“Are you still hoping the person behind the drawing can replenish it?” she asked.

“I hope someone can.”

“That isn't the same question.”

Marcel called from the warehouse. Elias put out the light before joining him. In the darkness, the twelve chairs disappeared long before the table.

Chapter 11 — Marks on the Floor

The warehouse seemed smaller without the accident.

Elias noticed as soon as he entered. The crates had been rearranged in new rows, and a man with a broom walked through the place where he remembered a crowd. Light came through the same windows. It had no right to seem so ordinary.

At the threshold, Elias had stopped a moment too long. Gaston passed him with the folder, then turned without calling. That silent wait allowed him to enter by choice rather than follow someone.

As soon as he set foot inside, Elias sought the accident's location. A crate he did not remember occupied it. He moved it in his mind, superimposing the room he had reconstructed every night on the present one. The two images refused to fit.

The man responsible for access asked whether he needed a chair. The kindness brought blood to his face. He would have preferred a hostile remark he could answer with his prepared evidence.

“Not yet. Thank you.”

The man indicated where they could set their folders without obstructing work. Elias carried his notebook there and opened it at the warehouse plan. He knew it so well he no longer needed to look; for that very reason, finding the right page took him a few moments.

Etienne came beside him. The cap between his hands did not conceal his arm. He looked around and drew a longer breath than necessary.

“I didn't think they'd changed so much.”

Elias searched for something to confirm that, then realized he did not know which crates had already been moved before the test. He recognized his drawing. Not every day of the warehouse's life.

“We can mark the ones we remember for certain.”

Etienne nodded. The cautious plural did not seem to disappoint him. He only asked for another moment to look.

A man passed with a crate on a trolley and asked them to make room. Elias moved at once, then felt a flare of anger. Did they really still have to be careful not to disturb anyone here?

The wheels stopped against the toe of his shoe. Elias drew back his foot and the trolley passed. The crate brushed his jacket; he felt air move against the sleeve and found again the point where Etienne had worked. “Here,” he said, before forgetting which relationship he was looking at.

Marcel asked him to indicate where he had stood at the beginning of the work.

Elias went to a pale stone in the floor.

“Here.”

He did not activate Astrolabe. That day he was the witness to an earlier measurement; the examination belonged to Marcel. The distinction weighed on him, but it did not prevent him from remembering.

Etienne stayed near a pillar. Gaston opened the file on a clean crate. The person responsible for admitting them accompanied them to the bench, then stood far enough away to let them work, not far enough to stop listening.

A workman asked whether he should stay. Marcel said they would only ask about what he personally remembered, if it proved relevant. He did not order him to leave his work to attend a demonstration. The man returned to his crates.

Elias watched him go and understood that he was looking for a face able to confirm his own account. It was not very different from searching for a favorable number without looking at the rest of the table.

“Do you remember the light?” Etienne asked.

He looked at the windows.

“Yes.”

“So do I. Some of the rest comes back better than other parts.”

Elias nodded. The memory of the accident was not a shared recording from which each of them could retrieve the same image.

The portable bench was still there.

Elias felt the same absurd anger he had felt at his own clean shirt: things allowed themselves to be put right too easily. The metal's surface did not preserve the moment someone had screamed.

Marcel set two fingers on the edge and closed his eyes. When he opened them, he indicated a point for Gaston to mark.

He had not read the past. He had grasped the present space with a precision a measuring rule would take longer to verify.

Gaston recorded the first dimension. Then the second.

Marcel had him check the rule against the same reference points too. The man overseeing their access came close enough to follow the reading.

“With your power, could you show us anything you wanted?” he asked.

“I could be mistaken myself,” Marcel said. “That's one reason to repeat the comparison.”

The man seemed to find the answer less impressive than he had hoped. Elias suddenly found it important. His mentor's faculty gave him access to a geometry. It did not oblige others to believe him because he had closed his eyes.

The measuring tape bent near the first mark. Gaston straightened it and asked the access supervisor to hold the far end steady. Elias saw the man seek the exact point on the edge, then let it slip to make room for the master's hand.

“Stay there,” Marcel said. “I need to read it from your position.”

There was nothing supernatural about the correction. Even someone who had never heard of Astrolabe could hold the end of a tape well or badly. Once it was stable, Marcel had the supervisor read the value before repeating it to Gaston.

Elias followed the distance on his plan. The section matched, but the sheet preserved the previous arrangement. He wanted to lift it beside the bench to show everyone the difference at once. He left it flat, where each person could see which reference he started from.

The supervisor asked whether the second reading should replace the first. Gaston showed him the two headings. For the first time, Elias understood that their conclusion must survive a question asked without bad faith. A correct number was insufficient if its presentation led a reasonable person to use it in the wrong place.

When the ordinary reading matched, the man moved to shade the sheet Gaston was writing on. It was the first time he had helped them without being asked.

“This isn't the position on my drawing,” Elias said.

“We can see that.”

Marcel's words took away his need to repeat it. Elias stepped back.

The problem was not establishing that the bench was different now. It was knowing when that difference had entered the work.

Etienne indicated the passage between two rows of crates.

“I came from that side. My arm didn't go behind it.”

The man overseeing them began to explain that the crates were no longer the same. Marcel asked him to preserve the observation without making it say more. Etienne had provided no coordinate, but a relationship between his work and the bench.

Marcel asked him to describe the route he had taken before the reading. Elias pointed to the bench, then the wall and the place from which he had checked the reference. Midway through, he heard his voice quicken. He was approaching the sentence he had repeated too often at home.

The master stopped him at a small detail: where he had rested his hand.

“Here.”

“On the edge or the plate?”

Elias hesitated. The memory was not a complete picture; he possessed the pressure of metal beneath his palm and the irritation of a cuff, but the object before him was turned differently.

“On the edge. The one facing the passage.”

Marcel had the distinction recorded. He did not ask him to repeat the power to prove he could still use it. Elias felt the relief only when the next question concerned the certificate.

The access supervisor wanted to see the reference on the plan. Elias turned the notebook toward him and let the man follow the line himself. It would have been easy to add that it was obvious. Instead he waited until the man reached the point.

“On this side, then.”

“In that position of the bench.”

Hammering came from the next room. The noise made him tense the back of his wounded hand. For an instant he recovered the sound of the accident; when the hammering continued in a regular rhythm, he managed to separate it from the memory.

Etienne asked whether the gauze needed changing. Elias said no. He wanted to apologize for being there with a tiny cut when Etienne had had to reconstruct whole weeks of his life. He realized in time that this would also ask the worker to reassure him about that.

He looked back at the plan. The values did not need him to suffer enough to be correct. He had to show them without burdening them with everything he hoped to obtain.

Gaston knelt before two mounting marks. Other traces lay beside them, older, one filled with dust.

“We can't put a date on every hole,” he said.

Elias crouched. How convenient it would have been to find a fresh wound in the stone and call it April twenty-first.

He only drew the marks.

The two sets of readings Gaston had kept had different headings and reference points. The first matched the arrangement described in Livia's copy; the second took the bench after the move. No magical transformation of the paper was necessary to see it, now they had chosen the right thing to compare.

“This value isn't wrong,” Marcel said, touching the first set.

Elias felt his throat tighten.

“I know.”

“And it doesn't become wrong because the second one is different.”

The man responsible for their access came closer.

“There's only one certificate.”

“That's precisely what we need to clarify,” Gaston replied.

Marcel had the tape laid along the first reference, then the second. Elias followed its end. The second reading was correct. He bent over his notebook, returned to the first, searched once more for a subtraction error.

“It can't be here and there at the same time,” he said.

“The bench can't,” Marcel replied.

Gaston ran a finger over the dates. Elias watched both lines until the numbers stopped seeming competing versions of one truth. There was a move between them. Somewhere, someone had gone on using the first sheet after making it.

Etienne waited until they had finished, then asked whether he could sit. Elias brought him a stool without making a display of generosity.

“I remembered the passage being narrow,” the workman said. “Not how narrow.”

“That's enough for what you're saying.”

Etienne looked at him with a relief Elias had not deserved until that moment.

Gaston brushed dust from the notebook's edge with the back of his hand. A corner of a page had folded beneath the folder, and Elias straightened it too quickly, almost tearing it. He left it alone when he noticed the gesture.

They had worked in the room and were leaving with something more precise. The place had grown no less ordinary. Across the bench, the man with the broom waited to reclaim the patch where their feet still stood.

Elias signaled that they had finished. The man came through at once and pushed the dust toward the door. For the first time, Elias could watch that movement without feeling it erased what had happened.

The supervisor cleared the weights holding down the paper off the bench. Elias thanked him before closing his notebook. The man nodded and returned his gaze to the work that had waited for them.

When they collected the drawings, the bench remained in its present position. They did not move it back to construct a picture of a more convenient truth.

Outside, Marcel stopped to reread the notes. The sun had dried half the paving. Footprints remained in the other half.

“Now we need to speak to Vern,” Elias said.

Gaston closed the file.

“Yes. And let him answer what we ask.”

At the door Elias turned. The bench remained in the second position; in his notebook the certificate still referred to the first.

“Let's ask when he stopped waiting for calibration,” he said.

Marcel closed Gaston's folder before the wind lifted its edge. This time nobody told him to ask a different question.

Chapter 12 — A Day's Work

Vern Soller did not receive them from behind a desk.

He was dividing a job among three men, and finished before turning to Marcel. One of the three came back to ask him to repeat a measurement. Vern did so without irritation, pointing to the place where he did not want an estimate.

Elias found it unfair.

He would have preferred to discover an incompetent man, someone who got everything wrong and had never had the right to make decisions for others. The lot supervisor knew his work. That was a part of the problem Elias could not erase.

One of the men returned with a tool unsuited to the task. Vern sent him for another, then added an explanation rather than merely correcting him. Elias recognized the patience of someone who knew how much time was lost when an order was only half understood.

That patience irritated him more than the first brusque answer. He could not even promise himself that next time he would easily recognize a dangerous person.

Etienne was looking elsewhere. He did not seem occupied with constructing an image of Vern that would contain all his anger. He wanted something more precise. Perhaps that was why he had less need to hate him in every gesture.

“Are you finished?” Vern asked.

The lot supervisor waited for an answer that did not immediately come. Elias was looking at the three men behind him. Their presence made him want a sentence that would show at once who was wrong; he feared that hesitation before them would become another proof of incompetence.

Marcel passed him the folder without opening it. That small weight put his hands back to work.

“We want to compare the readings with the time the passage was cleared,” the master said.

Vern looked at the door. People uninvolved in the conversation were coming through, trying to work out whether they could pass.

“Come to the side.”

They moved far enough not to block the entrance. It was no concession of privacy, and Vern made no attempt to send Etienne away. The worker rested a palm against the wall, then removed it at once because of the dust. Elias saw him wipe it on his jacket and thought he would remember that gesture more easily than the entire opening sentence he had prepared.

Vern still had a tool in his hand. He put it on a crate to listen better. A small patch of sweat remained on the metal.

Vern called an instruction toward the continuing work. One man answered and left with the tool on the crate. Elias watched the quick, habitual movement and felt Etienne's wrist in his hand again. The same man knew how to keep the lot working and had chosen something that injured him.

He sought the beginning of the first series in the folder. Gaston helped by turning two pages, then left him room to speak.

Marcel said they had compared the two positions. Gaston waited, holding the closed file.

“The bench was moved,” Elias said.

Vern looked at him.

“I've never said it's stood still since they built it.”

“After my measurements.”

“We cleared a passage.”

The answer came so easily that Elias lost his next sentence.

Marcel gave him time to find it.

“The certificate stayed the same.”

Vern looked at Gaston's file.

“There were declared margins. And a day's worth of men already here.”

“Would recalibration have taken another day?” Marcel asked.

“It would have stopped the lot until you'd done it again.”

“You have my signature,” Elias said. “Not my presence after I left.”

Vern turned toward him. “I also have a crew paid to work. The margins were declared.”

“For that position.”

“Then show me the difference.”

Elias had the plan in his folder and an answer ready in his throat. But the warehouse was over there; pointing at a line from the corridor would show nobody the field. Marcel touched his elbow before his tone could do the evidence's work. Elias moved away, angry with him too.

Gaston opened the file.

“The second set is later. We can establish that without arguing over margins.”

Vern took the sheet. He held it far enough away to read comfortably. Elias watched his fingers on its edge and realized he was waiting for a tremor, a sign of fear that would grant him a simple satisfaction.

The man returned the page.

“Bring it to the hearing.”

“It will be there,” Marcel said.

Etienne, who had stayed near the door, spoke only then.

“My day's work wasn't recovered.”

Vern turned to him.

“I know you've lost work.”

“Then don't ask me to sign that everything's consistent with someone else's mistake.”

Etienne had approached without leaving the wall. He was not holding his arm in front of him. Elias looked briefly at his notebook, ashamed of how easily the worker's healed body became an argument whenever he wanted either to deny the harm or make it more visible.

“I've read those sentences,” Etienne told Vern. “If I sign, I have to stand by them outside this place too.”

The man tried to answer about the proposal's preparation. Etienne let him finish, then asked whether he agreed with its contents. The question lacked the elegance Elias would have sought. It had the weight of a person who would go home with his signature on the sheet.

Vern moved to take the document. Gaston handed it over, open at the right passage. For a few moments, only the sound of workers putting away tools remained. It was worse than a hostile audience: nobody seemed to know beforehand which side they would want to applaud.

Elias felt a sudden fear that Vern would find a sentence different from the one remembered, an explanation he would not know how to answer. He realized he was holding his breath and released it.

Marcel did not fill the silence. Nor did he offer a more accommodating version of the question to make the answer easier. He waited with Etienne.

The lot supervisor brushed the margin of the sheet. He seemed older than he had while assigning work. Elias forbade himself to make evidence of that too: a long morning could tire an innocent man, and knowing that would not erase what they were asking Vern.

The three men behind them had stopped talking. One lowered his eyes to the piece he was carrying. No one needed to understand every figure to know what was being discussed.

Vern ran a hand over the back of his neck.

“I didn't write every sentence in that offer.”

“But a sheet of paper didn't make the decision about the passage,” Elias said.

Vern looked back at him. Weariness had hardened his face.

“No.”

It was his shortest answer, and the hardest to forget.

Elias looked for Marcel's eyes. His mentor gave no gesture of victory. He let that no remain what it was: an answer about a real decision, not a confession to every imaginable responsibility.

Gaston recorded the words, then read them back so Vern could correct them. The man did not ask to change them. Instead he looked at the workmen waiting behind them, as though only now realizing that his way of making decisions had become something other people could observe.

“You can ask them to change it,” Etienne said.

The supervisor did not answer immediately. Then he said he would examine the request. It was less than a promise and more than he had said when they arrived.

Outside, Elias walked too quickly. Marcel caught up without calling him.

“He knew.”

“He spoke about margins.”

“He knew.”

“And you have to keep the words he said. Not the ones you hoped he'd say.”

Elias stopped. His injured hand throbbed beneath the gauze.

“It wasn't difficult to understand. If you change the reference point, the measurement doesn't apply to the new position.”

“No.”

“Why did he do it?”

Marcel looked toward the entrance.

“You can understand a rule and think that, just this once, the cost of following it is too high.”

Elias remembered yesterday's stairs, Gaston saying he had used them a hundred times. The resemblance irritated him. He did not want every error in the world to become an excuse for Vern's.

“That doesn't make him right again.”

“I wasn't trying to make him right.”

Etienne came out after them. He had spoken to a colleague and seemed less tired, although he had been the only one to ask to sit.

“Is the inspection over?” he asked.

Marcel nodded.

“Then I'd like something to eat before going back.”

Elias wanted to review every note immediately. He surprised himself by asking where.

They walked without speaking for a while. Gaston had closed the folder but kept his hand on it, as though wanting to feel whether the recorded words still had weight. Etienne stopped at a threshold to let a woman with two bundles pass, then resumed his place beside them.

Elias wanted to know whether he was pleased. He did not ask. Looking at him sideways, he understood how much there was to lose in that question: the worker would have to choose between protecting the boy's relief and explaining yet again that his wages had not arrived.

Marcel pointed out a place to eat. The room had few seats and the warm smell of something just taken from the fire. Etienne looked at what was available and chose without asking whether the others approved.

Elias realized he had felt no hunger until someone offered him the chance to think about it. Tension left hollows in the body as a day's work left marks on the hands.

Gaston sat carefully, mindful of the ankle they had already checked. The gesture reminded Elias of the stairs, the toolbox, the woman in the courtyard. He had almost turned that accident into a parable about Vern, forgetting how little elegance there had been in his efforts not to lose the handle.

“I couldn't say everything today,” he murmured.

Marcel put down his cap.

“You can prepare what's missing. You don't have to hold the hearing now as well.”

The answer did not free him from fear. It merely gave him back the rest of that morning. Elias sat and listened to Etienne ask about bread.

They went to a nearby shop. Etienne chose his bread, and when Elias tried to speak about the bench again, he told him instead something his wife had said the evening before. The story concerned how Etienne had tried to put something away at home without asking where it belonged. His wife had found it in the first place he had already checked, and now considered this her strongest argument in any discussion about tidiness.

“It might have been there from the beginning,” Gaston said.

“That's her position.”

“And yours?”

Etienne broke the bread with his healed hand.

“That she could have spared herself waiting until I'd searched everywhere.”

They laughed. Elias had heard similar stories many times at the workshop; that day, he found it surprising the worker still possessed one to tell. He himself had reduced Etienne in his mind to the man injured during the test.

When Etienne resumed eating, Elias left the notebook closed. He was learning nothing he could present to an office. He was finally ceasing to demand proof of his innocence from everything the other man said.

The shop kept filling with small sounds. A plate arrived at the next table, someone asked for water, the door admitted a cool gust. Elias managed to remain seated long enough to feel hunger return.

It was no use to the hearing. Elias listened to the end.

Chapter 13 — The Duke's Friend

Before Maurice announced the guest, Alexander stood near the window with an unreasonable wish that he had prepared his face better. He had memories of a friendship without the comfort of calling them his own without feeling the difference.

He did not leave the father's note on the table. He put it with the other documents, where nobody could mistake it for a question directed at whoever entered. If he wanted to know the baron, he must receive the baron. Etienne had come to see someone dear to him whom he had nearly lost.

He heard the young man's voice in the corridor. The tone was far more alive than the one preserved in memory: a greeting spoken a little too quickly, a shoe stopping just before striking the rug. Alexander let his prepared sentence go. He could not speak to a memory and call that imitation a meeting.

Etienne Ravelle entered with a cheerful remark that died on his face.

Alexander understood the problem at once. The young man had prepared to find a patient, perhaps to hide his fright behind a joke. Instead he found him standing, dressed, with the color of a man who had slept well.

“You can say it anyway,” Edran said.

“It was worse than I remembered.”

“Then something's improved during my recovery.”

Etienne laughed, relieved. He was twenty-one and occupied a room in a way Alexander's memories knew intimately: the gesture first, then the doubt that he had gone too far.

They embraced. Edran had time to think he could only have avoided it by making the visit different from what the young man had come to find.

The embrace was brief. The harder guilt had no need to last longer.

They went into the garden. Etienne spoke of the news he had received, a letter left unanswered, a concert he had missed because he did not know whether enjoying himself would seem indecent.

“You could have gone.”

“I know that now.”

Edran looked at the dead boy's friend. No one had assigned him a mourning period. Still he had tried to inhabit its boundaries with the same inexperience as the duke.

“And your father?” he asked.

Etienne raised an eyebrow.

“He sends you advice even when you don't ask. Why should I start?”

Edran smiled without answering. The remark was believable precisely because it was defending nothing.

They walked along a stretch of gravel. Edran remembered the steps of two younger boys, a foil put away badly, an argument over the score that had lasted longer than the lesson. Memory offered him a ready-made intimacy. Each time, he had to choose whether to use it.

Etienne asked whether they should go back inside. Edran said they could continue to the bench. The gravel sounded different where shade had preserved the night's moisture; his friend instinctively avoided the darker patch, then noticed Alexander watching.

“If your sister sees me dirty my shoes, she'll say I wasn't brought up properly.”

“She may say it anyway.”

The young man laughed. Edran remembered how easily Celine moved from this friendship to sharper teasing, secure enough not to ask adults' permission to want to speak. He felt relief at finding at least one relationship in the house that did not begin with his crisis.

Etienne asked after her. It was enough to know she was at home and had resumed her own activities. He did not ask to see her to prove that the Ravelle family still deserved access; he could not know how soon that thought would poison every visit.

Edran felt his body ready for anger before he had decided to feel it. He possessed no proof against the father. He had a name on a gift and a son who trusted him.

“Have you managed to take anything up again?” he asked instead.

“I'm trying to stop asking everyone how one ought to behave.”

“Is it working?”

“No. I've just asked you.”

Etienne laughed, then stopped before replacing his hat. “If I go and enjoy myself, someone will think I could have stopped worrying sooner.”

“Someone will think something if you stay home too.”

“Ducal consolation.”

“I grant it without a seal.”

His friend nudged him with an elbow. Edran sought the next joke and found the name on the gift's note. He let it die. Still smiling, Etienne bent to pick up a stone from the path.

“I've been told you want a position,” he said.

Etienne stopped.

“By whom?”

“We'd already discussed it.”

It was true. The old Alexander had listened without promising. The young man's embarrassment confirmed the memory.

“I didn't come for that.”

“We can still talk about it.”

Etienne picked up a flat stone and turned it between his fingers without throwing it.

“I'd like someone to read an application before reading the surname. Then I feel like an idiot, because I know perfectly well who paid for my education and introduces me to people.”

Edran did not offer him a place. He asked what work he actually wanted to do.

The answer came less readily than the joke at the door. Etienne spoke of public responsibilities, of being useful without waiting for a title that would go to his brother, of how easy it was to seem ambitious when you had nothing to lose yet.

He bent to pick up another stone, then left it where it was. Edran recognized the habit of occupying his hands as he searched for less brilliant words.

“My father says patience is a virtue,” Etienne added.

“It is, when we don't ask it only of other people.”

His friend laughed, then grew serious again.

“I don't want a position where everyone has to pretend I already know how to do the work.”

Edran thought of the people who had once been afraid to admit a gap in their knowledge before him. He had always preferred honesty. He had not always made it easy to practice. The young man beside him did not need that history to make the same request.

“Then write down what you need to learn too,” he said.

“In an application?”

“If you know the limit already, at least you can choose how to face it.”

Etienne stayed silent, then admitted he disliked how sensible that was.

“What would you say about someone who introduces you simply because you're his friend?”

Etienne raised his shoulders and let them drop almost immediately.

“That he might at least have read what I'd written.”

“Then let us begin there.”

His friend bought time by looking at the bench. The flat stone was still in his hand. He placed it on the edge and steadied it so it would not roll; the gesture gave him a few seconds to abandon a clever answer.

“I dislike needing all of you.”

He had used the plural of the house, the title, the people a name allowed one to reach. Edran recognized its painful precision.

“And should I pretend that knowing nobody is enough to make one just?”

Etienne turned the stone in his palm. “I don't want to send an application they'll read only because of your name.”

“Then let me read it before putting mine on it.”

“Are you going to be unbearable?”

“Only in the parts I dislike.”

His friend laughed and looked back at the path.

“I want you to tell me if something I write is foolish,” the young man said at last. “Not merely whether it would be polite to send it.”

“That is a request you may regret.”

“I know. I've seen you correct a thank-you letter as though it were a treatise.”

Edran remembered the letter and the blush of the boy he inhabited. Laughter came before he could choose whether to pretend to remember. Familiarity had found a path neither had planned.

Etienne watched with the relief of someone recognizing a sound, not the precision of someone seeking proof. Then he bent to retrieve the stone and changed his mind, leaving it on the bench.

Edran wondered how many small things about Alexander he would understand only after seeing them touch another person. The memories were whole. Friendship would need days before it became lived experience.

“You can bring me the application,” Alexander said. “I'll read it. I'm not promising you a position.”

“I know.”

This time the young man seemed pleased to know it.

They resumed walking. Edran could have asked about the cordial, his father's visits, conversations during the coma. He did not need to do so to know where the gift had come from. The sheet was in the study, and its sender could answer in person.

It was not mercy toward the baron. It was a refusal to use a son as the easiest route to his father.

They passed a bench. Years earlier, the old Alexander had left a jacket there. Etienne had returned it after hiding a joking letter inside. The memory contained the embarrassment, the relief, the color of the paper. It did not contain the right to consider that trust already his.

Edran slowed. He could have continued imitating the boy with cruel precision. He preferred to let his friend find something changed, even without understanding why.

Etienne noticed his silence.

“Are you tired?”

“I'm thinking.”

“You used to do that too. Usually it was when you became unbearable.”

Edran laughed. The young man gave his shoulder a small shove, recovering a confidence he had not yet earned.

Near the turn, Etienne stopped to remove a pebble from his shoe. He leaned against the low wall, raised his foot, and immediately lost his balance. Alexander offered his arm before he could decide whether to laugh.

“Don't tell anyone.”

“That you can only stand with a duke to support you?”

“Especially that.”

Edran felt the ordinary weight of the hand on his sleeve. His body did not need it, but the gesture held him there in the garden more firmly than any thought of his return.

Etienne straightened and let go. He did not ask whether the grip had hurt him. For a moment he had granted him the simplicity of being someone who could help without immediately becoming the center of concern. Alexander resumed walking, surprised by the gratitude this small favor received had left him.

When they returned toward the house, Etienne asked whether he could visit again the following week. Alexander said yes, leaving him to choose a day to confirm with Maurice.

At the door, Etienne looked for his hat and realized he had left it where Maurice had told him to put it. The butler handed it over before he could ask. The young man thanked him, then looked back at Alexander.

“If you don't want me to come, have someone say so. Don't invent an engagement to protect me from seeing a sick man.”

Edran felt how closely the request resembled those people in other times, under other names, had made of him. Power made it easy to regulate distances without explaining to those left outside why the door had closed.

“I shall let you know.”

He did not promise nothing would change. Etienne seemed to notice and, rather than asking for greater reassurance, put on his hat. The meeting still had the right to end before every possible thing had been said.

His friend departed lighter than he had arrived.

Edran stayed on the entrance step until the carriage was out of sight. Then he called Maurice.

“I'd like to receive Baron Ravelle tomorrow. Ask him to come personally, if he can.”

“For territorial business?”

Alexander looked at the road.

“For a conversation.”

His tone was enough to prevent further questions. He did not like how easy it had been.

Chapter 14 — A True Mark

Livia did not write immediately.

She had read both sets, listened to Marcel and Gaston describe the inspection, and asked Elias to distinguish what he had seen from what he had concluded afterward. Now she held the sheet still on the bench while a slice of bread with bitter marmalade waited to be finished.

“It's drying out,” Elias said.

“The conclusion?”

“The bread.”

Livia broke off a piece without looking up.

The bread had left crumbs beside the paper. She gathered them in her palm and tossed them out the window before returning to work. Elias realized he had credited her with an absolute orderliness she did not possess even when eating. A copyist could be hungry, forget a cloth, find the sound of a file irritating, like anyone else.

“Would you mind if we moved the lamp?” he asked.

Livia let him move it, then returned the sheet to where she could read it more easily.

“That's the spot where you work well.”

He withdrew his hand at once. The small correction seemed so much like their larger problem that it became funny.

“One concern at a time.”

Gaston had left the copies for comparison, not his entire professional file. Marcel stayed until they had distinguished the original position from the one examined that morning. Then he returned to his work a few steps away.

“I can attest to the comparison we make here,” Livia said. “Not the moment someone moved the bench.”

Elias nodded. The word attest carried a different weight when she said it.

Before using her ability, Livia chose a place on the table where every sheet could lie open without covering the others. Elias helped her move the drawing. He found himself paying more attention to his own bandaged wrist than to her fingers.

“If the cloth gets dirty, change it,” Livia said.

“It isn't from the test.”

She looked up.

“I know. I was talking about a hand.”

Elias lowered his eyes and laughed without pleasure. He had managed to hear a question about his responsibility even in advice about his cut. He wondered how many conversations he had forced others to repeat by arriving already intent on defending himself.

Livia passed him a sheet to hold steady while she found the one to place beside it. Its edge tickled his thumb. The gesture was as simple as holding a tool for Adrien; yet, knowing he was about to witness Archive, Elias caught himself expecting a tremor in the light.

“What should I see?”

“The sheet. You don't have to help me produce an effect.”

Marcel put down his file and approached close enough to check the comparison with them. Elias wanted the master to look at least a little impressed. Instead he saw him settle where he could read, calm as a man who did not need to despise another power to acknowledge his own trade.

Livia reread the heading. She returned to the passage Elias had reported too confidently and asked whether those had been the words. He closed his eyes briefly without activating anything. Not to find a hidden recording: to stop being guided by the sheet on which they had already written the tidier version.

He corrected a preposition. The meaning became no more favorable to Vern or to himself. The copyist changed the text and asked him to read it again. It was a laborious way to make something simple, Elias thought, until he realized the simplicity had only now arrived.

Livia prepared the medium and set two fingers on it. She was not merely drawing a mark someone could copy. Elias felt his gaze return to the point where the paper did not seem changed enough to justify the impression.

“What are you preserving?”

“The relationship between what I've observed and my attestation.”

She had him compare the mark with an ordinary copy. Visible resemblance could not reproduce the imbued bond. Elias did not possess Archive. He did not pretend to read every part of it. But he understood the difference between a shape and the supernatural act that had made it authentic.

“So if someone copies everything—”

“They'll have copied everything. They won't have observed what I observed.”

Elias approached the ordinary medium. He had reproduced the figure with his eyes, seeking a point where its shape might betray a difference. The harder he tried to study the stroke, the more the mark resembled a signature made by a patient hand.

Livia asked him to stop looking for a material flaw. It did not need to be ugly to differ from the act she had performed.

“So I don't learn to recognize it just by drawing it a hundred times.”

“You learn to draw it.”

Elias brought the copy beside the original until the figures almost touched. The first offered him only ink. In the other, he knew he had seen something come into being that shape alone did not contain.

“A forgery can be prettier.”

“Much prettier.”

Livia said it without smiling. Elias lifted the genuine sheet away from the copy. Magic was not making it easier to trust a page: it had just shown him another way a page could persuade him wrongly.

“What if you're the one who's wrong?” he asked.

Livia lifted her fingers from the sheet.

“You'll have an authentic attestation of what I can attest to. Not a mind incapable of error.”

Elias stared at the page. There was something reassuring about the limit, and something that made him wish for a less honest magic.

He told her about Vern. He tried to remember the words without improving them. Livia had him repeat the sentence about the declared margins. When he added so he knew, she separated the two things with a line.

“That's your conclusion.”

“It's yours too.”

“Perhaps.”

The answer made him laugh. Once again he had been looking for someone to spare him the task of remaining precise when he had reason to be angry.

Livia set the medium back down. The mark looked so quiet that Elias felt a small disappointment, immediately followed by shame at wanting a more visible light for something that truly mattered to him.

“If someone doesn't possess Archive, how can they avoid treating it as a signature?”

“They can ask what I'm attesting. They can refuse to rely on something they can't verify. I don't have to tell them they're foolish.”

Elias looked at the warehouse plan. It was the sort of respect he wanted from others when he measured with his eyes closed; he did not always grant it to people who asked him to explain.

“How did you come to trust my measurements?”

“You didn't ask me to trust every measurement you would take in your life.”

The answer left him without a joke. Livia put the ink case away. Beside her bag's hinge was a recently repaired seam in a slightly different color. Elias noticed because he had stopped looking only at what she could do for his case.

He could have asked who had repaired it. It would not have been an important question. For that very reason, he seemed no longer to know how to phrase it: for days he had steered conversations toward information that would justify the time spent obtaining it.

“Are you finished working when you leave here?”

“I've finished this work.”

“I meant to ask whether you've eaten.”

She looked at the bread he had already commented on, then gave him an amused glance.

“You've been inspecting it for ten minutes.”

Elias rubbed his face. If he still wanted friends once he cleared his name, he would have to learn to speak when he was not trying to save something, too.

“Have you ever authenticated something useless?”

Livia finished her bread.

“To a court? Plenty.”

“And to you?”

She closed her work case, leaving out her personal notebook.

“The letters I want to keep don't all have to become evidence.”

She took her time closing the ink. Elias understood he could let the conversation end there.

“Once I thought a wrong address was something I could fix later,” Livia said instead.

He stayed still.

“I corrected it. I didn't give that person back the time when they hadn't received the notice.”

Elias remembered the first testing plan, still correct while Etienne was carried out. Livia had left her personal notebook beside the work one. He wanted to ask whose notice it had been, but she was already screwing on the cap and offering him bread.

“Is that why you get angry when I add a conclusion?”

“I also get angry because you're sometimes irritating.”

Elias laughed. Livia finally took another piece of bread, and the conversation became something they could afford between friends again.

Elias remembered Lise's sheets crushed beneath his own. He let a moment pass before returning to work.

For a few moments they said nothing. Marcel had resumed filing; the short sound traveled back and forth along the same edge. Livia slid the case away from a drop of water Elias had left near the lamp.

“When you want me to stop asking questions, you can say so,” he murmured.

“I can do that without your permission.”

Elias nodded, then laughed with her. It was no new rebuke; perhaps it was the first time he had heard it without immediately needing to justify himself.

The copyist bent to find a sheet and asked him to hold her bag steady. He did so without opening it farther. They stayed that way as long as it took to find the page. No secret was about to be discovered; one person had simply asked another for help.

When Livia straightened, Elias gave her room and realized that, for a moment, his desire to speak had ceased to be a race against fear.

From the alley came the sound of a wheel stopping. Gaston appeared in the doorway again, this time without the toolbox.

“Ilian has an answer. He'd like to bring a sample tomorrow.”

Marcel put down the piece he was filing.

“Who requested it?”

“The request comes through him. I said I had to check with you.”

His mentor asked that no component from the workshop be prepared to leave. Any comparison would concern the object brought in and the documents provided.

Livia looked at Elias.

“Is this the matter of the drawing?”

“Yes.”

“Does Etienne know about it?”

“It has nothing to do with the test.”

She nodded, satisfied with the distinction.

Elias put the warehouse copies into one folder and the drawing into another. The two matters had not become independent merely because he separated them with cardboard. But at least he was ceasing to behave as though an unknown enemy had to explain every difficulty.

Elias capped the pen. A dot of ink remained on his fingertip; he let it dry before touching the clean sheet.

Before leaving, Livia asked for a copy of his account of Vern's words. Elias gave it to her.

On the sheet Elias had written: therefore he knew. Livia had drawn a line beneath Vern's words, leaving that conclusion outside.

He kept both. The line irritated him; he would return to the warehouse precisely to find what was missing above it.

Chapter 15 — An Ordinary Piece

The sample arrived wrapped in a clean cloth.

Ilian set it on the bench and waited for Marcel to signal him to open it. Elias recognized the central curve from the drawing, but the actual proportions immediately explained the discrepancy.

It was another piece.

Not proof that he had imagined his own hinge incorrectly. Not an offer to repair the one he knew.

He felt disappointed before he felt cautious.

The sample had a weight the drawing could not convey. Ilian had set it down with both hands, first protecting the bench with a fold of cloth. Elias followed its curve and found the first plan's error again: a difference large enough to hide, within a resemblance, a materially different thing.

“Did you receive it like this?” Marcel asked.

“In this wrapping. I haven't taken anything apart.”

The draftsman showed where he had lifted the cloth. There was no untamperable package to display as an absolute guarantee. He had merely preserved what he could and remembered the hands that had passed over the piece before his eyes.

Elias brought a lamp closer. The metal changed color where light met the edge, and for an instant he thought he had found the property that would explain everything. It was only the cloth's reflection.

He put the lamp back. Ilian watched without comment. It could hardly be the first time someone had expected more from an object than the object had promised.

“I'm interested in comparing that part,” Elias said, indicating the joint.

“As part of this piece,” Marcel specified.

The boy nodded. The master had uttered no secret; he had prevented their interest being mistaken for an offer to show another. Elias realized how much more attention that distinction required when he was keen to be useful.

Ilian opened his notebook to find the request. A smaller sheet slipped toward the edge, and he stopped it with the back of his hand. Its fold stubbornly curled back on itself.

Ilian had brought his bill too, folded inside his notebook. He did not put it on the bench. Elias saw it because it slipped as he searched for the request, and understood that correcting a drawing was not, for everyone, an exercise that could be repeated without consequence.

“Have they paid you?”

“Not all of it.”

“Does the comparison depend on the balance?”

Ilian put the sheet away.

“The balance depends on what work they recognize as finished.”

Marcel looked at the sample without touching it.

“Then let's also describe what you weren't given in order to finish it.”

Ilian nodded. He did not seem to want someone to be offended on his behalf. He seemed to want a sentence he could bring a client without losing his right to be paid.

“May I?” Marcel asked.

Ilian nodded. The written request concerned comparison and assessment, not a higher source given away for free. One note bore the name Xenia Helren, with observations about the sample's documentation. It did not certify all the properties another sheet attributed to it.

Marcel spread the two texts side by side.

“These don't say the same thing.”

“I noticed,” Ilian replied.

His relief had given way to a more concrete nervousness. He had already worked on the drawing, and someone expected a finished sheet, not new questions.

Elias showed him the enlarged copy of the lines.

“The dimension error is one thing. The shape is another.”

“That's why I wanted the piece.”

Marcel checked that the sheet identified the sample actually received. Ilian showed him the available correspondence, distinguishing his own contribution from the earlier copyist's. Being asked to redraw it had not automatically made him responsible for every line.

Elias thought of Gaston, the second set of readings, how easily one heading could be put over work done at different times.

“Can we speak to Helren?” he asked.

“I can pass on the request for a comparison.”

It was not a meeting already guaranteed. It was still more useful than a name to suspect without knowing.

Ilian set his fingers on the metal. His gaze stopped following what his eyes could see. There were no sparks or spectacular colors. When he spoke, he distinguished materials and joints with a confidence that did not come from merely looking at them.

Elias had seen demonstrations of Crucible before, but the stillness of that procedure seemed stranger than a flash of light. Ilian was not guessing at the substance. He reached it through a faculty Elias did not possess.

“That doesn't confirm an imbued passage,” Marcel said.

“No. I'm examining the components.”

Ilian withdrew his hand. The sample remained an object on the bench, opening nothing.

Marcel took the sample only after Ilian gave permission. He turned it on the cloth and held it still while Elias compared the side view. The paper's lines finally became a set of surfaces a hand could reach.

“The edge doesn't continue here,” Elias said.

Ilian brought the sheet beside the object. The break matched an area the copy had flattened. The discovery brought them no nearer a distant room or a hidden workshop; it simply explained why the drawing had lied without the copyist having decided to deceive them.

“A shadow mistaken for a boundary,” the draftsman said.

Elias remembered his irritation at the case that was too short. He had judged someone before seeing what the protruding part was for. He was tempted to do the same with this sample: attribute to it the ill intent that would make distrusting the client easier.

Marcel had him hold the sheet while he checked the opposite part with an ordinary instrument. Ilian had just completed a supernatural reading; the master still measured an edge. Neither seemed to consider the other's work useless.

“May I mark this break on my plan?” the draftsman asked.

“If you distinguish what you see now from what you received.”

Ilian took the time to mark the point with a fine line, leaving the previous stroke intact. Elias watched him choose where to place the note so it would not look like part of the object. There was a minute creativity in this work, different from the one with which he had imagined passages and connections. It consisted of giving a doubt a readable form without filling it out of embarrassment.

The draftsman lifted the sheet. Light passed through its margin, and for an instant the lines on the reverse overlapped the dimensions. He laid it back on the cloth, where it could actually be read.

The commercial request spoke of maintaining a connection and asked for comparison with a working specimen. The prospective client's name now appeared in full as the contact for further conditions: Mael Vaur.

Elias read it twice. It meant nothing to him.

“Do you know him?” he asked Marcel.

“Not well enough to hand him an object.”

Ilian sat down. The sample's edge was rough where the drawing made it smooth.

“They also asked whether I could correct this surface.”

“Can you?”

Ilian took a small scrap of material from his own supplies, separate from the specimen. He passed a finger over it. When he offered it to Elias, one area had changed texture. The boundary could be felt beneath a fingernail.

Elias ran his fingertip over the scrap, then tried his nail at the indicated point. The surface offered different resistance from one area to the next. He had seen no heat, abrasion, or tool to account for the change. The piece reached his hand already different.

He moved his finger over the boundary and felt how narrow it was. Ilian watched with the concentration of someone waiting for a client to stop looking for the trick and decide whether the service was useful.

“May I mark where I feel it?”

The draftsman nodded. Elias did not use the sample as a reference or place the scrap on it. He marked the point on a separate piece of paper, then compared it with the one Ilian had indicated.

It was a small demonstration, performed without claiming to open a passage. Perhaps that was why it seemed so convincing. A power could be real even when its possessor refused to promise the thing for which people hoped to pay him.

“One simple property. Not what they're claiming here.”

Marcel waited for Elias to return the scrap.

“And you don't want to do it to the sample before the comparison.”

“If I change it, how do I explain what it was like?”

Ilian put away the scrap he had transformed. He continued to touch the real sample only with his eyes.

“If they ask me to smooth this edge before Helren sees it, I won't,” he said.

“Have they asked?”

“They asked for a more convincing drawing. A clean edge would make it so.”

Elias looked at the irregularity. Having touched it, he knew how little it took to change the story told by paper. Ilian had the ability to erase the difference and the need to be paid. His thumb stayed still outside the wrapping.

“Write it down,” he said.

Ilian looked up.

“That I won't modify it?”

“That you don't want to transform the object before describing it.”

Marcel added that he would answer about the sample and the limits of the examination, without presenting an old piece's presence in his workshop as an offer to sell it.

Ilian nodded. He rolled the drawing more carefully than when he had arrived.

“Shall I leave the sample for comparison?”

“Only with a recorded handover and conditions for its return,” Marcel said.

Ilian spread the empty wrapping to check that no small element remained inside. Elias waited, keeping his hands away from the object. The piece required no secret preparation for safekeeping; it required someone to remember why it was there and when it must leave.

“What if Helren's answer takes longer?” Elias asked.

“We'll let the client know. I don't decide by myself to leave it with you for ever.”

Ilian checked the date in his notebook. “If I leave it, I have to return to the client without the sample.”

“With the receipt,” Marcel said.

“A receipt won't please him as much as an answer.”

Elias showed him where to record the next comparison. The man copied it in the space below the bill.

“I don't want to make things difficult for you.”

“Then let's write down properly what you received.”

The draftsman had not asked him to guess the solution to his entire life. He indicated the sample, the request's wording, and the date. Marcel added the terms of return. They reread the page long enough to remove a sentence that seemed to attest more than they had done.

When Ilian put his pencil away, Elias looked at the clear space on the bench. They had obtained an object to study, not the right to consider the person who brought it available too. The draftsman folded the cloth and put it in his bag with the care of someone who would need it again.

They filled out an ordinary receipt. No one signed that the piece was what the commercial sheet promised.

When Ilian left, Elias remained before the client's name.

For days he had searched for someone responsible who could explain a measurement that had become dangerous. Now he had a correct measurement, a man who had decided to move the bench, and, in another folder, a question asked by someone who wanted to see a working object.

He turned to Marcel.

“We aren't showing him ours.”

His mentor set the sample in its case.

“No.”

Elias felt none of the old resentment at being stopped. He looked at the motionless pin and finally wondered why that client was not satisfied with examining what he already had.

Chapter 16 — The Baron's Gift

Baron Marcel Ravelle arrived with a walking stick he did not need for walking.

Alexander remembered that stick. During a visit years earlier, Etienne had used it to imitate his father until a maid burst out laughing. Now the silver knob rested between two hands perfectly capable of gripping it.

“Your Grace. What a joy to see you recovered.”

“Sit down.”

Edran had ordered the curtains opened. The baron examined him in the study's full light, and the satisfaction on his face had time to arrive a little late.

It was not evidence. Alexander made himself remember that before he began enjoying his own attentiveness.

Maurice served water and withdrew. The door remained closed, not locked. Teren waited in the corridor as instructed that morning; no guards had gone to occupy the Ravelle residence.

The baron asked about his rest, then about Celine. Alexander answered without supplying clinical details. The conversation could have ended there, with enough courtesy to make every subsequent question seem discourteous.

Ravelle left the glass where Maurice had placed it. He asked after those who had cared for Alexander, pronouncing Mirel's name with the familiarity of someone who knew how the house worked. Edran answered only about what the doctor had already made shareable.

“You were fortunate.”

The sentence could have been sincere. The duke watched the tip of the cane, still beside the chair's leg. There was no shadow to betray the crime, no smell of fear the world would deliver merely because he had returned.

“I was.”

Ravelle spoke of the strain of recent weeks, the need not to address every matter in a single day. He had a patient way of making relinquishment reasonable: he began with the burden everyone would acknowledge and arrived, without a visible leap, at who should carry it in Alexander's place.

Edran remembered hearing that voice in the boy's memories. Then the concern had been met with gratitude and youthful embarrassment, fear of seeming unequal to his role. Now he could recognize the movement without yet knowing which part of the courtesy concealed intent.

“Have you a specific recommendation?”

The baron smiled.

“Resume gradually. Rely on those who knew your father.”

“And if they knew only his habits?”

Ravelle raised his eyebrows slightly. He said the household's experience would be valuable nonetheless. It was a correct answer to a question Edran had framed to see how easily it could be tamed.

He had not invited the baron for a contest of allusions. The thought cooled him enough to pick up the prepared sheet. He placed it on the table without pushing it toward his guest like a sprung trap.

“I found the note that accompanied your gift.”

Ravelle lowered his eyes to the table.

“A small token. I hope it was welcome.”

“Do you remember what it contained?”

“A cordial. You seemed exhausted.”

“Did you choose it yourself?”

“Certainly.”

Alexander placed a copy of the note between them. The original was elsewhere with the receipt, and the vial remained in Mirel's custody. He did not wish to discover how quickly an elderly man could spill a glass over something that frightened him.

The baron did not touch the sheet.

“Is there a problem with the preparation?”

“I drank it on the evening of the fourteenth. Then I was unconscious for four days.”

The walking stick tilted. Ravelle straightened it.

“How terrible. I could never have imagined—”

“I haven't told you yet what you imagined.”

The silence changed in substance.

In the memories of a dinner, Edran saw the young duke taking care not to interrupt a man his father had received courteously. The boy had tried to become serious enough to be heard. Someone had poured death into a small token of kindness.

For a few moments he wanted to seize the baron's wrist. Destruction would have been perfectly simple; the questions would have remained for whoever cleaned the room.

He left his hands on the armrests.

“Tell me what you did to the preparation intended for me. Choose to answer accurately, without concealing your actions behind anyone else's.”

He did not raise his voice.

Ravelle looked at the jug. For an instant Edran thought he wanted a drink. Instead the man settled against the backrest and drew breath, relieved by a decision it now seemed appropriate to have made.

There were no strings on his fingers. The cane remained his, his mouth chose its words, his eyes could still look at the door. Only his reason to stay silent no longer belonged to him.

Edran felt the imposed intention find a voice. The baron spoke as calmly as when he had advised the boy to rest.

“I altered it before it left my house.”

Alexander did not move.

“Did you want to make me ill?”

“I wanted you to die.”

The branch against the window was silent now. Maurice had had it tied back the evening before.

The baron continued. He said he had prepared the gift and entrusted it to the ordinary delivery service. No one in the ducal kitchen had promised to help him. Etienne did not know. No one had asked him to do it.

“Before delivery,” Edran repeated. “Tell me what you did yourself.”

The baron returned to the preparation. Where he had not seen the transport he stopped; where he remembered his own hand his voice grew precise. Edran let him speak. Somewhere between gift and bed the boy's life had ended. The words were reaching that point with an ease that made him want to break the table.

Alexander asked whether anyone had altered the contents after they left the Ravelle house. The baron answered what he knew and stopped where he had not personally observed the delivery. Edran let that limit remain visible.

The light in the room had not changed. Someone passed in the corridor with a tread regular enough not to be listening. The world could contain such a confession without producing thunder to warn those outside.

“Did your son touch the gift?”

Ravelle explained that he had arranged its preparation without confiding the adulteration to him. He did not describe Etienne as an accomplice to protect. He placed him on the margins with a naturalness that made listening harder.

Edran saw the previous day's embrace again. The father had let his son visit the man he wanted dead. He could find no evil grandeur to make it more comprehensible. There were ambition, exploited trust, and the ordinary capacity to continue a day after delivering a gift.

He had the preparation repeated. He requested no formula, sought no pleasure in hearing the inflicted pain described. The boy was already dead; no precision about the poison would restore what had gone out in the bed.

Ravelle answered with less hesitation than he had shown earlier over Celine's health. The mandate imposed an intention; it did not remove the language in which he had learned to make his decisions acceptable. He kept explaining, and that ease was more atrocious than a scream.

Alexander returned to the moment when the baron would have had to await news. He wanted to know what he intended to do once death was confirmed. Ravelle's fingers moved on the knob. Then his voice continued in the same tone, as though they had finally reached the part of the visit he had prepared for.

“And afterward?”

“Your sister would have needed continuity.”

Alexander felt his fingers press against the wood.

“From whom?”

Ravelle explained the temporary council. Four counts who would hesitate to grant precedence to one another; a baron modest enough to seem a solution. Contracts to review. Appointments. A request, still unsigned, in his own study.

He had not secured enough support. He had killed before obtaining it.

The baron described a house in which every decision would require advisers. The minor would bear the name and receive the education owed her; the others would guarantee continuity. The word recurred so consistently that it seemed the conversation's real subject, something Alexander's body had been imprudent enough to interrupt.

“Who had agreed to this?”

Ravelle distinguished contacts, expectations, and a project that had not yet received the necessary signatures. Edran felt his anger change target. To obtain a half-certainty, he had sacrificed a whole life. He had faced no network so perfect there was no escape; he had taken advantage of a weary young man who trusted a drink delivered to his own room.

He asked what he would have told the girl. The baron returned to guardianship, mourning, duties. He found no sentence that required naming her as a person capable of wanting anything beyond his administrative arrangement.

Edran had known that emptiness in other languages. As a child, he had heard decisions about his body pronounced without being consulted. Later, he had gained enough strength to impose silence on those who remembered him only when they feared him. Possessing that history did not make him better than everyone present; it prevented him from mistaking clean language for an absence of violence.

The hand on the armrest relaxed. Not because his anger had lessened. It had finally found a precise place to stop: listening long enough not to let the baron drag the possibility of knowing into his own end.

“Celine would have retained her dignity,” he said.

For the first time, Alexander smiled.

The baron fell silent before that smile, but could no longer choose to use it as an opportunity to lie.

“Her name above your decisions.”

“She would have had experienced advisers.”

“She had a brother.”

The sentence remained in the room with a precision Edran had not foreseen. The memory of Celine at the bedside stopped him from doing something final.

He asked about the deaths of Lucien and Eliane. The baron had played no part in them. He asked who had known about the adulterated gift. No secret network emerged, only a man who had relied on the trust of a grieving household.

Edran ended the investment of power.

Ravelle remained seated. He looked at the note, then at Alexander, and his own explanation seemed to reach him from another direction. He really had spoken those words. No gap in his memory took them away.

“I...”

He stopped. Alexander did not repeat the command.

“Now you can choose to remain silent.”

The baron gripped his walking stick. His knuckles had turned pale.

“What have you done to me?”

“For a few minutes, I took away your choice to lie to me about your actions.”

He did not offer himself the comfort of calling it an ordinary conversation.

The baron moved as if to rise, then stopped. He had received no new command. He looked at the door with the attention of a man trying to remember who was close enough to hear him.

Edran felt the desire to offer a terrible sentence, something that would remain in memory as long as the confession. He let it pass. That self-indulgence would teach him nothing about the dead boy, and the room already contained enough fear without his decorating it with his own.

The cane's knob struck the chair's edge. Ravelle gripped it at once, but the sound had reached the corridor. Alexander saw the man's ear strain toward the door. Even without the mandate active, the silence between them had not become free again.

He rang the bell. Teren entered and saw an elderly man sitting before a perfectly still duke.

“The baron will remain with us while we request intervention from the proper authority,” Alexander said. “No one enters his house on my order alone. Preserve what you have already received.”

Teren looked first at him, then at Ravelle.

“Shall I call Mirel?”

Alexander realized his breathing was too regular.

“Yes.”

He could have described that morning as an easy victory. He had found the culprit in less time than it took to serve a second pitcher.

When the baron rose, Edran remembered how Etienne had embraced his friend the day before.

The easy part was over.

Chapter 17 — The Price of Comparison

Xenia Helren did not look like the name Elias had constructed in his head.

That was reason enough to distrust his own head, not the inspector.

Pale ridges ran beneath the skin of Helren's hands, more regular than veins. When the inspector bent a finger, the mineral network followed the joint without interrupting the movement. The collar revealed a similar stretch along the neck. Elias looked away as soon as he realized he was staring.

“I prefer people to look at me first,” Helren said. “Afterward, I'd like you to look at the piece.”

“I'm Elias Veyne.”

“That I knew.”

They were in Ruy's workshop. Ilian had passed on the request and stayed long enough to confirm the appointment, then returned to his own work. Marcel had left only the received sample and the relevant correspondence on the bench.

Ilian remained on the threshold to make sure the introduction sufficed. Once it was clear Helren had received the same request, he closed his notebook and announced he would return to his own work. Elias nodded. Introducing them had not earned the draftsman the task of sitting there until the end.

Helren waited for the doorway to clear before moving. Window light crossed the ridges on his hands, leaving some paler than others. Elias looked away too quickly, making his stare still more obvious.

The inspector spared him a second observation. He asked where he could sit without obstructing the passage. Marcel moved a crate with his foot and Elias cleared tools irrelevant to the comparison from the bench's edge.

He had prepared to meet a name beneath a note. A person was now asking him for a chair, the sample, and the reason to devote part of his day to it. None of the three requests was unreasonable. Elias found them difficult because, in his mind, Helren's arrival should already have been an answer.

“I corrected one of the dimensions,” he said.

“On the plan or on the piece?”

“I marked the discrepancy in the drawing.”

“Then show me the drawing you started from as well.”

Elias looked for it in the separate folder. He did not need to dispute the inspector's authority to feel observed; a precise question alone prevented him from choosing only the page on which he had worked well.

Helren set a folder beside the chair, not on the bench.

“The comparison has a price.”

Elias nodded too quickly. The inspector noticed.

“Do you know what it is yet?”

“No.”

“Then you haven't agreed yet.”

Marcel turned toward a shelf. Elias suspected he had hidden a smile.

Helren explained the proposed work: examine the sample, compare two versions of the request, and state which claims had been authorized. The result would be supplied in a restricted extract. The entire commercial correspondence was not for sale.

The requested sum was not enormous. Elias only had to imagine subtracting it from his own money to feel that it was not small.

The price had the simplicity of a figure that took no account of the people forced to subtract it. Elias looked at Marcel's hand near the sample. It would have been easy to wait for the master to intervene, to offer payment and spare his apprentice the embarrassment of discussing a sum that was not enormous.

Instead, Marcel gave him time. Not because he ignored money's weight: he knew how a solution offered too soon could become the only one a person learned to request.

Elias thought of the plan still to be reproduced to scale. The discrepancy required no authority he lacked. It required time, patience, and a signature responsible for what left the workshop. It was work precise enough to offer without selling his gratitude.

“I don't mean to ask you to do it for nothing,” he added before a complete proposal occurred to him.

Helren set down the folder so its flap did not touch the floor.

“That is not what I asked you.”

The answer removed the comfortable role of someone entitled to feel rejected. The inspector was not accusing him of poverty or insolence. He had asked whether he accepted an assignment, and Elias must understand what he could offer in return.

Elias looked at the sheet. His injured hand could hold a ruler, provided he did not try to work as though the cut did not exist. The cost of two hours was no abstract sum. He would know when he had spent them and what had come of them.

“Could I do part of the work?”

“Which part?”

“Produce the scale drawing of the sample. With measurements separate from those in the first drawing. It's my trade.”

Helren asked whether his permit allowed that work. Elias wished he could answer without feeling the blush rising up his neck.

Marcel intervened.

“He can prepare it here under my responsibility. He won't sign an independent professional certification.”

Helren asked him to indicate which parts of the reproduction he could complete without interpreting missing lines. Elias showed the outer edge, the dimensions to transfer, and the part to leave recognizable as a gap.

“And if the finished drawing looks ugly?”

The young man looked at the broken line. He could have completed it with a plausible curve in less time than it would take to explain why he had not.

“Then it has to look ugly there.”

Marcel asked how much time he wanted to spend distinguishing notes from measurements of the sample. Elias began answering too quickly, then took a page and sketched only the arrangement he proposed. It was easier to show where a note would appear than promise abstract clarity.

Helren followed the margin with his eyes. He did not request a free demonstration of the entire plan's execution. He wanted to understand what he was accepting and how he could establish that the work had been done.

Helren returned to the drawing's incomplete edge. Elias took his pencil and marked where he would stop the line without closing it with the curve that came naturally. The inspector followed the point; Marcel checked the scale. For once the next question had an answer Elias could deliver by working.

The inspector assessed the sheet already on the bench, then requested two hours of work limited to the reproduction, to be delivered on the twenty-fourth. No maintenance of the inspector's instruments, no availability for future emergencies.

Elias agreed. Marcel agreed to check the work. Only then did Helren open the folder.

“I wrote this.”

The note concerned the documentation required for an examination. It stated that a drawing alone did not demonstrate an active connection. A cautious answer to an imprecise question.

“This, however, I did not.”

The second text retained enough sentences from the first to seem the same document. Helren had him follow a line: in the genuine sheet something remained to be checked; in the other it had already been verified.

Elias returned to the signature. That was the passage that had reassured him. He would have brought the example to meet someone whose approval had never existed.

Elias set his nail beside the altered line. That was where he would put the question.

“Did Vaur have it rewritten?”

Helren did not answer at once.

“I received a request through that chain of contacts. I disputed this version. I did not see a hand changing it.”

“But your name—”

“Is precisely why I'm here.”

The inspector took out the retained sheet. Two fingers rested near its margin, investing the relationship between the mark and that object. Elias saw a fine trace appear where ordinary eyes would have found only paper.

Helren turned the document over and showed Marcel its back.

“If someone tampers with this testimony after it has been bound, the mark bears the alteration. It doesn't certify what I haven't seen.”

Marcel followed the comparison. Elias knew enough Archive to recognize that this was not a prettier True Mark: the testimony had been bound to its object, and a subsequent intervention could not simply change that object while leaving the relationship intact.

That did not teach him to read every component of it.

“Can you supply the extract?” Marcel asked.

“After the final comparison and the agreed work. This version has already been disputed. You aren't buying my discovery of the wrongdoing.”

“We're buying something we can use.”

“Exactly.”

Elias looked at the sample. At first the discrepancy in the measurements had seemed an obstacle to remove on the way to the important piece. Now he was facing someone who had tried to use an incomplete description to borrow an authority.

“Why did they want a working specimen?”

“A good question to ask them.”

“Didn't you ask?”

Helren half closed the folder.

“I asked what I was supposed to attest. The answers weren't sufficient. I did not agree to move people to allow a demonstration.”

Marcel looked up.

“People?”

“A proposed use. Not an abduction I witnessed.”

Street noise entered through the open window. Someone was bargaining over the price of a repaired wheel in a voice heated enough to make the workshop conversation seem motionless.

Elias realized he wanted a greater revelation than the one he had received. A recognizable threat would have spared him the task of treating the piece as a question still incomplete.

The sample stayed in the middle of the bench while the inspector arranged the documents. Elias noticed the two texts were not put away alike: the one Helren acknowledged was kept apart from the disputed version. The correspondence did not cease to matter merely because another hand had made it suspect.

“May I ask you something unrelated to the piece?”

Helren stopped.

“You may ask.”

“When someone uses your name, how do you keep from answering more forcefully than your knowledge allows?”

The inspector looked at him long enough to make him want to withdraw the question. Then he indicated the disputed text.

“That sentence claims more than I did. If I answer by doing the same, I give them my method as well as my name.”

Elias nodded. He did not know whether he would remember those words when he needed them. He found it significant that Helren had said them without claiming to feel no anger.

The master put the sample in the agreed position. Safeguarding the object resembled no triumph; it was a commitment not to alter it while someone else waited for its return. Elias followed the case's edge with his eyes and felt how the proposed work finally had bounds. He did not need to settle the whole affair in two hours. He needed to execute a plan well, distinguishing received parts from imagined ones.

When Helren closed the folder, the young man made room for the chair without using an ability. It seemed the simplest way not to turn even the farewell into one last answer to obtain.

Helren rose.

“When I return, the sample must be in the same condition. If you change it, say so first.”

“We aren't leaving here with your material,” Marcel said.

“Nor have I asked to take yours away.”

The inspector said it while looking at Elias. There was no need to know what was kept elsewhere to understand that the two craftsmen were protecting something.

When the door closed, Elias remained with his pencil in hand.

“Two hours,” Marcel said.

“I remember.”

“Good. Caution won't do your drawing for you.”

Elias spread out a clean sheet. This time he did not place his future at the center of the drawing: he placed the sample actually before him.

Chapter 18 — After the Answer

Mirel would not let Alexander tell her his conclusion before showing her where it came from.

He was irritated. The irritation told him enough about his own condition to make him let her finish the question.

Teren had escorted Ravelle to a guarded room in the residence, pending the intervention requested from the district court. The baron had not been beaten. His walking stick and personal effects had been listed, not made into trophies for the guards.

Teren returned from the door with the expression of someone given an entirely executable order that was no easier to explain to others. He stopped where he had entered before. Alexander indicated a chair. The officer hesitated, then sat without treating it as an invitation to rest.

Mirel looked at the untouched glass. She did not ask why nobody had drunk. Alexander realized he had prepared a whole account around a detail to which the doctor had not yet assigned meaning.

“Begin before the answer,” she said.

He could have described the gift, the name, the baron's visit. Those were the facts in which his role still seemed clean: he had sought to understand an attempt on his life, received a man, asked a question. What followed became no easier simply because he had obtained the culprit.

Teren rested both hands on his knees. The gesture left the table clear, offering the duke no foothold from which to force the conversation straight to the documents.

Edran saw they were waiting for him. They did not possess the revelation that would let them recognize an ancient being and attribute every choice to cosmic necessity. They knew Alexander, an attempted murder, and a detained guest. They deserved an explanation framed for them.

He began with the name on the note and reached the moment he had decided to act. He did not describe that transition as anger's involuntary effect. Fury had been present; he had made the decision.

Now the study seemed too large without the man who had filled it with words.

“I compelled him to answer,” Alexander said.

Mirel's pen hung still.

Teren, near the door, did not pretend he had not heard.

“With a threat?” the doctor asked.

“Through his will. I imposed the purpose of recounting what he had done to the gift.”

He said nothing about Edran, the other Effigies, or what existed within him. He had just disclosed an ability sufficient to change how two people might feel in that room.

Mirel put down her pen.

“Are you doing anything like that now?”

“No.”

The answer did not produce the relief a truthful word would have deserved in an easier world.

Alexander looked at the doctor's hand, then at Teren's. Neither owed him the assurance of not having been commanded merely because he promised it.

Mirel did not pick up her pen immediately. The silence had lost the shape of waiting and taken that of a choice. Alexander stayed still, leaving her the chance to find words he had not already supplied.

“What you asked me during the examination,” she said at last. “Must I regard it in the same way?”

“I did not exercise that ability on you.”

The answer seemed painfully insufficient precisely because it was exact. He did not want to say he had not needed to: it would sound like a threat, an assurance that she had behaved well enough not to deserve a command.

Teren looked from the doctor to the duke. The ordinary words a superior spoke already carried weight in employees' lives; now they must consider the possibility that it did not end there.

“This is not necessary to issue an instruction to the guard,” he said.

“I know.”

“I cannot attest that a choice wasn't imposed because it feels like mine,” Mirel said.

Teren looked up. Edran stopped searching for reassuring wording.

“No.”

The doctor left the pen outside the notebook and closed it. “Then your word alone isn't enough even for this room.”

Edran felt the answer strike something very old inside him. He had received supplications from people who would never have dared use that tone. Mirel was still waiting. He took his hand from the armrest and indicated the documents that could go to outside scrutiny.

“You can request a relevant examination without letting me choose who carries it out,” he added. “I won't require you to consider this conversation independent on my word.”

Teren moved first.

“For now, I'll distinguish what you heard from what I can report having seen.”

“Do that.”

Alexander reconstructed the questions. He did not turn the answers into a perfect transcript: he recorded the words he was certain of and identified the passages he summarized. It was a less comfortable discipline than recollection, because it required admitting that remembering a man exactly was not the same as having brought another witness.

“He says he altered the preparation before delivery,” Mirel said. “We can compare that with the residue. I can't deduce it from the course of the illness alone.”

“His draft is in his study. Not in this house.”

Teren nodded.

“I'll report its existence. Access must be authorized by whoever has the standing to do so.”

Alexander could have reached that room by means entirely different from a request. His ability to do so would not have taught the court to distinguish what he had found from what he could have fabricated.

For an instant it seemed intolerable. Then he remembered how reasonable the baron had found deciding for everyone else.

“We won't give him time to destroy it,” he said.

“I'll request urgency,” Teren replied. “I won't promise that a sheet still exists before I've seen it.”

The doctor watched him longer than was necessary to follow his final sentence. She asked whether he had eaten after the baron's visit.

Alexander was about to answer that he needed no assistance. That was not the question. He looked at the tray and realized he had not thought about food.

“Not yet.”

“Then at least sit while I finish checking the notes.”

The request did not measure his strength. It did not require an ancient creature to tremble like an ordinary convalescent. Mirel had seen a young man confront an accusation of murder, then declare that he had bent a will. Her way of putting something back in place was to check the body before her, as far as the patient allowed.

Edran sat. The anger did not dissipate with the gesture; it merely stopped requiring him to stand to demonstrate it.

Teren asked whether he should leave. Mirel said he could remain for the documents they needed to compare, and looked at Alexander before opening the clinical section. It was a simple boundary, maintained while everything seemed to have grown beyond the scale of their habits.

The duke agreed to the relevant comparison. She sought the marked page without making him feel the time it took. Edran stayed seated until she found it. For a few moments he did not have to produce an answer to order the future: he had to listen to what another person could truly report.

Mirel closed her notebook and had the vial brought in. She did not open it to offer him an improvised explanation. She checked the closure, its custody, and the notes already taken, then separated the request for analysis from her own clinical observation.

The glass was small. Alexander noticed it with a disproportionate hatred for the object's harmless shape.

“Before anything else, I want the staff to know I'm not accusing them.”

“Do you have grounds to exclude every person?”

“I have a direct statement placing the adulteration before delivery. I have no evidence against Marta or Maurice. I don't want suspicion to become a punishment while we wait for a more convenient name.”

Mirel agreed to that wording.

When they left the study, two people in the corridor fell silent. News of a detained guest had already found legs without receiving an order.

Alexander called Maurice. He told him what could be communicated: an investigation into the gift, intervention requested, no accusation against the household staff. He asked that the message also reach those who did not work near the principal rooms.

Maurice reread the prepared message. There were words a household would enlarge as soon as they passed from room to room: gift, investigation, baron. Edran asked him not to erase them all to reassure the staff. Leaving only silence would give more room to the imagination of those who had reason to fear for their positions.

“May I say that staff should not be questioned by their colleagues?” the butler asked.

“Yes. If someone remembers a fact, they should report it to those gathering information. They should not have to defend themselves while working.”

Maurice nodded and altered a line. Edran watched him find a way to communicate a limit without promising that no question would ever be asked again.

The attention did him good. The study had just contained a power capable of bending a man; now someone sought a comprehensible sentence for the people who would wash the glasses. The difference in scale did not make the second task superfluous.

Voices resumed quietly in the corridor. Not everything withheld from the duke was a conspiracy. Edran knew that; he had to remind himself nonetheless while his body remained ready to react to every sound.

Maurice waited before asking the next question. He had recognized that the news must also reach someone for whom a service message would not suffice.

“Celine?” the butler asked.

Edran did not answer at once.

He could delay until every question had an official answer. Meanwhile, the duke's sister would learn to seek them in the adults' silences.

“I'll speak to her. First, let Etienne know I need to see him. Not through his father.”

Maurice looked at the sheet in his hand.

“What reason should I give?”

Alexander wanted to use a vague kindness and leave the burden of the true sentence to himself. He would be bringing a friend into a trap made of good intentions.

“A grave accusation concerning the baron. I want to speak to him directly. Don't ask him to come if he'd prefer a letter.”

The court's reply arrived that afternoon: a representative would come to the residence to take the complaint and address the measures within the office's authority. It was not a verdict.

Teren read the notice without visible satisfaction. Responsibility for access to the baron's room would be handed over, not extended through mere habit.

“Ravelle asks to speak to you,” he said afterward.

“To say what?”

“He didn't say.”

Edran could still feel how easily he could obtain every word. Easy did not mean without cost. Not for the person bent to his will, nor for the one learning to find no further reason to stop.

“Tell him his request will be passed on. I'm not returning to question him now.”

Before being left alone, he looked at the chair Mirel had vacated. She had not promised to consider him harmless. She had stayed long enough to do her work.

Alone, Alexander took the baron's untouched glass of water and carried it to the tray.

He could have unmade it with a touch. He left it whole.

Chapter 19 — The Distance Across a Table

On the morning of the twenty-second, Lise asked Elias not to use his Effigy.

He had just rested his hand on the model, and withdrew it as though he had touched a hot pan.

“I hadn't done anything yet.”

“I know. That's why I asked now.”

Cardboard, string, and a small frame of wooden strips covered the table at home. Lise had cleared the place where Elias habitually left his papers. To build the model she had used some of the material Adrien kept for repairs, with his permission; an open box waited for the scraps.

Elias sat down.

“What is it supposed to do?”

“Carry a load from here to here. Without dropping it.”

“A simple matter.”

“You said that before seeing the load.”

Lise showed him a small chipped cup, then put three pebbles inside. The frame tilted as soon as she released the string. Elias instinctively reached out; Lise looked at him, and he stopped.

The cup hung badly.

It was ridiculous that a man who could sense a distance without measuring it should find it so difficult to sit before a problem that did not belong to him.

“May I hold this end?”

“Yes.”

This time the question spared him a rebuke.

Lise tried two positions for the counterweight. At the second, the base slid. Adrien saw it from the doorway and brought a cloth to put beneath the frame.

“Have you considered the table?”

Elias and his sister turned toward him together.

Adrien raised his hands.

“Only a question. I withdraw my expert opinion immediately.”

When he left, Lise suppressed a smile. Elias recognized that discreet satisfaction in his father: having helped without needing to remain the owner of the solution.

Adrien remained at the threshold long enough to see that the frame had stopped sliding. Lise thanked him and he indicated the cup with a gesture small enough to ignore without offending him.

“If you want me to leave you in peace, I really do have to go.”

“That's a system we could try,” his daughter said.

He disappeared, laughing quietly. Elias looked at the base. The cloth improved its footing, but the cup still hung from the wrong point. It had been easy to attribute everything to the one defect their father had found, as though identifying one cause guaranteed they had found them all.

“Can I hold this part while you try?”

Lise moved her brother's hand to a specific slat. She told him not to press where it would alter the base's inclination. Elias felt his weight reach his fingers and eased the pressure without using his ability.

The cup moved. A pebble struck the rim and stayed inside. The girl was following the string rather than the load and missed the point where the system began to rotate.

“Stop,” Elias said.

She froze. Her brother indicated the base and waited for her to look. He did not yet tell her what to change.

“It wasn't like this here before.”

“I see it.”

Lise returned the load to its starting position and rubbed her thumb against dried glue. Elias wanted to promise they would find the solution immediately. Trying twice already did not make that true. Instead he asked whether she wanted to begin again or first observe the movement from another side.

His sister stood and made room. For the first time, she held the slat while he followed the path with the string. The base tilted in the same way. The defect did not depend on her hands, and her expression changed before they understood how to correct it.

Elias knew that difference. It was the small, fierce relief of discovering that something failed even when the person trying was not the one accused of incompetence.

The cloth helped. It was not enough.

Lise removed a pebble. Then she put it back.

“If I only make it lighter because it doesn't work, it isn't the model I'm supposed to bring.”

Elias looked at his sister's fingers. A dry streak of glue marked her thumb. She had already tried enough not to need another adult explaining how to begin.

“Tell me what you've attempted.”

Lise began with the first frame, which had bent, then showed him where she had shortened a strip. She did not fill every pause. Sometimes she asked him to raise the cup or mark a distance, and Elias complied without replacing her hands with something she could not take to the interview.

The solution came after a particularly bad attempt: the cup turned, the pebbles fell, and one rolled beneath the cupboard.

Lise said a word Adrien, in the next room, chose not to hear.

Elias knelt. He saw the pebble between a leg of the cupboard and the wall. Reaching it through a small passage would have been easy. Instead he slipped a spoon handle into the gap and rolled it out.

The pebble had scored a line in the dust beneath the furniture. Elias wiped it with the cloth before returning it to the cup. Lise held the load and tilted it slowly to understand which side had begun to tip.

“Do you want to try without moving the base?” he asked.

She looked at him, then restored the frame to its previous position. Elias held the string while his sister let the cup hang close enough to the surface not to spill the stones again. The rotation returned, smaller and clearer.

Lise followed it with her fingers without immediately correcting it. She had spent so long preventing the model from going wrong that she had not properly watched which direction it wanted to go wrong in.

“If I don't hold it, it'll fall.”

“We can hold it without straightening it.”

She tried. Elias kept supporting the frame and let the cup end up tilted. The lower edge indicated a problem they could not solve by making the knot stronger.

Lise set the load down. She took a moment to study the string's line from above, then close up. Her brother added no further suggestion. He had done enough by proposing a way to see the defect; the answer she would need at the interview should not arrive as an order to follow.

When he rose, Lise was already changing the point of support.

“It's not just the weight. I'm hanging it crooked.”

Lise dismantled the suspension point without taking the whole frame apart. Elias held the cup while she freed the string. She wound it round a finger to preserve its length, then checked the position she had marked during the previous trial in her notebook.

“If I move this, the load will arrive lower.”

Elias looked at the destination. The table edge was no precipice to avoid at any cost, but the model had to set the cup where Lise had decided. They could not declare a test successful by changing its destination whenever it proved inconvenient.

“We can see how much before fastening it.”

She nodded. She tied a temporary knot and asked him to check it only because she did not want to confuse the suspension problem with that of loosening string. Elias turned it between his fingers and returned it without replacing it with the knot he would have tied.

They resumed their positions. The empty cup moved with less swinging. Lise stopped before the end, seeking the point where the base tended to lose balance.

“Don't let go yet.”

Elias maintained the support. The string pressed against the first joint of his finger. By not seeking a shortcut, he was beginning to feel the real fatigue of such a small task: holding one point steady while someone else learned to move another.

His sister retied the suspension. When she told him to release it, he did so slowly, with an absurd fear of spoiling the test precisely because he wanted it to succeed.

The cup arrived. For an instant neither spoke. Success, still without a load, was too fragile to deserve a celebration. Lise took the pebbles and counted them in her palm, letting each find its place before trying again.

The model worked first without pebbles and then with all three. The cup crossed the table's distance, swaying, but did not tip.

Lise did not cheer. She did it again.

Elias realized he had held his breath more during the second attempt than the first.

“Now,” she said.

She looked at her brother, then at her notebook.

“If they ask how I did it, I can answer.”

That, more than the load arriving intact, made him understand the original request.

Lise marked the new point on the frame. She did so before removing the load, while she could still recognize which part of the movement had changed. Elias watched her return from the trial to the page and understood that he could not have described such simple work properly had he recorded only the result.

“If they ask what didn't work?”

“I'll tell them.”

“Even the pebble under the cupboard?”

“If that's what they want to know, yes. Otherwise I'd start with the suspension point.”

She laughed and took the cup from his hands. She put it where nobody would mistake it for one to drink from. The defect in the rim remained visible; successful transport had not repaired the rest of the object.

Elias gathered the fallen string and wound it too tightly round his palm. Lise showed him how to store it without forcing the next person to undo a knot. That small lesson pleased him more than he would admit.

“May the laborer come back when needed?” he asked.

“If he doesn't give advice while holding the load.”

“That's a severe condition.”

“It's a delicate model.”

Elias set the cord in the box. For one morning, his help had possessed a substance he could describe without mentioning the test, an ability, or what he feared losing.

They gathered the scraps. Elias found one of his notes beneath the box and moved it without reading. He had agreed his time at the workshop; he was not secretly stealing hours from the drawing promised to Helren.

“Will you be back tonight?” Lise asked.

“I think so.”

She waited.

“If anything changes, I'll let you know before I leave you waiting,” he clarified.

“That's better.”

Adrien reappeared with two pieces of bread. He placed the first before Lise and offered the other to his son.

“For the director of the works,” he told the girl. “And for the unskilled labor.”

Elias accepted his qualification without appeal.

At the door Lise returned his calipers. “I'm not taking these to the interview.”

“You need them to check the model.”

“I need you not to spend all morning looking for them.”

Elias pocketed them. The glue on his cuff had begun to harden. Reaching the workshop, he found it with his nail while thinking how to tell Livia about his demotion to laborer.

At the workshop he found Livia waiting with the program for the twenty-third. She did not reproach him for being late, because he was not late.

“How did it go?”

Elias showed her a glue stain on his cuff.

“I've received a qualification lower than my previous one.”

Livia laughed before asking what he meant. For once he had time to tell her without turning it into a lesson for someone else.

Chapter 20 — The Son

Etienne came on the morning of the twenty-second and did not embrace him.

Alexander had had the reason for the request written clearly. He therefore lacked the comfort of surprise when his friend entered with the letter still in his hand.

“Where is my father?”

“The court is handling the measures concerning him. It isn't a decision left to the household guards.”

“I didn't ask who decides.”

Edran indicated the chair. Etienne remained standing.

The previous evening a representative had received the complaint, heard the available statements, and taken charge of the acts required for custody. Alexander had supplied the son with the office's contact details for inquiries about his father. He did not know every subsequent conversation, and did not invent them.

“You can ask that office about him. I haven't ordered them to deny you information because you're his son.”

Etienne looked at the chair and finally sat. He laid the letter on his knee without letting go.

“You accuse him of poisoning you.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

Alexander moved his chair to the other side of the table, leaving free the one Etienne had chosen. He did not want the comfort of sitting beside him as though yesterday's intimacy remained something he could rely on without asking.

The young man held the folded letter in one hand. He had opened it so often that its edge had lost its stiffness. Edran recognized the words dictated to Maurice and, for the first time, tried to imagine them arriving in the son's house without his own voice to explain them.

“Were you told anything else before it arrived?”

Etienne looked up.

“Do you want to know whom I've spoken to?”

The question stopped him. Context alone had converted a request for understanding into possible surveillance.

“I wanted to understand what you already know. You need tell me only what you wish.”

“I know my father came here and didn't come home.”

He did not raise his voice. The fact sounded worse that way, deprived of the protection an argument would have offered both of them. Edran felt tempted to say how absurd it was to ask for the peaceful return of a man who had attempted murder. Etienne had not yet asked that. He had stated what he knew.

Alexander picked up the documents. The gesture cost him the quickest explanation: describe what had happened in the room and expect his friend to accept his certainty. If he wanted Etienne to keep distinguishing facts from friendship, he must begin by letting him examine the former without promising the latter.

Alexander showed him the copy of the note, the delivery record, and the reference to the residue. He did not arrange the papers as though the number of sheets should become guilt.

“This proves he sent a gift,” Etienne said.

“That's true.”

The young man looked up, surprised not to have to fight over that distinction too.

Alexander recounted the conversation. The preparation before delivery. The intention to remove him. The project for a provisional administration around Celine.

“He told you that?”

“I compelled him to answer.”

Etienne remained still. His thumb tore a small strip from the edge of the letter.

“Compelled how?”

Edran could have hidden behind a phrase vague enough to resemble an ordinary threat. He had not done something ordinary.

“I acted on his will. He had to choose to recount his actions accurately.”

“You can do that?”

“Yes.”

Etienne rose.

For a few moments Alexander thought he meant to leave. Instead, the young man went to the window, as though he needed a part of the room the explanation had not already occupied.

“Since when?”

“I hadn't spoken to you about it before.”

Etienne waited. The question remained without the date it had asked for.

“Since before yesterday?”

“I possess that ability. I did not use it on you.”

“That's the same answer.”

Edran looked at the place where his friend should have sat. For the first time the duke's memories suggested no continuation: nobody in those shared days had needed to ask to whom the desire to speak belonged.

The young man's hand settled on the windowsill. It stayed there, away from the bell, as though he had chosen a part of the room where his next action was not yet obvious.

Alexander lifted a hand from the table and put it down. Etienne was not looking at him, but the window reflected part of the room. Edran saw his own reflection seated and his friend's still before the glass. Between them the water had not been touched.

“Yesterday you asked me about work,” his friend said.

“Yes.”

“And I answered.”

Edran nodded before realizing he could not see him. He answered aloud as well.

“I remember wanting to speak.”

The sentence reached him with all the cruelty of the next question, still unspoken. Remembering the desire was no proof of its origin. Edran could not repair that discovery simply by insisting he had not used the power.

The young man looked at the glass on the table. He had not drunk. Alexander noticed and did not offer more water as a way of appearing harmless.

“You can leave whenever you wish,” he said.

Etienne turned his head slightly. For an instant his gaze hardened into something resembling no memory.

“Can you make that seem like my choice too?”

Edran received the question without defending himself against its harshness. He possessed an ability that made the fear reasonable. He would not invent a comforting limit merely to be less feared in this room.

“I am not exercising that ability on you,” he replied. “There is no ordinary gesture by which I alone can prove it.”

Etienne looked outside again. His fingers had stopped pressing on the sill. It was not restored trust. Perhaps it was an attempt to decide what remained possible even without obtaining all of it.

Etienne turned his back on him.

“Have you done it to me too?”

“No.”

The silence that followed was the exact consequence of that ability, not the ignorance of someone who feared it.

“How am I supposed to know?”

Alexander did not search for a sentence to repair what a sentence alone could not.

“You can't establish it merely because I tell you.”

Etienne laughed once, without amusement.

“At least there's that.”

He returned to the chair but did not sit. The way he looked at his friend had changed; Edran accepted it without calling it ingratitude.

“I'm not asking you to believe every word obtained that way is already sufficient evidence for a court,” he said. “I've indicated what can be checked outside me. The residue. The delivery. The draft your father says he keeps.”

“And if they don't find it?”

“I won't say they found it.”

Etienne pressed the letter into his palm until the paper yielded in a new crease.

“My father has known you since you were a child.”

Alexander remembered. He did not need to ask the young man to make the sentence less painful.

“I know.”

“He let me come here.”

This time the accusation no longer seemed directed only at him. Edran let Etienne formulate it without immediately offering him a place on his own side.

“I want to speak to him,” Etienne said at last.

“I won't prevent you. The conditions of the meeting aren't mine alone to decide.”

“I don't want you making him say what I'm supposed to hear.”

Alexander nodded.

“I won't do that to prepare your conversation.”

Etienne slipped the letter into his inside pocket. Only then did he notice the note on the table, and he studied it longer. Alexander did not ask him to recognize the handwriting: the young man had come as a son, not a tool.

The young man reached toward the note and stopped before touching it. Alexander told him it was a copy; the original was kept separately. Etienne did not use the explanation as a reason to take it.

“My father writes like that when he thanks someone for dinner, too.”

Edran looked at the handwriting. The name had ceased to be merely the first useful point in reconstructing the bottle's route. It had existed in Etienne's life before any of this, on sheets nobody had thought of keeping as evidence.

“You do not have to recognize it for me.”

“I recognize it anyway.”

The young man's hand fell. Of someone more distant, Edran could have asked a distinction between knowledge and feeling. In this room, the request would have been savage. Etienne was already trying, without yet deciding whom to grant the right to listen.

A footstep sounded in the corridor. His friend turned toward the door, then back. Edran understood that the house no longer seemed somewhere Etienne could enter without announcing himself. Everyone he met on leaving would know something about his father before knowing what to say to him.

Alexander could have called Maurice and requested an especially discreet departure. He let Etienne choose when to rise. He had already received enough decisions made by others around his family.

“The application for the position...” he began.

“You can still submit it. It doesn't become a fault because of what your father is accused of.”

“I'm not asking a favor.”

“Nor am I offering one in exchange for agreement.”

That precision seemed to hurt him more than a generous promise. Perhaps because it genuinely left him the possibility of not being grateful.

“What does Celine know?”

The question came when Edran thought the conversation almost over. He looked at his friend and recognized the effort with which he had stopped, at least for a moment, asking only about his father and himself.

“I must speak to her directly.”

Etienne lowered his head. Dust from the letter remained on his knee, a pale mark he had not seen.

“She mustn't think I came yesterday to ask how you were on his behalf.”

“I do not think that.”

“You already have more things to base your decision on.”

Edran let the words deprive him of his prepared reassurance. Celine would learn of the father's visit, her own possible succession, the plan of someone who would have spoken to her as a minor incapable of wanting anything. A shared name would make it easy to unite responsibilities that remained distinct.

“I shall tell her the accusation concerns your father. I shall not ask her to consider you guilty because you are his son.”

Etienne nodded. He did not ask him to force her to trust him; perhaps he understood how terrible such a request would sound here.

He took the letter from his inner pocket, opened it once more, and folded it along a new line. The sheet did not return to the shape in which it had arrived. He put it inside his jacket nonetheless and ran his palm over the cloth.

Alexander stayed seated as his friend turned. It was a respect for his space small enough to deserve no thanks. Remaining where he was still cost him, with all those memories showing him how to close the distance.

When Etienne looked toward the window again, Edran stopped seeking the gesture that would end the conversation well. It could end badly and still have the right to end.

Etienne reached the door. His hand remained on the handle.

“I don't know if I want to come back next week.”

In Alexander's memory there were dozens of ways to keep his friend with a joke. Edran left them where they were.

“You don't have to decide now.”

Alone, he did not destroy the copy of the note. He put it away in the file.

What had broken was not glass, and his hand had not been the one to touch it.

Chapter 21 — A Small Key

The key ceased to be on the bench and appeared on the nearby shelf.

Edith reached out at once, then stopped before touching it. She had seen no throw. The metal had not crossed the space in the way her body continued to expect.

“May I take it?”

Elias nodded.

The key was as cold as before. Edith checked both sides, almost as though she had been handed an imitation prepared for a performance. Elias knew that expression. The first time, he had worn it before his own hands.

“Is it a workshop key?”

“For a small box. Marcel knows we're using it.”

“Good. I'd have been sorry to discover afterward that I'd touched some fundamental secret.”

Livia moved a chair for her. Afternoon light slanted through the window, exposing the part of the floor where Elias had abandoned a ruler the day before. He picked it up before anyone tripped: not every workshop hazard deserved an Effigy.

Edith had come to agree on her contribution for the twenty-third. Before beginning, she had asked Elias what he could actually do. She did not want to discover it while an injured person waited to be moved.

“I can transfer this,” he said, indicating the key. “Not you.”

“And if you make a person lighter?”

“They don't stop having a spine.”

Edith looked at him with fresh interest.

“Can I have you repeat that tomorrow?”

He smiled.

He had not performed the transfer to demonstrate a rank he did not possess. The two points were nearby, reached, and connected; the object was small. Even so, he felt the exercise's cost behind his eyes. He took water and did not offer a second attempt merely to see surprise on the nurse's face again.

Edith did not put the key back on the bench immediately. She held it on her open palm, studying the point from which it had vanished. Elias awaited acknowledgement of the power and realized from her next question that the nurse was thinking of something else.

“What if I move while you do it?”

“I didn't take your hand as the destination. The point was the shelf.”

She nodded. She was not seeking a way to make things difficult; she wanted to imagine the situation without remaining still in the one position that would let him succeed.

“And if someone needs the object you're taking away?”

Elias looked at the key. It would have been easy to speak of impossible thefts, locks, things glimpsed for an instant. His ability's conditions were not those of every adventure someone could imagine on hearing the word transfer.

“I have to reach what I exercise the ability on. Wanting something whose location I don't know isn't enough.”

Edith repeated the gesture with which she had withdrawn her fingers. She did it slowly, then left her hand on the bench, well away from the shelf.

“So I shouldn't expect you to put a tool in my hand while I think it's coming from somewhere else.”

“You can expect me to warn you.”

She smiled. It was the first question whose useful answer required no explanation of a magical limit.

Elias realized his mind kept seeking a second, more convincing demonstration. He had acknowledged the cost and drunk water, yet others' surprise remained a pleasure difficult to abandon. He stayed seated. His spiritual reserve was not the only thing he would consume if every conversation became a request for admiration.

Marcel passed through the workshop and asked whether the key had finished traveling. Edith raised it. He indicated where to leave it afterward; he did not make it so precious an object that their discussion must stop.

For Elias, that naturalness remained harder to learn than the transfer. The ability had entered his life as an immense promise, but a key could have been moved without deserving the entire morning.

Livia had brought a sheet with three requests. The first concerned what Edith could say about Etienne's recovery. The second proposed a brief lesson on helping an injured person somewhere without a doctor. The third was Elias's, added at the bottom in quicker handwriting: establish whether the apparatus could be useful for an evacuation.

Edith read everything and placed a finger on the third line.

“What do you already know?”

Elias described the two points, the distance within the same lot, the limited duration. He did not use the word salvation to sell a passage not yet tested for the purpose.

“And if someone can't stand?”

“We won't test it with an injured person.”

“That was the condition I was about to ask for.”

Livia added a note in the margin, then stopped her pen.

“Writing it down isn't enough. If something changes tomorrow, we stop.”

No patient had been invited. The planned weight was an inert, measured load. Marcel would direct the check, keeping the ordinary exit available.

Edith put the key down before Elias.

“For the first request, I want to speak to Etienne before the meeting. I can't bring anything he hasn't authorized me to show.”

“Even if it would help us?”

The question escaped before Elias could make it less revealing.

“Especially then.”

Livia drew a line beneath the request, not through Edith's name. They were not erasing a person for refusing a service.

Edith returned to the first request and had Elias describe what he wanted from the account. He began with the measurements, the bench's position, and the error attributed to preparation. He stopped on realizing she had witnessed none of those three things.

“I want nobody to be able to say we're hiding the injury.”

“I'm not hiding it.”

“I know. I meant…”

She waited. Elias wanted Livia to intervene with a better sentence, but the copyist gave him time to distinguish his need from the service he requested.

“I'm afraid it'll look as though the recovery is convenient for me. That I use it only when it suits me.”

Edith leaned against the chair's back. She did not call the fear absurd or particularly noble.

“Then when we speak to him, we let him decide what information to make available. That doesn't establish who is right about the bench.”

Elias nodded. He had found someone ready to do something for the dispute, but not to become an answer to his fear of resembling whoever had prepared the first offer.

Livia changed the request's heading. She put the concrete task first, leaving aside the reason they wanted it. The page became simpler to read. Elias could have shown it to Etienne without also burdening him with understanding all his reasons.

Edith checked the time. She had to remember her shift even while enjoying herself here. Elias saw the movement and stopped adding a new question every time the previous one was answered. He wanted her to return because the meeting had been useful, rather than discover too late that she had lost part of her day.

The nurse rested her notebook on the table. The cover's edge was worn from being pushed into a pocket too narrow for it. She showed Livia a page without clinical information: the ink had spread where water had reached it.

“Could you have prevented that?”

“For a short interval, with access to the surface. Not on all your notebooks forever.”

Livia took a scrap, wrote a few words, and invested its surface with power. She held it against the damp edge of a cloth. The letters retained their boundaries where water should have spread them through the paper.

When she removed the sheet and let the exercise end, Edith examined it without easy enthusiasm.

“During a long shift I might need it while you're doing something else.”

“Yes.”

“Then I still need a better cover.”

Elias laughed. Livia handed her the scrap, amused too.

For a few moments none of them spoke about the meeting. Edith described a uniform with pockets so tight she had to carry her scissors where she should never have needed to search for them. Livia compared that cruelty with a dress that would not let her sit down without losing a pencil. Elias concluded he had never properly valued his waistcoat.

The protected scrap passed from hand to hand. Elias watched it tilt toward the window; it was the same writing he had seen appear beneath Livia's pen, still legible where damp should have broken it apart.

Edith asked to hold it beside her notebook's cover without wetting the pages. The copyist allowed it after reminding her that the exercise had ended. The nurse chiefly compared dimensions: she wanted to understand how much ordinary surface a protection would need to cover for her to carry it even without a Bearer beside her.

“I bought it too small,” she said of the notebook. “To fit in my pocket.”

“And now the pocket ruins the notebook,” Elias observed.

“How perceptive. I'll gladly entrust you with the next inventory of my poor decisions.”

Livia laughed and removed a ruler before Elias could actually try measuring the cover. They found themselves discussing where things belonged on clothes and how often their designers imagined hands that were always free.

Edith tried slipping the notebook into her pocket. It caught at the edge; she jerked it out and set it before Marcel. “A cover that survives this. Without calling a Bearer whenever it rains.” Livia turned the notebook to inspect its spine.

It was Edith who returned the conversation to the key.

“Sometimes I think I'd like to learn Heart.”

She did not say it as a question addressed to a recruiter. She sounded almost annoyed by how easily the desire took the shape of a solution.

Elias waited.

“Then someone tells me I'd heal more people, and I get angry. As though every ability I might acquire already belonged to whoever would need it.”

Livia did not tell her she should therefore abandon the idea.

“You aren't coming tomorrow to promise that,” she said.

Edith nodded and closed the notebook.

Marcel came looking for the key before closing time. Edith showed it to him, and he indicated the small box it belonged to. The nurse put the metal into the lock, turned it, and watched the lid rise just as it would have a moment before the transfer.

“I wanted to check it could still do this,” she said.

Elias laughed. He had not considered the demonstration from the perspective of someone who wanted a usable object back, not merely an impossibility.

Marcel closed the box and left the key on her palm again so she could compare it with the point from which it had vanished. They activated nothing. The supernatural demonstration was already over; now Edith was trying to bring her ordinary perceptions together.

Edith put down the key and withdrew her fingers once more. “Now I expect it to vanish even if you aren't looking.”

“It won't.”

“I know.” She picked it up and put it where Marcel had indicated. “My hand hasn't understood yet.”

Elias looked at his own fingers. For him the stretch between bench and shelf had been an accessible route, a small target, a recognizable effort. Edith had seen the world skip a step. He smiled before finding a better explanation.

They agreed on the time, the contact, and the staggered arrival. When she left, Elias returned the key to Marcel and removed from the program the idea of a demonstration with someone needing rescue.

The test would show a passage. It would have no right to pretend it was already a treatment.

Chapter 22 — Celine's Room

When Alexander arrived, Celine was moving a chair.

It was not heavy enough to require help. Edran stopped on the threshold instead of demonstrating how little it would cost him to lift it with one hand.

“May I come in?”

The girl looked at the chair, then at him.

“Yes. That one's not for you.”

“I'd already prepared to be rejected by it.”

Celine indicated another seat near the window. She had rearranged the things on her desk; books occupied less space than papers. A house continued changing even when its nominal owner did not know how to look at it.

Sabine took her leave after asking Celine whether she still needed her. No gesture from the duke canceled the question.

Alexander waited for the door to close.

“I want to tell you why Ravelle was detained.”

“Someone told me he'd felt unwell.”

“That isn't it.”

The girl stopped winding a ribbon around a bundle of sheets.

“I thought so.”

Celine set down the ribbon. She noticed one end trapped beneath a book and pulled gently, persisting even after realizing it would not come free that way.

Alexander could have lifted the volume for her. He waited. She took it in both hands, freed the ribbon, and put it back with excessive care.

“Is this something I need to know, or something you want to tell me so I'll behave?”

She had prepared the question. Edran heard it in the precision of her voice, less certain at the end than at the beginning.

“It concerns you. If you do not understand what I say, you can stop me.”

“I don't need permission not to understand.”

This time Alexander smiled without trying to disarm her with a joke. Celine moved the book back to the pile and sat on the chair's edge, her feet far enough back to prevent comfort.

Edran looked at the window. Beyond the glass, the world remained a clear day in May. The news would grow no less monstrous for being spoken in a tidy room. Nor did his capacity to bear it entitle him to choose how much fear his sister should feel.

He began with the moment Alexander had drunk. He omitted details he did not know and images whose only purpose would be to make her brother's pain more vivid. Celine listened while watching his mouth, as though that was where she might learn when the worst part was coming.

Edran told her about the gift, the admitted adulteration, and the accusation delivered to the court. He did not describe a toxic formula he had no reason to teach her. He did not turn a confession obtained by himself into a judgment already pronounced by others.

Celine listened until Etienne's name.

“Did he know?”

“I have no evidence against him. His father said he didn't know. I don't want you to have to treat friendship as evidence of guilt.”

“I didn't ask what I should do.”

The correction reached him with a sharpness her brother would have recognized.

“You're right.”

Celine took up the ribbon. She tied a poor knot, undid it, and left it on the table.

“Was he responsible for Mama and Papa too?”

“No. I haven't discovered an attack against them. The Bellance doesn't become something different because of what Ravelle did afterward.”

He did not promise her certainty gathered from every corner of the world. He gave her the fact they had and added no new fear to make the culprit greater.

The girl nodded once. Then she looked outside.

“If you'd died, would he have decided?”

“He would have tried to put himself forward to lead a temporary administration. He had no right to become duke. He hadn't already secured everyone's support.”

Celine shifted backwards and finally rested against the chair. The movement loosened a shoe; she put it back on without looking.

“When he came here, he didn't seem to want anything.”

Edran remembered the baron's visits through the duke's memories. It would have been easy to reread every courtesy as evidence they should have seen. It would have handed Celine the impossible task of never trusting a polite person again.

“He could behave courteously and have an intention we did not know.”

“Then it's no use.”

“It helps to know what it can prove. Being kind to you gives him no right over your decisions.”

She looked at him and asked whether the title would have protected her. The question struck him with the savage simplicity of her age: possessing something great and discovering that it did not guarantee the very thing for which everyone seemed to want it.

Alexander distinguished the right of succession from the people who would try to wield influence around her. He did not promise every adult would care about her freedom. He told her whom she could approach, and that she need not learn to make herself heard in a single morning.

“Did you know how?”

Her brother's memory offered enough hesitation to prevent a heroic answer.

“I pretended I knew how before I managed it in many things.”

“You still do.”

Edran agreed. The girl had recognized something true without knowing who he was. For a moment, that seemed less painful than being perfectly mistaken for a dead man.

Celine looked back at the pile of papers. There were matters in her life that did not need to interest a baron to deserve space. The possibility of losing them all beneath a more solemn name left her silent.

“And me?”

“You would have been first in the succession.”

Celine knew that. She had not asked the question to receive the proper name for one of her rights.

Edran let enough time pass to understand what she was asking him.

“He would have tried to speak for you. To make you seem safe while taking decisions he wanted for himself.”

The girl turned toward him.

“Would Yselle have let him?”

“He didn't even own the choice of who could defend you. Your aunt and the family would have had a voice. You could have asked to be heard.”

Those possibilities did not bring a dead brother back to life. Edran realized it before using them as balm.

“But he tried to leave you alone,” he added.

Celine lowered her eyes. Her hand above the ribbon trembled faintly.

Alexander remembered men who had called their own convenience order, a door that opened only from outside, a child too angry to cry before those counting out his hours. It was not the same life. The resemblance was enough to make him wish Ravelle no longer had a voice.

He did not bring that desire into the room as a gift for Celine.

“Are you really well now?” she asked.

“The body has recovered.”

The girl stared at him.

“I didn't only ask about your body.”

Edran leaned back. He could not give her the whole truth without tearing away another part of the house.

“I'm angry. And today I was frightened by how simple it would be to become cruel.”

Celine did not answer like an adult satisfied with a well-worded confession.

“I don't want to know everything you could do to him.”

“All right.”

“I want to know if someone comes in here.”

Alexander called Sabine and asked her to agree with Celine which visits should be confirmed beforehand. He did not announce protective imprisonment, nor leave his sister the impossible task of guaranteeing every access herself.

Sabine sat beside the desk without taking it over. Celine asked whether she would need an escort even to go from one room to another. The governess looked at Alexander, then back at the girl.

“That is not what we are agreeing.”

“When everyone's worried, things change without anyone telling me.”

“Tomorrow I want to go to the library,” Celine said. “If Teren thinks someone might enter, he tells me before I reach the door.”

Sabine looked at Alexander. He felt the whole room awaiting his yes and thought how easily protection could become the rest of this girl's life.

“Teren will explain the changes to access,” he said. “The library hasn't been closed.”

Celine pulled the ribbon until the book released it. She set it on the table without looking to the governess to learn whether she had obtained enough.

Celine turned the ribbon in her hands. She passed it beneath a stack of papers and tied a simple knot, this time without pulling too tight.

“Can I finish this first?”

Sabine said yes. Celine did not seem to have obtained an exceptional concession; she had asked for time to finish her own work.

Alexander looked at the chair his sister had moved at the beginning. Deciding a piece of furniture's place must have seemed easier than interrupting those who, in the household's name, decided a person's place. It cost him to realize how closely his arrival could have resembled the same thing had he kept speaking without listening.

Sabine suggested Teren explain the changes in understandable words. Celine agreed, but asked that the meeting not occupy the whole morning.

“I wanted to finish this,” she said, indicating the papers.

Edran looked at the untied ribbon.

“May I help?”

Celine passed him a pile of sheets.

“Put these in order. Not according to what you think matters most.”

Alexander accepted the stack of papers without beginning to read their contents. Celine showed him the small mark establishing their order. He need only follow it. When he found a sheet that seemed more urgent, he left it where it belonged rather than promote it to the room's principal problem.

She took the first arranged page and checked it. She indicated a folded margin. Edran smoothed it with his palm, waiting for the paper to flatten enough to lie in the pile.

“Can Etienne write to me?” Celine asked.

“I shall not prevent you receiving a letter because of the accusation against his father.”

“I wasn't asking whether you'll read it first.”

“I was not saying I would.”

She held his gaze long enough to find something usable in the answer. Then she handed him another group of sheets.

“This goes after that.”

For several minutes they worked without speaking of the baron. The silence remained tense, but it allowed a gesture to be completed. Alexander understood he must offer her that too: time not constantly transformed into another check that she was all right.

They stayed at the desk. After a few minutes she asked him to move the chair she had moved at the beginning.

“You said it wasn't for me.”

“It isn't. You're not supposed to sit in it.”

He lifted it with both hands, as a man would for whom a chair was still a chair. He put it where she indicated.

Celine checked the pile her brother had arranged and moved it into the space the chair had cleared. She did not thank him as though he had restored all her lost safety; she simply passed him the sheet left out.

The girl returned to her work. She was not calm, and she had not forgiven the world.

She had simply begun choosing where to put things again.

Chapter 23 — An Opening

The wall did not become transparent.

Elias would have found something like that easier to look at. Transparency still belonged to the world of windows. What appeared before the service entrance instead placed two locations side by side without granting the space between them the right to intervene.

Marcel had aligned the marks and turned the lever. The click had been small, almost offensive in its ordinariness.

Elias had prepared the load on the floor to keep the passage clear. The box closed with an ordinary catch. He opened it once, counted the pieces with Livia, and closed it again. He wished the precaution resembled everyday work less closely, as though a miracle deserved preparation made of the same extraordinary substance.

Marcel asked him to check that the ordinary route remained clear. Elias walked to the antechamber and returned empty-handed. The hall was where he had left it; the object they were about to use was not necessary to reach it.

That was precisely what made the gesture difficult to explain. A distance could be short enough to walk and real enough to justify seeing what happened when an apparatus treated it differently.

Livia returned to the destination and told him not to bring the load until he saw the landing place clear. Elias nodded. They did not rehearse the surprise of meeting by pretending something beyond them had summoned them. They had agreed a time, a place, and concrete work to observe.

The master positioned himself beside the lever. His hands bore decades of work, but the rank he lacked did not appear between his fingers to reward his experience. If the check revealed something needing correction, they would have to stop and seek expertise neither yet possessed.

Elias waited for Marcel to finish. The box's weight still rested on the floor. He would have time to lift it after the opening; he need not make waiting harder to prove himself ready.

First he had examined the imbued teeth and accessible surfaces, looking for deformation or wear that would require them to stop. Elias had assisted without confusing that check with the recharging neither of them could perform.

Now, beyond the opening, Elias could see the antechamber.

He knew how many steps it took to reach it by the ordinary route. He had checked the distance, the changes of direction, the location within the same lot. None of that knowledge was false. The passage nevertheless allowed another action.

“The load first,” Marcel said.

The test box contained ordinary pieces already weighed. Not a commercial sample, not medicines taken from the clinic, and not a person who had accepted risk merely because they were ill.

Elias lifted it. They had agreed on a single transit into the room, without returning through the opening; everyone would leave by the normal door. There was no need to approach the limits for people or baggage to verify a concrete use.

Livia was already on the other side. She had reached the room by the ordinary route with the material needed for the meeting. She did not gesture for him to hurry.

For a moment, he saw the hall side and the warehouse side as two perspectives his mind tried in vain to arrange in one drawing. This was no opening cut through the wall's thickness. Beyond lay a place he knew how to reach by walking another way.

Elias took the box in both hands. He did not lighten it to make the test more elegant. He wanted to know the agreed transit, not one improved by another exercise they would later have to remember to distinguish.

He lifted the load until its bottom cleared the edge. His grip pressed near the cut, but the gauze stayed put. Behind him, Marcel merely said he was watching.

The box projected beyond the boundary. Elias saw his right hand before the antechamber and still felt his left heel on the warehouse floor. He could not find the length that should have connected those two things.

Instinctively his body sought a step. He stopped his foot before raising it too high.

“The floor is there,” Livia said.

He could see it. He had to persuade his knee. He shifted his weight, gripped the box, and took a step as long as one before an ordinary door. The antechamber floor met him without a jolt. No resistance, no gap to cross: the rest of his body followed while part of his mind kept seeking the missing route.

The smell changed. Enclosed wood, lamp oil. He found himself laughing breathlessly and almost sought the table with the box's bottom too soon.

Livia indicated the clear surface. Elias set down the load and looked back. Marcel remained on the other side. The distance he remembered between them had not diminished; yet they could speak a few paces apart.

He turned.

Marcel was close enough to speak to him. For a moment the closeness made him forget that his mentor would have to take a different route if the connection failed.

“How do you feel?”

Elias set down the box before answering.

“Whole.”

“A start. Be more precise.”

No nausea. No loss of grip during the passage. He had not felt the apparatus drawing on his body, and using it had not given him the ability to open a threshold elsewhere.

Livia checked the box's contents with him. The pieces had arrived. The fact deserved a simple sentence.

When Marcel closed the opening, Elias saw the previous location return to occupy his side of the entrance entirely.

It closed before the ten minutes were up. The closure did not restore the charge.

In the register his mentor wrote the date: May twenty-third. Two openings used since the apparatus had been reactivated; one remaining from the initial reserve.

Elias remained before the register after the master finished writing. The first use's date did not change. The new one had consumed a possibility even though they had closed it before the maximum time.

Marcel left the pen beside the number one. Elias still felt the step in his legs. He looked at the lever, imagined repeating it, then looked back at the register.

“Next time we have to really need it.”

“We needed this time,” Marcel said.

Livia was checking the box. Elias joined her and found every piece where they had put it. The vanished opening had taken none of them away; of the reserve, though, only one possibility remained.

Livia asked him to move the box out of the entrance route. He lifted it beside the indicated chair. The load had passed from one side to the other without making any person present the owner of the space.

He remembered the warehouse worker and how he had asked them to leave the passage clear. The resemblance did not diminish the miracle. It made it habitable: after an impossible thing happened, someone still needed to enter without tripping over what they had left on the ground.

Marcel returned by the ordinary door. He took the time the route Elias had walked earlier required. That minimal delay showed precisely how the apparatus had changed their gesture, and how little of the rest of the world it had erased.

Elias sat only when the master reached the hall. Edith's place remained empty until the time she had agreed. Nobody filled it with an explanation about a lack of enthusiasm.

The second line mattered more than the wonder Elias would have liked to preserve without accounting.

Livia stayed beside Elias while the surface became merely a surface again. She asked whether he wanted to sit, and he said no. Not because he was not tired: he needed a few ordinary steps before explaining what had happened to his body.

He walked to the chair and back. The sound of shoes on the floor made him feel the time the gesture required with almost irritating clarity. The passage had not taught him to walk faster.

“I expected to notice more of the moment in between,” he said.

“More than you can describe?”

Elias considered the question. He had seen the place his load and foot reached change. He had lost no stretch of memory to fill afterward with solemn images.

“I expected it to be difficult to cross.”

“We prepared it so we wouldn't discover the difficulty while you were doing it.”

He nodded. The absence of disaster did not erase the preceding work. He put his palm to his chest: his heart was still faster than necessary, without proving injury or cosmic revelation.

Livia let him make the gesture and returned to the sheet. She asked him to describe the transit before listening to the account she could give from the other side. Elias found words shorter than he had imagined. Lift, advance, set down. They seemed unworthy of the wonder until he realized they let him recognize what he had actually done.

He wrote the three actions and the limits of his observation. He sought no adjective capable of restoring all his astonishment. That part would have to remain in his body a little longer.

Marcel then arrived by the ordinary entrance. Edith came last, at the time she had confirmed. She left her coat in the antechamber and stopped before the floor where the connection had recently opened.

“Is it over already?”

“The transit check is,” Elias answered. “The meeting isn't.”

She nodded. She did not seem offended at having missed a private spectacle.

They sat at the table. Twelve places, four people, one box Marcel moved beside his chair rather than leaving it at the center like an idol.

Elias opened the program.

He wanted to say something that would distinguish the meeting from every other work conversation. He looked at Livia, who was searching for a pen that had fallen into her case's lining, and abandoned the solemn sentence.

“The first thing is to say what we haven't verified.”

Edith rested her forearms on the table.

“I like that beginning.”

They had not verified an immobilized casualty. Nor a crowd. Nor operation after recharging. Nor a demigod. The test demonstrated the transit completed under those conditions, and the group would not offer a greater service merely because the word passage allowed people to imagine it.

Marcel added that recharging required a qualified Astrolabe III operator able to examine those marks. Neither his experience nor Elias's enthusiasm bridged the gap.

“Helren can't do it,” Elias said.

“Archive isn't Astrolabe,” Livia replied. “Even when someone reads well.”

Edith smiled, then indicated her notebook.

“Etienne authorized me to describe the condition of the limb and the observations I made myself. Not to hand over his entire file.”

Elias immediately recognized what he wanted to ask: that it be enough. But recovery did not owe him the favor of absolution.

“What do you need to prepare a usable extract?”

Edith opened her notebook. The meeting truly began at that moment, when the most extraordinary object remained closed and a person could set the limits of her help.

Chapter 24 — What Cannot Be Bought

“I want you to write that he's healed too,” Elias said.

Edith did not take offense. Worse: she asked him why.

The table seemed wider as he searched for an answer that was not merely the desire to hear her say it.

“Because it's a fact.”

“It is. And what do you want to do with it?”

Livia held her pen above the page. She was not waiting her turn to rebuke him; Elias would have preferred an opponent that simple.

“Show that the consequences aren't what they wrote at the beginning.”

Edith showed him two different places in the notebook without turning it toward the others. Information unnecessary to the comparison remained on her side of the table.

“An injury can have been serious and later heal. I can describe both things. I can't write that nothing happened.”

“I'm not asking that.”

“Then we need to be able to read it without hearing it.”

Elias leaned back. A faint knock came from the corridor, an ordinary warehouse sound. For a moment he was grateful for a world that did not await his consent to continue.

“You're right,” he said.

Edith did not grant him a moral victory for admitting it.

“The request for the full file is refused. I can prepare the extract of authorized observations by the twenty-fifth. Etienne will read it before it's used.”

Livia recorded the two decisions separately.

“Would you like me to authenticate the comparison with what you show?”

“Yes. Not a judgment on treatment you didn't perform.”

“Not that.”

Elias looked at the page. For the first time it seemed an agreement that had not become poorer after the word refusal.

Edith closed the notebook's clinical section. The gesture separated the subjects better than a heading. Elias realized his body was still tense, ready to answer the first sentence that could be understood as abandoning his defense.

“Can I ask you something without putting it in the extract?” he said.

The nurse nodded.

“When he's healed, what changes in how you look at him?”

Edith asked whether he meant the work a person could resume or the account of what he had suffered. Elias could not choose. It was precisely the distinction he had kept skipping.

“I can be pleased that he uses his arm again,” she said. “I can also ask how he is without expecting the answer to make me feel competent.”

“If they ask why I wanted that extract?” Elias said.

“You tell them what you asked me.”

“It'll look as though I was trying to defend myself.”

Edith returned the sheet. “You were. I can tell you which part I won't give you.”

Elias reread the refusal. It took few words; beside it was the work she had accepted. He had brought his case to this room hoping the others would simplify it. Edith was leaving him an answer he could bear even if it were read aloud before the office.

Edith then offered to show them how to prepare an ordinary move of an injured person: space, supports, questions to ask, circumstances in which not to improvise. She did not say one lesson would make them all nurses.

“In exchange?” Marcel asked.

“Two small things. Livia helps me choose an ordinary cover for my notebook and shows me the limits of the one she can imbue with power. You tell me where I can put a bag without obstructing the exit.”

Marcel considered his own box beside the chair.

He moved it.

“I'm paying the second part immediately.”

All four laughed. Elias realized how close he had come to making laughter impossible through the need to give every gesture the weight of founding something.

The demonstration occupied a clear space in the room. They used an ordinary blanket and the box in place of a body. Edith stopped Elias when he tried to lighten it.

The weight beneath the blanket shifted as soon as he moved his fingers. The exercise had not begun: Elias had merely sought an easier grip on the cloth, thinking that lightening it would correct the hold.

Edith had him put the load back on the floor. He did so without activating the ability and rubbed his sound palm against his jacket.

“I wasn't trying to spare the others effort to show off.”

“I didn't say that.”

She waited for him to stop answering an accusation different from the correction received. Then she asked where the person would be if the box were a body, and which part of the movement he could not see.

Elias looked at the support, Marcel's hands, Livia's position. Four people eager to help could hide from one another the very point requiring attention. The problem did not become simpler because one possessed an extraordinary ability.

“I can't see the master's grip from here.”

Livia changed sides and told him what she could observe. They had added no strength to the load; they had found a voice able to warn them before support failed.

Edith asked him to repeat their stopping signal. Elias did so without turning it into a joke about his incompetence. That would strip seriousness from the question just as he began to understand it.

When they gripped the blanket again, the weight was unchanged. The scene no longer seemed a test of how well they could imitate a nurse. It was a first chance to discover which questions to ask her before believing themselves ready to move someone.

“First you need to know why you're moving it. A power can make a mistake easier.”

She did not have to humiliate him to teach him. She made him try the grip again and asked Livia to watch what Elias could not see from that position. The most useful result was discovering how little room remained when four people wanted to help in the same spot.

The blanket gathered badly on the first attempt. Elias had sought a grip strong enough not to slip and ended by depriving Marcel's hand of space. Livia pointed it out before they lifted the load.

Edith stopped them. She did not immediately put them in the correct positions; she asked each whether he knew what the other was waiting for.

The silence lasted long enough to make them laugh in embarrassment. Elias had awaited the master's signal. Marcel thought the young man was still finding his grip. Both watched the box, as though the object would announce when it felt ready to depart.

“Imagine it's a person,” Edith said. “Not speaking doesn't give you permission to improvise.”

Livia changed position to see the side hidden from the others. Elias described his intended action before moving. The nurse corrected wording too long to be useful while someone genuinely awaited help.

They repeated the gesture without activating abilities. The load retained its weight. This was no restriction imposed on power to make it less effective; it was how to understand which problem their hands were solving before choosing whether an ability could help.

On the second attempt Elias felt the moment the other's grip grew uncertain. He stopped instead of compensating by pulling harder. Marcel looked at him, recovered his purchase, and said they could resume.

The box reached the agreed spot. Edith did not declare them able to carry any injured person. She asked what they would do if the room were smaller or the load expressed pain. Elias could answer only the part concerning available space. He left the other questions on the sheet without feeling obliged to fill them with good intentions.

As they put the blanket away, he realized he was more tired than after watching the passage. He had had to attend to a person on the side he could not see, which was unlike sensing a coordinate in advance.

When they sat down again, Marcel discussed the commercial request. He showed only the parts of the disputed text he could share, without exposing Helren as a new resource belonging to the group.

Elias spoke Mael Vaur's name. He did not accompany it with a criminal title he did not know.

“I'd like to know if anyone knows him.”

“I don't,” Edith said.

Livia shook her head. Marcel did not add some knowledge he had withheld to test them.

The question remained unanswered. That was insufficient reason to ask every acquaintance in the city while carrying the room's name along with it.

“First we get the extract Helren agreed to provide,” Livia said. “Then we decide what's missing.”

Elias agreed. He had hoped a large table would produce more knowledge. That afternoon it was teaching him it could also produce better-declared ignorance.

Livia reread the agreements, separating sentences expressing a wish from those by which someone had accepted work. Elias listened to the end without adding a commitment on anyone's behalf.

Before they finished, Edith asked whether her presence entailed a regular commitment.

“No,” Elias answered.

“Can I help you again without deciding now whether to join?”

“Yes.”

Livia passed him the sheet so he could read what she had written too. Edith was a consultant for the agreed exchanges. No new member, no promise of Heart, no unrestricted access to the workshop.

They did not set an eternal weekly schedule for the next meeting. They confirmed only that the extract would be compared after Etienne had read it, and that the necessary contacts would take place by ordinary means.

They left at intervals, collecting the objects they had brought. The opening was not reactivated. The door's key remained with its custodian.

Elias returned home with less news than he had hoped for, one service accepted, one denied, and a single opening still available.

On the landing he heard Lise arguing with Adrien about the proper way to pack the model.

On the landing, Elias stopped to free a thread caught in his sole. Laughter came from inside, followed by Lise accusing their father of wanting to pack the model like a machine bound overseas.

“If it breaks, I'll have to mend it,” Adrien answered.

“If you close it like that, I'll have to break it to get it out.”

Elias realized he was smiling before anyone saw him. He had spent the afternoon thinking what the group must become to be important enough. Behind the door, two people were trying to protect a small object without making it unusable.

He knocked softly before opening. Lise immediately indicated their chosen material. She wanted an opinion, not an arbitrator between herself and Adrien. Elias set down the folder and asked her to show him where she needed to grip the model to remove it unharmed.

His father made room at the table. Nobody asked whether they had founded anything memorable. He would need time to describe his day; first he could attend to the edge Lise was indicating.

As Lise showed him where to grip the model, Elias stopped wondering whether he had done enough to deserve his tiredness.

Chapter 25 — The Extract

Helren returned on the twenty-fourth and began with the work Elias had promised.

Not with the threat concealed behind the sample, nor with Mael Vaur's name. With the scale drawing, the measurements, and the place where a line in the previous copy had turned a rough patch into a regular surface.

Elias had worked the agreed two hours; Marcel had checked the reproduction. The sheet carried no independent certification his permit did not allow him to sign.

Elias had cleared enough bench space to spread the plan without laying it on the sample. Before Helren's arrival, he had checked the margin so often that a small eraser stain had become intolerable.

Marcel had asked what made the sheet less readable.

“Nothing. But you can see it.”

“You can also see you erased a mistake.”

He had left the stain. Now he watched the inspector seek the point where the first copy had hidden the irregularity. He could no longer improve the plan by touching it; he must wait for the finished work to speak in its own form.

Ilian had arrived looking like someone who would rather be elsewhere but had decided to come anyway. He removed his coat only after seeing where to put it. Elias recognized the difficulty of entering a place where one's work risked being described as part of someone else's deception.

He offered him space beside the window. The draftsman did not thank him for being treated as innocent; he asked whether the light would interfere with the examination. Elias shifted the plan slightly. It was a concrete question, and therefore did him good.

Helren began at the reference indicated at the top. Following his gaze was impossible; Elias ended up rereading his own words, pretending not to seek the moment the inspector would find a flaw.

The inspector examined it in silence.

Ilian, beside the window, had brought the correspondence from his commission that he could show. He had not been summoned as an accused man. He had agreed to attend because his name risked remaining attached to a statement he had not produced.

“Here,” Helren said.

Elias moved closer.

“The original error is identified as such. Good. You haven't corrected it by making the version you started from disappear.”

The praise was so small Elias nearly failed to recognize it.

Helren accepted the work and placed the promised extract on the bench.

The authentic note, the one the inspector had written, requested identification of the sample before attesting a connection. In the subsequent version, that same request became a reference to a positive certification. It was not merely a wrong measurement.

Ilian read the passage twice.

“My drawing was going to be an attachment to this?”

“That's what the correspondence you've shown asks you to prepare,” Helren replied. “It doesn't mean you've already signed the statement.”

The draftsman leaned against the bench. His fingers closed over its edge without exercising Crucible.

Elias remembered the bill folded inside the notebook. Part of the payment was still on the other side of that request.

“If you refuse this form, you can still ask for the balance for the work you've done,” he said.

Ilian looked at him.

“Asking isn't receiving.”

“What if they don't pay?” Elias asked.

Ilian closed his notebook. The blow shifted the weight on the drawing.

“I ask again. I've already bought the paper.”

Helren looked at the reproduction. Then he slid a copy of his genuine response toward Ilian. “Attach this when they ask for my approval.”

The draftsman reopened the notebook more slowly. Elias saw the bill and note go between its pages together. The finished work still needed the client; the client was trying to obtain something nobody present had sold.

Marcel separated the delivered drawing from the one Ilian had worked on for the client.

“I can attest the work you did here. Don't promise the rest to make something you couldn't verify look complete.”

Ilian was looking at his own plan without touching it. Elias understood this was not simply about claiming payment. The drawing was correct within the received assignment's limits, yet someone wanted it to represent a service the draftsman had never seen.

“Can you deliver this without the certification sentence?” he asked.

“I can propose it.”

The absence of greater reassurance prevented him from describing refusal as a choice the world had already rewarded. Ilian could lose the client and be left with an outstanding balance. He still needed to eat even if he was right not to sign.

Marcel said he would describe only the work done in his workshop. He neither offered to pay in the client's place nor urged a heroic gesture others could observe without bearing its cost.

The draftsman reopened his notebook. He scanned the request, seeking where he had accepted reproduction rather than a guarantee of operation. The page preserved what he had written at different times without making all those hours equivalent.

Elias thought of his restricted permit and how much he wanted someone to acknowledge the work he could still perform. Watching Ilian, he understood he must not turn that resemblance into forced intimacy. It was enough to indicate where Marcel's answer could make a distinction legible even to the client.

The draftsman followed the point with his nail. He asked Elias to hold the sheet while he rewrote a sentence. Elias did so without proposing another solution for a balance he did not possess.

“And if they go to someone else?”

“Perhaps they will. Your name doesn't have to follow them.”

The inspector showed the bond on the testimony and compared the extract with the document kept in custody. The relationship between them was not merely the resemblance of letters. An ordinary copy could reproduce the mark's design; that would not make it the same attestation.

Elias did not ask Helren to invest a greater certainty. He recorded what they could say: the text shown did not authorize the reference to certification. He did not record having seen Vaur falsify a sheet.

“What did they ask you to move?” Marcel asked.

Helren kept one hand on the folder.

“A group during a demonstration of the connection. I asked who they were, what consent they had given, and who was responsible for the operation. I received more descriptions of the apparatus.”

“Not names.”

“Not names usable for those questions.”

Ilian raised his head.

“I was supposed to draw a component.”

“And that's the work you've declared,” Helren said.

For a moment, the workshop's things lost their familiarity to Elias. A staircase, a bench, a door: ordinary elements could become part of an action no one showed whole to those hired to carry it out.

The Hinge did not leave its custody to help him understand. It was not even named before the inspector.

Marcel prepared the commercial reply: willingness to return the sample under the delivery agreement, examination limited to what had been observed, no offer of a working specimen, and no certification of the transit of people.

Ilian wanted to read the sentence concerning his work before it was copied.

He changed it.

Not a great alteration. He removed a word that might have made an approval requested by others seem his own. Elias let him finish without suggesting a more heroic formulation.

“I can carry the reply,” Ilian said. “Not the piece you haven't offered for sale.”

“The sample will be returned through the agreed delivery,” Marcel replied. “We won't use your willingness to skip that too.”

Ilian nodded. He folded the sheets with a care that did not make his face seem any less angry.

Ilian reread the altered sentence with the sheet nearly upright. The light clarified its margin; Elias could see where a letter had been rewritten not to resemble another.

“I hadn't understood the drawing would be presented like this,” the draftsman said.

Nobody answered that he should have understood. The distance between reproducing a component and attesting a connection was real even for someone who knew enough magic to understand that an object could lie through its form.

Marcel had him identify work already delivered and work the client had not yet received. Separating them did not pay the balance. It would let him request what was owed without retrospectively lending his signature to a service never performed.

Elias listened. He wanted to know the client would accept, that a correct answer would produce an orderly conclusion. Helren did not give him that prediction. He returned the comparison to the text he was authorized to provide and left the reader's reaction to the future.

Ilian put the sheet in its case, checking that no edge remained exposed. The same precaution as at their first meeting now seemed to Elias a gesture of obstinacy: preserving one's work well even when someone else had wanted to misuse it.

He moved to let him pass and did not detain him with a promise of indefinite help. Both had tasks to finish. The prospect of discussing it again would be more credible if neither had to pretend the meeting had solved everything.

Helren gathered the folder. The extract remained on the bench under the agreed limits of use, not the inspector's whole archive. No new name had joined the Exchange merely by entering the right room.

Once Helren had put the documents away, Ilian stayed beside the bench. The commercial reply was almost ready, and Marcel asked him to reread it without waiting for the page to be formally finished.

The draftsman shifted the sheet to avoid casting a shadow. The hands he had used to sense components now sought only a line. No ability could choose for him which sentence he would agree to carry to a client.

Elias observed his own name in the margin of the reproduction the master had checked. It was no new licence, no guarantee the world would stop misusing a plan. It acknowledged a specific job he had completed while the restriction remained in force.

Ilian closed his notebook and nodded to him. They had not promised friendship. They had spent a morning together and prevented a sentence growing larger than its support.

The master waited until the visitor left before returning the box to its place. The door stayed open onto the alley; ordinary work began asking for room again. Elias should have felt only relief, but instead felt a sharper unease too. Someone had tried to purchase a piece of certainty for a purpose they would not fully explain.

He gathered the reproduction sheet without opening the apparatus's case. Understanding the danger a little better did not oblige him to show it a shorter route.

When they were alone, Marcel asked Elias what they had obtained.

He looked at the reproduction, the reply, and the sample still enclosed.

“A reason not to hand ours over.”

“We already had that.”

Elias nodded. He took time to formulate the difference.

“Now we know someone tried to buy the missing part of a proof. And that the people caught in the middle don't have to keep going just because they've started.”

Marcel left the reply to dry.

Elias still did not know what the client would do when faced with refusal. For the first time, though, he did not confuse that ignorance with an obligation to open the door.

Chapter 26 — The Healed Arm

Etienne was carrying a chair when Elias arrived.

He held it in both hands. There was nothing extraordinary about the gesture, which was precisely why Elias stopped to watch a moment too long.

“You can take the other one,” the worker said.

Elias obeyed. The door to the room borrowed for their discussion was narrow; they entered sideways and placed the chairs near the table. Edith had already opened her notebook. Livia kept her own case closed.

It was May twenty-fifth. The promised extract was ready, but it did not yet belong to Elias.

The chair creaked as the worker tested it with his weight. Elias had taken the other and still held its back, unsure which side of the table would be less intrusive.

“You can read there,” Etienne said, indicating the place by the window.

“I'm not here to correct your sheet.”

“No. But if you stand, I feel I should ask whether we need to leave.”

Elias sat. Edith moved the notebook toward the person to whom its observations belonged. The copyist waited with her own materials closed. Nobody needed to speak immediately to prove they knew why they had met.

Etienne removed his cap, set it on his knee, then moved it to an empty chair because it kept slipping. He regarded the document as something useful to him, not final proof of having behaved well during treatment.

Elias noticed a marginal correction: a few words rewritten to clarify which observation they referred to. Edith had not brought an immaculate sheet obtained by erasing the work of preparing it. She had reached this version through questions he had not heard.

“Did you have to search much?” he asked.

“I had to separate what I could say from what others would have to say.”

Her voice did not suggest those limits made the extract useless. Elias felt grateful finally to read something that did not require the worker's body to remain injured to be credible.

Etienne brought the sheet closer to his eyes. The room's window was small; Livia moved her chair to avoid casting a shadow. That was enough for reading to resume.

Etienne sat down and began reading. His lips moved slightly over the longer passages. Elias wanted to indicate the important point and forced himself not to decide which one it should be for the person who had received the treatment.

“Here you say I can resume ordinary movement,” Etienne observed.

Edith showed him the date of the observation.

“Under those conditions, yes.”

“Not that I worked every day.”

“No.”

He returned to the page. His hand rested on its edge, whole, capable of a gesture so common it made recalling the sound of the accident without feeling ridiculous difficult.

Elias did not feel ridiculous. He felt relieved and guilty about the shape he had given that relief.

“May I tell you something?”

Etienne looked up.

“If it doesn't begin with what I should sign.”

“When I saw you move your hand, I thought perhaps the worst was over.”

The worker watched him without helping him finish.

“For me,” Elias added.

The room remained quiet. Edith did not turn the sentence into a confession to reward, and Livia did not write it down.

Etienne bent his arm, then left his hand on the table.

“Part of it was over for me too. But you'd gone back to measuring. I was waiting for someone to tell me whether I'd be paying for those days.”

“I know that now.”

“Good. You don't have to have known before to listen.”

That concession hurt more than an accusation. Elias could have defended himself against someone demanding perfection; Etienne was asking for something he could actually do.

Etienne looked at his arm. He opened and closed his hand without demonstrating strength. The ability to use his fingers already belonged to him; it need not be won back whenever someone mentioned it.

“When you saw me improving, I was afraid everything else would stop counting,” he said.

The words reached Elias somewhere his good intentions could not repair. He had feared hurting the man, then used relief to measure how threatened he himself still was.

“I don't want you to have to seem less recovered.”

“That's why I'm carrying chairs.”

The worker smiled faintly. Elias returned it with the effort of someone discovering he had been acquitted of a different accusation from the one he had made against himself.

Edith returned to a line in the extract. She asked whether the description matched the conditions they had observed then. Etienne read it again and asked only that the day be made clearer. The nurse did not accuse him of rewriting his treatment merely for asking that the text not seem to describe every subsequent day.

Livia listened without taking possession of the page. Elias understood how easily he would have imagined his own service complete once the document was authenticated. Instead, the difficult point was here, before the mark: a person must recognize himself sufficiently in those sentences to accept their use.

Elias read the amended passage softly. Etienne did not interrupt before the end. Then he nodded and left the sheet on the table, his fingers clear of the place that would receive the attestation.

Elias had already drawn breath to ask whether things were better now. Etienne was still reading. He lowered his eyes to the next line and waited. A load being set down sounded through the window; the worker barely lifted his head, recognized it, and returned to the sheet.

They read the extract to the end. Edith had separated the observed injury, the assistance, and the recovery. She had not written who ordered the bench moved. Etienne's health did not become an expert assessment of Vern's intentions.

Etienne turned the sheet toward Livia. “This part can be used.”

The nurse withdrew her notebook, leaving the extract between them. Elias had kept his hands away throughout the reading; now he brought the copy beside the document without managing to stop watching the arm that had pushed it. He remembered that open palm in the warehouse. Now two fingers held the paper while the other hand sought the cap.

“Do you need one of them?” Etienne asked, seeing him stare.

“The sheet,” Elias said. “I think the cap's already taken.”

Livia asked whether she could compare the authorized copy with what was being shown. Etienne agreed to that use, not unrestricted circulation.

When the copyist imbued the mark, Elias did not seek a promise of victory in it. He looked at the worker's hand beside the sheet. He had to remember to see both.

Etienne reread the sentence about recovery a third time. Elias asked whether anything did not match. The worker shook his head and stayed with the sheet.

“It's that reading it here makes one thing seem to end and another begin,” he said. “When it happened, it wasn't so orderly.”

Edith nodded. She did not suggest turning the extract into a complete account of the weeks he had lived through. She explained that separating observations prevented one erasing another; the page could not contain every day, but it could avoid denying them.

Elias realized how often he had wanted a document more complete than life. If he found the perfect sentence, he thought, nobody could misunderstand again. Then the sentence reached someone with a different question, and the world became uncertain once more.

Livia asked him to hold both pages steady while she checked the authorized comparison. He rested a finger on the margin, away from the line Etienne was still reading. He felt useful in a very small way, enough not to demand that the next act resolve his fear.

“It isn't a sheet against you,” the worker said.

“I know.”

“You say it as though you're learning that now.”

Elias looked at him. He could have insisted he had known from the beginning, defended his good faith until it became the room's subject again. He chose to nod.

Etienne gave him time to do so. Then he asked again where he should go on the thirty-first, whom to approach, and which documents he would have. Edith checked the time and Livia closed the part of the work requiring her ability.

The conversation became practical again. They had a meeting to attend and people who would ask questions. It was less reassuring than a victory already written, but much closer to the life they would actually face.

Edith made him repeat what he would submit for consideration on the thirty-first. He listed the extract, the two sets of measurements, and the account of the inspection. He stopped before adding a sentence about anyone's innocence.

“I'll tell the rest when they ask what I did myself.”

Etienne nodded.

When the work was finished, the worker rose to put the chairs away. Elias took his own without offering to lift the other with a power as well.

Edith stayed to arrange the papers. Livia waited for her to decide which ones went back into the bag. Her clinical file had not lost pages by becoming useful to someone.

“Do you have a shift afterward?” Elias asked the nurse.

“Later. First I'll eat.”

“Could I—”

He stopped, and she laughed.

“You can ask. Not every question is an assault.”

He suggested getting something together if she wanted. Edith accepted on condition that no one brought a notebook to the table. Livia said that was finally a clause she would work on for free.

Edith picked up the pencil that had slipped beneath the case. She put it in her bag, then took it out to check she had not left the cap on the table. Livia watched with an expression Elias did not know: amused relief at seeing someone else interrupt an orderly gesture for something trivial.

“Don't laugh. If I stain the lining again, I'll have to take it apart,” the nurse said.

“I thought your bag was incapable of mistakes.”

“So do I whenever I look for something and find everything else.”

Elias laughed. He realized he had nothing to demand of the remark to make it useful to the case. Livia checked the time; time for a meal was concrete, not to be spent standing there politely postponing it.

Elias indicated the shortest way out. Edith asked whether he also intended to take the chair. He realized he had gripped its back again without knowing where to put it.

He returned it to the agreed place. Etienne had put his own away and was looking for his cap. Their gestures finally resembled the end of a meeting between people. No central object held them together until the next check.

“You can come too,” Elias told the worker.

Etienne thanked him without accepting. He did not look at his arm before answering; he had other things to do, and for the first time Elias managed not to hear his departure as a judgment on his own capacity to be forgiven.

Etienne did not stay. He had to go home and discuss the financial proposal still open; his day did not end where the service he rendered Elias ended.

On the threshold he lifted his cap.

“On the thirty-first, I don't want everyone speaking for me.”

“We won't,” Elias replied.

Then he asked whether he needed someone to hold the door.

“That, yes.”

It was an ordinary request. Recognizing it without turning it into something more seemed work enough for that moment.

Chapter 27 — The Papers Outside the House

The document did not smell of the baron's room.

The words were the baron's, copied by a hand that did not know their tone. Alexander sought Celine in the text and found her where a signature was meant to decide for her.

The court official put a finger on the reproduction's provenance. The originals had been acquired in Ravelle's study and remained in the office's custody. Edran followed the reference, then returned to the guardianship line.

He had not heard that sentence under Will. It had existed before Ravelle entered the room, when the baron still imagined a house without Alexander.

It was the twenty-fifth. In the intervening days the world had continued moving enough to produce a result without requiring him to enter every room.

The draft proposed a temporary administration. Not the transfer of the ducal title to the baron, nor a national vote obtained by proxy from a minor. It was subtler: make indispensable the hand that would sign beneath Celine's name.

Beside it were notes on appointments and contracts to review.

The official arranged the sheets so every reference could be found again. He had not brought the entire investigation as a gift for the duke. His work required distinguishing what he could show from what remained in custody, and Edran let him explain before reading.

The copy described a future Alexander would never have lived. A household after mourning, a minor surrounded by counsel, a man ready to make his own presence indispensable. The lettering was composed. Had the young man died, the paper could have appeared on a table with the same practical air with which Albin had presented the wages.

Edran stopped at a phrase. He had heard it spoken without malice so often: for the sake of continuity. He understood how little banning words from the house would accomplish. The problem was the place left to those who must live inside them.

“This line concerns his administration,” he told the official.

The man indicated where the plan proposed it as a solution, not a right already granted. The distinction increased his anger rather than reducing it. A young man had been killed for a project its author had not even made inevitable.

Alexander turned the sheet. The reverse offered no further signatures. He returned to the first page and asked which parts had been reproduced for comparison. The official showed him without guaranteeing what the delivered documents did not contain.

Edran took time to examine the margins too. Not to discover an occult sign: he wanted to distinguish what he read from what his imagination had already put in the baron's room.

Albin waited until the duke passed him the page. The administrator knew enough of the house to recognize where Ravelle's project began speaking of people as though they were merely available positions.

Albin read without quite sitting down. When he reached a reference to an estate matter, he placed his finger beside the line.

“This description is incomplete.”

“In what way?”

The administrator explained which decisions had already been delegated and which required separate consultations. The baron's project was not a mechanism that would work as soon as Alexander died. It contained errors, underestimated resistance, and people who had not promised to obey.

Edran felt a cold anger.

A man had been willing to kill even for a future he had failed to organize properly.

“The four counts haven't signed,” he said.

The representative confirmed only what he had delivered: the draft obtained bore no such signatures. He did not guarantee that he had collected every conversation held in the duchy.

The official let him examine the margin where endorsements should have appeared. He did not present it as proof nobody had held ambitions. It was merely empty in the document they could examine.

Edran thought of the cruel pleasure a complete list would have given him. A name could be sought, questioned, forced to answer. An absent signature instead asked him to tolerate unfinished work without deciding its limits insulted his authority.

“Is this the copy you are leaving me?” he asked.

The man checked the reference before answering. He showed him how to identify it without confusing it with the original. Edran listened. He did not want to ask Etienne to believe a page existed merely because he had seen it in his study.

Albin took a separate sheet for his corrections. He avoided marking the reproduction the court had brought. It was no solemn gesture: he wanted to preserve the difference between the baron's plan and his present explanation of the household's operation.

For a moment Edran could watch their two hands working without superimposing the murderer's. He realized anger was trying to take even his ability to recognize an ordinary contribution. The people before him had no duty to perform his indignation well enough to make him feel less alone.

He took back the copy when they finished. He set it in the clear space at the table's center and let the official finish explaining the next steps. His certainty did not need to interrupt to exist.

Alexander accepted the distinction. A single guilty group would have been convenient; convenience had not become more innocent because this time he would have enjoyed it.

Albin asked whether he could note on his own sheet the parts that did not match actual jurisdictions. The official distinguished explaining administrative operation from a statement about the baron's personal agreements.

“I am not telling you whom he spoke to,” the administrator specified. “This procedure would not have obeyed that signature merely because he had envisaged it.”

Edran watched the finger beside the line. A few days earlier, he could have made the same practical error in a benevolent decision, confusing personal wealth with a ducal function. The analogy did not equate intentions. It prevented him from treating imperfect procedural knowledge as sufficient reason to recognize a murderer.

“How many could have stopped him?” he asked.

Albin said he could not count objections that had never occurred. He could indicate where other decisions would have been necessary, and that none of those people appeared as signatories to the draft before them.

Alexander nodded. It was less comforting than imagining a household able to defend Celine like an organism without needing to know. People would have had to take responsibility for a conflict, and the world did not provide advance proof that they would.

He looked at the study door. A doctor, a provisions administrator, a friend had entered there. The baron had used the same normality. He did not want a future in which every visit began with suspicion, but he must learn to leave a door open without surrendering his judgment along with it.

Mirel arrived with the result of the examination of the residue. She had arranged for the portion intended for testing to be separated and the remainder preserved, documenting the steps. The report indicated toxic adulteration consistent with the observed harm. The shape of the glass could not tell her who had introduced it.

“This doesn't identify a hand,” she said before he asked.

“But we have more than my account now.”

“Yes.”

The official noted the report's reference. Mirel checked it before he copied it. She added no conclusion about motive. Edran let the doctor finish describing what the examination could support without granting it the ability to identify a murderer from glass.

The small vial remained a clinical object, distinct from the spent Quiet Glass fragments; nobody attributed a power to it to make the investigation more interesting.

Alexander looked at the papers together. The delivery. The contents. A statement obtained through imposed will. A draft that existed outside his own house and his own ability to create a copy from nothing.

It was not yet a judgment. It was enough to stop seeking a different murderer.

“Other people will need to be heard,” the representative said.

“Without treating kinship as a confession.”

The man looked up. He had received no invested command; Alexander also left him room not to be impressed by a duke's clarification.

“Each person's position will be considered separately.”

When the representative left, Albin remained with the sheet of his own corrections.

“Do you want me to review access for all the notables?”

“I want access to correspond to the work people need to do. Not an inherited entitlement to trust because a door was open to my father.”

“That will mean saying no to people who haven't done anything like this.”

“I know.”

Edran was not certain he yet knew how often. He would have to discover it by living in the house, not merely defending it.

Mirel gathered her notebook. Before leaving she asked whether he had rested.

“I haven't needed to.”

The doctor was not persuaded that the answer settled everything.

“That isn't the same question.”

Alone, Alexander went to the window. A gardener was adjusting the support for the branch that had tapped the glass. He had brought a second piece of wood because the first was insufficient.

Edran could have created a perfect support. He could have unmade the branch. There was no concealed moral significance in the wood requiring him to leave the work to the man.

That afternoon, however, he did not summon him to demonstrate anything.

The page intended for Etienne remained blank a few moments. Edran sought wording that would let the young man receive news without feeling called to choose between two people before opening the letter.

He began with the relevant findings. The draft had been found; he did not attribute a perfection to it that it lacked. The residue report had provided corroboration; he did not turn it into the memory of whoever had adulterated it. He wrote enough to make the update's purpose comprehensible and omitted the satisfaction part of him would feel at seeing his account confirmed.

He reread the recipient's name. In recent days Alexander's friend had become an accused man's son whenever someone spoke his surname. Edran did not want to help him disappear into that definition.

He stopped before adding a request to visit. Etienne had said he did not know whether he wanted to return. The letter could reach him without becoming a summons.

Outside, the gardener tested the new support and waited until the branch stopped swaying. He did not look toward the study to see whether the duke had watched. Edran took up the pen and finished his interrupted sentence. His attention too must learn to remain where needed without constantly asking the world to demonstrate that he had returned.

He returned to the papers and wrote to Etienne that corroborating material had been obtained by the competent office. He did not add that his father had become a different monster from the man he had known.

It was the same man. That was the part no evidence would make simple.

Chapter 28 — The Second Position

Marcel did not reactivate the bench.

Elias had feared someone would want to repeat the accident to prove they understood it. Instead, his mentor brought wooden strips, string, and measuring references, and had the necessary space cleared without asking anyone to put an arm where Etienne's had been injured.

“Today we reconstruct the positions,” he said. “Calibration comes afterward. Let's not confuse a demonstration with an authorized test.”

Gaston arranged the two series on a crate. Vern stayed near the passage he had wanted cleared, his hands in his jacket pockets. Etienne had chosen to be there. He would not put his body in the place of a missing measurement.

It was May twenty-sixth. Five days remained until the hearing.

For the first time, that count did not seem merely something time could do against Elias.

The portable bench allowed the alignment of a local field to be checked against the points reached in the working arrangement. The certification concerned that relationship. Moving the furniture did not move the warehouse's reference points with it.

The access supervisor had a crate moved away from where they would stretch the reference. Elias waited until the load was set down. On first entering the warehouse, he had not known which movements changed the arrangement and which belonged only to that day's work.

Now Marcel asked him to look before explaining. The slats did not mark a truth found in dust: they made visible the two positions established from documents. The floor could be the same without remembering its history for them.

Gaston opened the first series. Elias recognized his writing in the part he had copied and another hand in the later notes. For weeks he had wanted to reduce everything to one sheet clear enough to acquit him. Seeing two kept separate finally seemed precision rather than proof he had complicated the problem.

Vern remained near the passage. He sought no place to lean his back. Occasionally he looked toward work continuing farther away, but called nobody to escape the verification.

Etienne sat until asked to describe the point he remembered. Elias stopped checking whether the worker seemed recovered enough or offended enough. He was there because he had chosen to witness the comparison, not to make Elias's position more convincing.

Marcel stretched the first length of string. He did not fasten it wherever convenient: he had them find a reference everyone could agree on before continuing. It took two attempts, and nobody seized the chance to declare the experiment worthless.

Elias felt relief at witnessing work that did not depend on the absence of all hesitation. A correct measurement need not appear inevitable from the first movement to be checked.

Marcel stretched the string between the marks they could verify and asked Gaston to read the first series.

Elias recognized his own distances. He did not need to close his eyes to want to defend them.

“If this was the first position,” his mentor said, “that reference is consistent.”

Vern looked at the string and the edge of the passage. He said nothing.

The second series described the bench after its relocation. Gaston read it without altering the first. The two reconstructions could not occupy the same place simultaneously, even when recounted beneath a single heading.

Marcel asked Elias to indicate on the sheet which measurement he had personally taken. He did not ask him to declare he had witnessed every move.

He answered and stopped.

“You can continue,” Vern said.

Elias looked at him.

“As far as I know.”

The lot supervisor took a hand from his pocket, then put it back.

Marcel used Near Coordinates on the present volume and had the corresponding point checked with the measuring rule. His power gave him a real perception of space. The ordinary comparison let Gaston follow the result without possessing Astrolabe.

They did not read who had walked on the floor a month earlier. They did not need to in order to show what the two series said.

The supervisor asked how both series could contain correct values. Marcel indicated the references and left the tape to Gaston. Elias followed the space with his gaze while the two men repeated the reading.

A number did not carry the wall it had been measured from. A moved bench could retain parts and settings without retaining the relationship a certification described. Elias had understood that before the accident; he had not understood how easily it could disappear inside an abbreviated account of work.

“So you're not remeasuring to make the first one fit,” the supervisor said.

“We're reconstructing what it referred to,” Marcel answered.

Vern looked at the cleared stretch of passage. He was not speaking yet. Gaston merely asked him to confirm it was the point his explanation referred to. He said yes.

Elias was tempted to address him at once. He could hear the sentence: now you see. He held it back. Vern did not need humiliation before the tape to acknowledge a decision; if he chose not to, the comparison would not become false.

Marcel moved to the second reference. He added no difficulty to make the movement more serious. The error was concrete enough not to require invented guilt around it.

A slat slipped when Gaston changed position. Elias bent, then stopped because Vern's hand was already nearer. He let the man pick it up. The gesture solved nothing; it merely prevented Elias telling himself that even keeping the test orderly was a task to which the other could not contribute.

Etienne went to the edge of the passage and indicated a distance with his healed arm.

“I worked from here. I remember that.”

Marcel asked him not to add a number he did not remember. The worker nodded, almost relieved to be a good witness without becoming an instrument.

Vern bent to pick up a wooden strip that had slipped. When he straightened, he held it rather than returning it immediately.

“The stated margin seemed sufficient.”

No one answered quickly.

He looked at the bench.

“I should have requested the new check.”

Elias felt the desire to seize the sentence and run outside with it. He left it in the room long enough for Gaston to ask whether he meant the decision taken at the time.

“I knew it needed doing again after the move,” Vern said.

Etienne lowered his arm.

The truth did not arrive in a flash. It came spoken by a man who, until then, had tried to make his choice bearable by recounting it in pieces.

“Did you order it?” Elias asked.

Vern looked at him. Then he said yes.

Gaston wrote, read it back, and let the man correct anything that did not correspond to his words. Vern did not withdraw the answer.

Elias lowered his eyes to the string. Vern had not disputed the distance. He had not disputed the need for calibration either. The man Elias had sought for weeks stood before him, and there was no measurement left to which he could address the final question.

“Why did you keep using that sheet?”

Chapter 29 — Payday

The button appeared between his thumb and forefinger, black and perfectly whole.

Alexander looked at it and laughed softly.

“Yes.”

“Not yet.”

It was not an accusation, which made it harder.

Edran did not offer her a false symmetry. He looked at the path they had followed.

Celine looked at the water suspiciously.

“That's an answer that avoids the place.”

“This way.”

Alexander changed course.

“He is right.”

“You too.”

Edran nodded. It was easy to want the household to respect his time and call only what was granted to him consideration. Celine had found a way to return a rule even to the person most able to forget it.

“I said I'm going back. Not that I'll stop complaining.”

“And if I can't tell the difference?”

“Start by asking.”

Before Nela left, Alexander asked her to convey his thanks to those who had shared the crisis shifts. Not a summons: nobody should have to return to duty to receive a courteous word. She nodded and resumed her departure.

That evening the button returned to the shirt. The thread passed through the holes four times; a tiny knot projected on the reverse. Alexander turned it between his fingers before putting it on. In the next room Celine asked whether there was still time to add a second line to her aunt.

“Do you know, or do you hope?”

His sister smiled, recognizing the kind of answer he would have given. This time they resumed walking before unwrapping it in the middle of the street.

Before climbing, Lise took the box back. She wanted to set it on the table and undo the ties herself. Elias surrendered the load without offering a reason to do it for her again. He followed her upstairs, close enough to open the door.

At home, Adrien asked how it had gone. Elias let Lise answer.

At home, Lise put the box on the table without immediately opening it. Adrien took a chair and listened with his hands away from the model. Elias noticed: their father could have checked every joint again while she spoke, turning the morning into a verification of work done at home.

He let her tell it. When Lise reached the unanswered question, he did not say it surely would not matter. He asked whether she wanted to seek the reasoning in her notebook. She answered that she wanted to eat first.

Elias put his folder on the shelf. He still had work for the thirty-first, but no secret deadline forbade staying for a meal. When his sister resumed describing the interview, he could listen without seeking a similar enough question in his own future to redirect the conversation toward himself.

The box stayed closed until Lise chose to undo the ties. The model had traveled and undergone an examination. It did not need to succeed a second time to prove her account true to her brother.

That evening, before returning to his notes for the hearing, he found a new line on the sheet with the question neither of them had known how to solve. His sister had already begun looking.

Chapter 32 — What a Father Says

Etienne brought a sentence instead of a question.

“He says he didn't do it for himself.”

Alexander let him in. He did not immediately show the papers he could have used to answer. Etienne had not crossed the city to argue with a file.

It was May twenty-eighth. The conversation with his father had taken place under the conditions set by the office managing his custody, not in a room prepared by the duke. Alexander had not been there and had imposed no words to be repeated afterward.

“Did he tell you what he did?”

Etienne stayed beside the chair.

“He spoke about the gift. He said he couldn't leave everything to a boy who wouldn't listen.”

Edran felt a harsh reply rise so easily he did not have to search for it. He let it reach his mouth and then did not speak it.

Etienne looked at him.

“You don't have to make him better because I'm here.”

“I don't intend to.”

“Or explain at once that now I ought to understand.”

“All right.”

Alexander offered him the seat without placing the proceedings' papers before it. His friend had not requested another reading of the evidence; he had arrived with his father's voice still close enough that he must repeat it to understand where it ended.

Etienne's collar was folded on one side. The boy whose memories Edran possessed would have straightened it with a joke, a gesture too familiar to require a question. Edran kept his hands on the table.

“Did you see him today?”

Etienne explained only what he wanted to tell about the meeting. Not every question, not every hesitation. Alexander did not exploit pauses to reconstruct the entire conversation; he was listening to someone returned from a father, not cross-checking an interrogation conducted elsewhere.

The letter sent after the draft's acquisition had reached him. He knew enough not to turn the crime into a sentence extorted from an innocent man by another's will. He seemed no less lost for it.

Edran felt the discomfort of having nothing to resolve with a precise act. He had identified Alexander's killer and had evidence preserved. The son's pain was no technical residue to eliminate before considering the job complete.

Etienne looked at the chair and pulled it farther from the table. The duke let him choose the distance. He could not ask him to stay until everything was recognizable again and call that friendship.

The young man sat. He had slept little; this time fatigue did more than make him look less cheerful. It took away the interval between a thought and the decision to speak it.

“He asked whether I'd prefer our family to lose every role.”

Alexander recognized the trap without a power. A criminal choice was being placed back in the son's hands, provided he agreed to call it a choice made to protect him.

“And what did you answer?”

Etienne looked at the table's edge.

“That I hadn't asked him to kill my friend.”

The word entered the room without an embrace. Edran did not try to use it to recover everything else.

“I'm sorry,” he said.

Etienne looked up.

“For what?”

“That he tried to make you choose whether to be grateful.”

The young man ran a hand over his face. He did not cry. Alexander stopped expecting a recognizable shape of pain that would let him know what to do.

The young man rubbed his thumb along his cuff's seam. He had stopped seeking a sentence that accused his father clearly enough to authorize grief for him.

“As a child, he told me to keep my word even when it didn't suit me.”

Alexander waited.

“I don't know what to do with that now.”

Edran remembered a voice from his own childhood, good things learned in a world that had not therefore stopped crushing him. He did not use the memory to steal his friend's place. He said a useful rule would not begin to cause harm merely because its teacher had chosen to violate others.

“But speaking of it sounds as though I'm finding something to defend him.”

“You can speak of what was yours without absolving what he did.”

Etienne looked at the window. The thought brought no immediate relief; it left a task harder than repudiating every earlier day. He must distinguish what he wanted to keep from what he was being asked to justify.

“I don't want to become the one who knows how to hate correctly,” he said.

“I am not asking you to.”

“They ask without saying it.”

Alexander nodded. A room falling silent at a surname was enough to invite a position before a person knew what he wished to tell. The duke's title did not exempt him from that pressure; it could increase its effect even when he believed himself kind.

He said they could stop discussing the proceedings if Etienne wished. He did not answer at once. Then he chose to ask about findings already received, as though examining the verifiable part again let him find support while the rest changed shape.

They discussed the corroborating material obtained. Etienne had received the information the office could communicate to him; not every record, not access to the lives of everyone heard. The draft existed. The gift had been adulterated. Guilt did not need to become hereditary to be grave.

“I don't want my surname to enter here as a plea for forgiveness,” Etienne said.

“It isn't something you can pay for him.”

“And I don't want to lose every opportunity because letting me stay would be too convenient for everyone else.”

“You can submit the application you prepared. It will be considered for what it requests.”

His friend stared at him for a moment.

“I don't know whether I want that position anymore.”

Alexander nodded. He realized his most useful answer was, once again, not to turn a right into a compulsory direction.

“Then you can change your application.”

Etienne looked toward the window. The branch was still on its new support. It held no special meaning for him; Edran was grateful for that small freedom.

“Celine wrote to me.”

“She told me she would.”

“She didn't ask me about my father.”

The relief in the young man's voice almost became shame. Alexander did not take it away by explaining what his sister had really wanted. The letter belonged to a relationship he had no right to summarize for both of them.

Etienne turned the glass between his fingers without lifting it. Light gathered in the water and shifted as the rim moved. Alexander followed the reflection to avoid staring while his friend decided whether to speak again.

“I wonder whether visiting means letting him use the family as an excuse again.”

Edran did not prescribe a visit. The boy could neither repair the crime nor lessen it merely by sitting before his father. But he could choose what he would listen to and when to end a conversation.

“If I go back and he says the same thing?”

“You do not have to find a better answer every time to deserve to disagree.”

Etienne looked at him. The sentence did not resolve affection, anger, or fear of becoming like a man he no longer fully recognized. It at least offered a boundary he need not reconstruct in every conversation.

“I don't want to ask you how to love him,” he said.

“I could not decide that for you.”

For the first time that morning, lacking a solution did not seem to Edran a failure to disguise. He possessed enough power to remove a man's ability to refuse an answer. He did not want to treat a friend's pain as another thing to correct until it reached his preferred conclusion.

Etienne drank. The glass returned to the table with a slightly excessive sound. He moved it away from the papers and watched the cleared space. He had not yet decided what to do tomorrow. The room could contain him in that doubt without turning it into a promise to obtain before he left.

They stayed silent long enough to stop expecting a final sentence.

“I'm still afraid of what you can do,” Etienne said.

Edran did not look away.

“I understand.”

“I don't want you convincing me not to be.”

“I won't.”

The promise did not erase his material ability to violate it. Alexander did not try to sell it as a magical defense. It would matter only through the limits he respected even when no one could compel him.

Etienne stayed for a glass of water. When Maurice entered, he asked whether he could have something to eat; he had not had breakfast.

Maurice waited only to learn what he might bring. He did not ask whether remaining in the duke's house after such a difficult visit was appropriate. Etienne found time to thank him before he left, with a courtesy that did not yet have to justify his surname.

The butler provided it without asking him to deserve his appetite first.

Maurice offered him a more comfortable place for the plate. Etienne moved without treating every consideration as suspicious. Edran felt relief and forbade himself to read it as reconciliation already beginning.

The butler asked whether they wanted anything else. Etienne requested only water. The world could still contain him as a person who had forgotten to eat, not exclusively as Marcel Ravelle's son.

“The application wasn't entirely mine,” he said after a while.

Alexander waited until he swallowed.

“There were things I thought I wanted because they seemed the right way to be useful. Now I read it and don't know which were really mine.”

Fear of Will made the sentence more painful but did not exhaust it. A family could give directions without any supernatural ability. Edran did not offer certainty that he could recognize every authentic desire from outside.

“You can leave it on the table for a day. You do not have to submit it to prove your father has not taken your courage.”

Etienne nodded, then asked whether he spoke as friend or duke.

“At this moment, I am trying to listen as a friend.”

Etienne broke the bread. A piece fell beside the plate and he picked it up before answering. “Try listening when you haven't got advice, too.” Alexander sought a reply and did not find one quickly enough. The young man drank some water. For a few moments they simply ate.

Etienne stayed seated. The conversation did not become easy, but for a few minutes they discussed a sheet unrelated to recounting the crime. Edran let that small space produce no new commitment to demand before he left.

Alexander watched him break the bread and remembered a younger boy imitating his father with a walking stick too large for his arm.

“I don't know whether I'll come again soon,” Etienne said as he rose.

Edran opened his mouth to answer that he need not justify himself. His friend spoke first.

“Don't tell me I have permission.”

“I wasn't saying that.”

Etienne looked at him, and for a moment it was almost one of their old arguments. “You were about to.”

Alexander accompanied him to the door. Only then did he remember his hat and take it from Maurice. He would answer Celine, he said. He left Edran the following day without an appointment.

Chapter 33 — Vern's Signature

Vern arrived at the workshop on the twenty-ninth with his own account.

Elias saw him from the window and thought, before anything else, that the man might still withdraw his admission. He hated himself for the relief he would feel at being able to be merely angry again.

Marcel let the lot supervisor in and called Gaston, who was already working nearby. He did not summon a crowd.

Vern rested the sheet on the bench.

“I want to check this says the same thing.”

Gaston arrived drying his hands on a cloth. He did not ask whether Vern still intended to stand by his words. He greeted them and sought somewhere to read alongside the others.

The lot supervisor opened the sheet and turned it toward the bench. A fold crossed a line; Elias had to hold it with a finger to keep a word from disappearing in the reflected light.

“Is this the version you want to take?”

“That's why I came.”

Elias wanted to ask why Gaston's account was not enough if Vern had already confirmed every sentence. From the care with which Vern followed the text, he understood that writing in the first person required another choice. He would not reward him for taking longer. But he wanted to know what he was truly willing to sign.

Marcel removed his tool from the bench. He improvised no seat of honour for the visitor. He cleared enough space that they need not read among objects forcing someone to hold the sheet throughout.

Elias smelled the damp cloth beside the vise. The detail was unimportant, yet helped him remain present while the man he had feared for weeks placed his account beside the measurements.

They began at the first line. Gaston followed the passages in his notebook with a finger. He had not hidden the verification's account to make correct wording harder to find; neither would he let Vern make it seem a different decision.

The opening lines named the first measurement, the decision to move the bench, and the reuse of the certification. Gaston compared them with what he had recorded during the check on the twenty-sixth. Elias read one sentence twice.

“This leaves out that you knew the calibration needed repeating.”

Vern looked at the sheet.

“I wrote that I relied on the margin.”

“That's not the same thing.”

The sound of files continued elsewhere in the workshop. Someone had not interrupted work to make their discussion more important.

Vern sat. He removed his jacket and folded it on his knees with a care Elias had never noticed during inspections.

“If I write it like that, I can't say it was a simple misunderstanding anymore.”

Elias pulled the sheet toward him. Vern still had a finger on its edge; the paper tightened between them.

“If you leave that sentence out,” Elias said, “tomorrow we're back to arguing whether the error is my measurement.”

The lot supervisor removed his finger. Elias noticed the small mark his nail had left. He turned the sheet so Vern could reach the blank space.

“It wasn't.”

Vern stayed still. Then he asked for a pen.

Vern held the pen without writing. Its point stayed near the line Elias had indicated, and nobody told him where to put the sentence without making the page untidier.

“I have people who expect me to keep work moving,” he murmured.

Gaston left the notebook open. Elias thought of answering that he did too. It would return them to a contest in which Vern's wrongdoing must be large enough to dwarf every other fear.

“You're not being asked to say you didn't know your job,” he said instead. “You're being asked which choice you made knowing what was missing.”

The man lowered his eyes. His cuff slipped over the hand holding the paper and he jerked it back. He no longer resembled a supervisor distributing duties without pause; he seemed someone trying to avoid a cost that had reached his name.

“They'll read this sentence without me there too.”

“Yes,” Marcel said. “That's why we're checking it.”

The answer needed no threat. Vern could not accompany the page everywhere to alter its tone. If he wanted understanding without denying what he had known, he must find wording he could support now.

Elias waited for him to write. He did not feel the satisfaction he had imagined at watching a hand tremble over a confession. The handwriting stayed steady enough. Acknowledging responsibility did not need to look humiliating to be real.

When the pen moved, Gaston shifted just far enough to cast no shadow. That gesture too left the sentence to the person who had chosen to add it.

The correction was brief. The page looked less clean than before. Elias preferred that version.

Gaston reread the passage, and Vern confirmed it. The signature did not alter April twenty-first. It altered what that man was prepared to maintain before others now.

“I'll take it to the hearing myself,” he said.

“We'll bring our checks too,” Marcel replied.

Vern nodded without protesting at the distrust. His promise on the twenty-sixth had not become a right to make every further corroboration disappear.

Etienne Brac arrived later, notified for the financial discussion. He had a list of unpaid days and a question far simpler than the language of the original offer.

“Does this part still depend on what I sign about the accident?”

Vern looked at the separate sheets on the bench.

“I've asked for it to be handled separately.”

“Who decides the payment?”

The lot supervisor identified the office that would arrange the balance. He did not claim the right to withdraw funds from a till that was not his. He said he would confirm the shifts and dispute an account that made them disappear.

Etienne had one day corrected. Gaston checked the available reference. They did not need an Effigy to find the right count.

Elias stayed beside the bench. His own rehabilitation did not coincide with that man's entitlement to the money, and at last he could remain in the same room without trying to join the two to feel safer.

Etienne compared his list with the shift reference. It took a moment to recognize a day entered in the wrong place. He said so without first looking at Elias, as though he needed no permission to make the account more precise just when everything seemed to be concluding.

Gaston found the reference. Vern checked and accepted the correction. He offered no different sum because the first page had changed; they were discussing lost work, not the price of a sentence.

“I want to know what I'll sign when I pay,” the worker said, then corrected himself with a dry smile. “When I'm paid.”

The laughter was brief but freed the room from remaining solemn until the end. Elias understood even a verbal mistake could be left to its maker without becoming a sign of confusion about everything else.

Marcel had him read the distinction between receipt and reconstruction of the accident. Etienne followed the line and asked which office would arrange payment. Vern indicated the step he could confirm and did not promise to arrive with the money himself.

Elias stayed beside them. He could have added gratitude to make acceptance easier; that was not what the worker asked. He was seeking a way home with an owed service finally recognized and the right to remember what happened without selling it with his signature.

Once they checked the copies, Elias moved the inkwell away from Vern's elbow. The man still held the pen. There was nothing further to add to the numbers before beginning the next sentence.

When the texts had been separated and copied, Vern remained with the pen in his hand.

“I didn't want you hurt,” he told Etienne.

The worker looked at him.

“I believe that.”

Vern seemed to breathe more easily too soon.

“It doesn't change that you made me work without the check that was needed.”

The man lowered the pen.

“No.”

They did not shake hands. Etienne did not owe the lot supervisor an ending beautiful enough to recount as forgiveness.

Elias escorted both to the door. Vern paused before the strip of wood Marcel was working on.

“It's crooked here,” he said, indicating a point with the precision of someone who knew how to look.

Marcel checked.

“Yes.”

The lot supervisor looked at the crooked point of the slat as Marcel turned it in the light. The observation's precision had not put everything else right. Elias felt how easy it would be to want him wrong even here, to avoid keeping competence and responsibility together.

Marcel checked the edge and agreed. He did not thank him so solemnly as to make the conversation seem to have ended well for everyone. He had acknowledged a fact and would use it to correct the piece.

Vern put on his jacket. The signed sheet was no longer the only object requiring attention, but it could not become a closed matter merely because someone had found a way to discuss the trade again.

“See you on the thirty-first,” he said.

Elias nodded. The date already existed in his notebook; hearing the other man say it restored the moment when they must actually appear.

Etienne stood a step beyond the door. He had found his cap's comfortable position and looked toward work still to do before returning home. He was not waiting for Vern to offer a final sentence good enough for forgiveness.

Vern reached the door and stopped to button his jacket. Etienne was already waiting outside. Elias looked at the signed sheet: he wanted to reach the thirty-first with that signature and without that silence. Gaston asked where to keep the copy. “Here,” he said, finally moving his hand from the edge.

He moved aside to clear the passage. Cart noise came from the street. It would receive both without assigning each the music Elias might imagine. He stayed by the threshold, letting them choose when to go. The master still held the piece.

Vern left. His mentor took up the piece again and did not erase that observation merely because it had come from the wrong man.

Gaston put the copy in its agreed place. Elias helped clear the bench without reopening the sheet to check the signature. He had seen it signed; they would take it to the hearing with the other verifications. For that afternoon, responsibility could be kept without reading it every few minutes.

Later Elias reopened the folder. The signature was below the missing sentence. He imagined the hearing table, someone asking why he had insisted on precisely those words.

“Because he knew,” he said softly.

Marcel looked up from the vise. Elias showed him the line. For weeks he had had to go from bench to sheet and sheet to his own defense; this time he could begin with a decision the man had signed.

He closed the folder. Relief still left an irritating tremor in his fingers, but he could hold the piece while Marcel took the next measurement.

Chapter 34 — The Work of an Arm

The evening before the hearing, Etienne asked him to hold a piece of wood still.

Elias had stopped by to confirm the time, not to inspect the man who would testify. He found the door open onto the courtyard and a box being repaired. Etienne's wife gestured him inside, then returned to her own business without offering him the role of important guest.

The wood moved under the blow. Elias placed a palm on it.

“Like this?”

“Nearer the edge. Away from the nail, though.”

He obeyed. The hammer fell, and the box made a normal sound.

For a moment Elias saw the warehouse on April twenty-first again. Not a complete image, but the point at which a working gesture had ceased being ordinary. Etienne stopped and looked at him.

“It's the wood, not the arm.”

Elias loosened his fingers. He had not realized he was gripping too hard.

“Sorry.”

“You don't have to apologize every time you remember.”

Etienne took the wood from the opposite side and set it down again. The first position let Elias hold firmly but left no room for the hammer. The second had the reverse problem. Neither gained anything by insisting the initial grip had been right.

The worker showed him where to leave his fingers. Elias followed the gesture and felt the board pressing his palm. His cut had healed enough not to need a new bandage; he still held the hand where it would not rub the tenderest spot.

“If it slips, say so,” Etienne said.

“You'll see.”

“I'll be watching the nail.”

Elias nodded. He could have explained how much space he perceived through a surface, but just then the other's work required a voice, not a demonstration of his ability.

The hammer fell. The blow reached his wrist through the wood and the box barely vibrated. Etienne waited until his assistant recovered his hold before repeating. They had no audience to convince that work could happen without pauses.

His wife appeared to ask whether the courtyard passage was clear. Elias was about to withdraw everything, but the worker indicated a side she could use without approaching the box. She passed, collected what she needed, and went back inside.

Elias followed her movement. Even this small domestic task occupied space in others' lives. It could not become a reason to make the whole household wait until they finished discussing the thirty-first.

“Now it holds,” Etienne said, testing the edge.

Elias loosened his grip only when he felt the wood remain still. It was a tiny outcome, but relief reached his fingers concretely. He had not needed to be the most competent person to contribute to something successful.

They continued. The task was simple enough to allow conversation and real enough not to require them to keep looking at each other while they spoke.

Etienne said he had read Vern's corrected account. He would confirm what he remembered, not distances learned from a drawing afterward.

“And if I get confused?”

“You say you're confused.”

The worker put down the hammer.

“Easy to say when you're the one who knows how to measure.”

Elias did not answer immediately. It was a fear different from his own, and it did not become smaller because he could give it reasonable advice.

“When they ask why I didn't prevent something I didn't see, I don't always know how to stop before I seem to be making an excuse.”

Etienne nodded slowly.

“Then tomorrow we'll need someone who lets an answer finish.”

His wife returned to fetch an object from the shelf. She asked whether the box would be usable or whether they would spend the evening convincing her that owning boxes was an excessive aspiration.

Etienne said the main defect was the assistant. Elias accepted the accusation and held the wood more firmly.

The box was repaired without lightening or transfers. At the end Etienne lifted it in both hands and carried it beneath the canopy. Elias let him arrive without running alongside him.

The worker put the box under shelter and returned for the hammer. Elias picked it up and offered it handle first. He did not send it traveling to show how easily he could avoid bending.

Etienne put it away. The gesture was natural to someone who expected to find his tools when needed, not a task of proving to anyone that his arm still worked.

“Tomorrow, if they ask me something twice, I'll think I've answered badly,” he said.

Elias looked at the table they were clearing. The fear was familiar, but he did not want it to become the measure by which he answered for the other man too.

“They might just want to understand.”

“Do you know, or do you hope?”

Elias smiled despite himself.

“I don't know which questions they'll ask you.”

“That's an answer I can remember.”

Etienne cleaned his hands and showed him where to do the same. His wife had left a cloth clean enough not to force a choice between keeping dust and transferring it to food. Elias washed without mentally reviewing the evidence list.

He could not protect the worker from every question or badly phrased explanation. But he could avoid promising an easy morning and then calling him ungrateful for emerging tired. He dried his fingers and left the cloth on the clear edge.

At the table, Etienne made room without asking him to summarise their completed work. The box was beneath the lean-to. It was enough to have finished repairing it.

They ate something before he left. It was not a dinner of thanks; it was what there was, and someone had asked whether he wanted some. Elias recounted Lise's model. Etienne laughed at the pebble under the cupboard and asked who had insulted the furniture first.

“Not my father.”

“Then he did it silently.”

Etienne's wife removed an object left beside the food and put it away without asking the men whether it still belonged to the repair. The house had not suspended its other needs to let them talk.

Etienne moved aside for his wife, then returned to the hammer without looking for it. Elias had seen that body at the bench and in the examination chair. Here it found tools by memory and knew which side his wife would pass before she spoke.

“That box never closed properly,” the woman said, looking toward the lean-to.

“Now it does.”

“Tomorrow you'll say it's because of the things I put inside.”

Etienne said she would have to find another fault before evening. She laughed and let the joke end without forcing herself into their conversation.

Elias ate slowly. He had not prepared to enjoy the house of the man he had feared seeing injured whenever he closed his eyes. That minute's peace did not allow him to consider the harm negligible. It merely let him see what he had wanted to protect rather than imagine only its loss.

Etienne asked whether they expected him home for supper. Elias said he had warned them of the visit but did not want to return too late. The worker indicated where he had left his jacket and did not treat his availability as a right to detain him.

“Finish what you've taken first, though,” he added.

Elias looked at the bread and smiled. It was an order he could accept without surrendering the rest of his evening. He stayed long enough to eat and left the table free to discuss the box, Lise's interview, or something that would enter no account.

When the conversation returned to the hearing, it no longer had to fill the whole table.

The amended offer provided for the disputed wages to be handled without abandoning the reconstruction of events. Payment still had to be made. Etienne did not confuse a better sentence with money already in his pocket.

“If it comes, tomorrow I want to go home,” he said. “Not stand outside an office to show I'm grateful.”

“Yes.”

“And if it doesn't, I want to know whom to ask. Not be told I'm well now.”

Elias nodded. This time he could listen to both conditions without feeling his own victory threatened.

Before leaving he asked whether they wanted him to take anything back to the workshop. Etienne handed him a clean spare strip of wood Marcel could reuse. Not a pledge, not evidence.

Elias carried it beneath his arm on the way home.

Adrien was waiting awake. He had repaired his son's jacket for the following day and left the collar comfortable enough not to force a choice between speaking and breathing.

“I'm not an accused man in a historical painting,” Elias said, trying it on.

“And I'm not the painter's tailor. Turn around.”

His father checked the seam, then let it go.

Adrien had him move his shoulders. The fabric pulled a little and his father adjusted the collar before telling him to stand straight again.

“I'll have to speak, not lift a machine.”

“I don't know how a man convinced everyone's about to judge him sits. I'd rather he could move.”

Elias looked at the dim reflection in the glass. The jacket gave him no greater authority and did not hide poorly slept nights. It was clean and comfortable enough to let him carry his body without thinking about it in every sentence.

“You didn't have to wait up.”

Adrien removed a thread from the seam.

“I know.”

Elias held the collar open until Adrien finished. Then he looked at himself in the glass again.

“What if I start talking too fast tomorrow?”

“You stop.”

“What if I can't?”

His father smoothed the fabric over his shoulder. “Your master will be there. Don't ask him to help only after it's over.”

Elias looked at the folder beside the door. He had prepared his answers as though he had to enter alone.

He placed the slat from Etienne beside his father's other materials. He would take it to the workshop tomorrow. It was no case document and could wait one night without special custody. Lise asked whether he had prepared his folder. Elias indicated the door and his sister stopped there, without demanding an inventory before wishing him luck.

Lise handed him the folder beside the chair. Elias took it without opening it a fourth time. Its weight was familiar; the pages already had their order. He thanked her and sought somewhere dry to leave it until morning.

Lise wished him people capable of reading papers to the end. Elias said he would try to be one himself.

When he removed the jacket, Elias hung it without folding the collar. His father's work would remain recognizable tomorrow. He washed his hands and realized his palm still remembered the wood's pressure. The repaired box would not appear at the hearing, but belonged to the day as much as the notes prepared for his defense.

That night he put the folder beside the door and went to bed. He lay awake a little, but did not get up to measure something he had already checked.

Chapter 35 — Staying

Alexander had left the door open.

Not the residence's door, nor the archives, nor Celine's room. The door to his study, for an hour on the morning of May thirtieth, while Maurice coordinated access and Albin brought one matter at a time.

It was a decision too small to enter a song. Large enough to make pretending that inhabiting the house was still a provisional convalescence difficult.

The first problem concerned ordinary work already authorized. The second, a new request. Alexander did not resolve both with the estate's uncommitted money, nor treat the existence of separate procedures as proof that his powers were useless.

He asked, understood, decided what fell to him. He left the others the parts for which they genuinely held a responsibility.

Albin set down the last open folder and showed him where the previously agreed point ended. Alexander listened to the missing detail before deciding whether the new request could be treated alike. It was the kind of resemblance that invited a hurried signature.

“If I answer now, who will have to change their work?”

The administrator indicated the relevant step without reciting every household duty. Edran felt how much more useful the question had become since he stopped asking it to prove himself different from the young duke.

He had all of Alexander's memories at his disposal, as well as the experience he himself had acquired. He had no reason to feign ignorance for another explanation. He stated what he recognized, asked what the sheet could not provide, and let Albin distinguish the matter from a similar one.

Maurice appeared at the threshold to ask whether he could admit someone already expected. The duke requested another moment. Leaving someone outside until the previous conversation ended was no humiliation; worse to invite them in and force them to guess which part they must not hear.

The decision that came next was sufficiently limited in its scope that he could explain exactly what it meant. Edran framed it without turning his role into a blessing upon the house. Elias gave his assent. Livia did the same after they had read together the conditions Edith corrected. Marcel remained the external technical guarantor: they did not need to make him a founder to thank him for letting them work.

Edith reread the availability sentence. She had removed a term that could make her intervention seem automatic whenever a member requested assistance. Livia asked whether the new phrasing matched what she wanted to accept.

“I want to be here even when I'm no use for treating someone,” the nurse said.

Elias nodded. He could have answered that they already considered her a whole person, but that would ask her to take comfort from their intentions. He preferred to let her alter the agreement until it could be read without guessing their affection.

“And you can ask for something too,” he said.

Edith leaned back. She had no immediate request to prove joining had been rational. Perhaps she would want information, a service, or someone with whom to examine a problem. The right to return did not depend on knowing already.

Marcel asked which contact to maintain for tasks concerning the place. He remained outside the group as naturally as he had helped it. Elias thanked him without adding a title that would force the master to become responsible for all their future decisions.

Edith chose a place from which she could reach her bag without making Livia rise. Elias began moving another chair to give her room; she stopped him with her foot.

“This one's fine.”

He left it. On the table, between bread and folder, the request sheet remained. None concerned his hearing.

“Next time I'll bring something I need,” Edith said.

Livia turned the sheet toward her. “About time.”

They were three members now. Four people at the table.

Edith did not immediately choose a pseudonym. She could do so when she had reason. Her own voice sufficed to say yes.

“I want to learn to open it myself,” Elias said before gathering the papers.

Marcel looked toward the antechamber. “That isn't the next exercise.”

“I know.”

“And it isn't something you try because you have an evening free.”

“I know that too.”

Livia turned to him. “Are you angry with the people who answered us, or do you truly want to learn?”

Elias had both answers. He had seen the passage's two sides, felt the distance he could not place between his hands. He wanted to return without counting openings left by someone else, understand enough not to have to choose between keeping it closed and entrusting it to a stranger.

“I want to learn,” he said.

Marcel stayed silent a moment. Then he returned his notebook. “Then come tomorrow and see how Yselle works. And finish your part properly.”

Elias put the visit sheet above the other pages. Edith asked the time of their next contact, and Livia recorded it before discussion could return to the hinge.

When they left, Elias waited until everyone had collected what belonged to them. Marcel locked the door with the key already in his custody.

Livia took a different street from Edith. His mentor returned to the workshop. The network did not need everyone to finish the day in the same place to continue existing.

Elias opened his notebook in the street. The sheet for tomorrow's visit bore Yselle Valcienne's name and the work on the pumps. Until a few hours earlier he had chiefly counted the days until his fear might end. Now he had to prepare to enter an unfamiliar workshop.

He turned the page. There was the incompatible dimension of the returned sample. The question about the client had stayed after the sample left.

On the blank sheet he wrote two things to ask Marcel: who could replenish the Hinge, and how to meet them without handing it over. Then he closed the notebook. He had no answer yet, but this time it was a question he wanted to take beyond the workshop.

Across the street a cart passed, hiding the door they had left for an instant. When it reappeared it was closed, ordinary, among the others.

Elias headed home. On June second he had work; afterwards, a room to return to. Before reaching the corner he looked back once more. He hoped that door would keep looking like the others.
