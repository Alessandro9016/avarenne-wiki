Alexander had left the door open.

Not the residence's door, nor the archives, nor Celine's room. The door to his study, for an hour on the morning of May thirtieth, while Maurice coordinated access and Albin brought one matter at a time.

It was a decision too small to enter a song. Large enough to make pretending that inhabiting the house was still a provisional convalescence difficult.

The first problem concerned ordinary work already authorized. The second, a new request. Alexander did not resolve both with the estate's uncommitted money, nor treat the existence of separate procedures as proof that his powers were useless.

He asked, understood, decided what fell to him. He left the others the parts for which they genuinely held a responsibility.

Albin set down the last open folder and showed him where the previously agreed point ended. Alexander listened to the missing detail before deciding whether the new request could be treated alike. It was the kind of resemblance that invited a hurried signature.

“If I answer now, who will have to change their work?”

The administrator indicated the relevant step without reciting every household duty. Edran felt how much more useful the question had become since he stopped asking it to prove himself different from the young duke.

He had all of Alexander's memories at his disposal, as well as the experience he himself had acquired. He had no reason to feign ignorance for another explanation. He stated what he recognized, asked what the sheet could not provide, and let Albin distinguish the matter from a similar one.

Maurice appeared at the threshold to ask whether he could admit someone already expected. The duke requested another moment. Leaving someone outside until the previous conversation ended was no humiliation; worse to invite them in and force them to guess which part they must not hear.

The decision that came next was sufficiently limited in its scope that he could explain exactly what it meant. Edran framed it without turning his role into a blessing upon the house. Albin recorded it and had him confirm the point on which to act. Nobody asked him to become more human to accept that check. They merely asked him not to make a service depend on interpreting a nod.

When the folder closed, Edran realized he had spent the hour on something unrelated to his return. For a few minutes the world had ceased being a problem to place within the history of who he had been.

When the hour ended, Maurice asked whether he wanted to extend it.

“I'll receive people again tomorrow. That's enough for today.”

He had not finished the duchy. He had begun a day without calling it a wait.

Mirel arrived for the discussion he had requested. She brought the updated clinical observations and the records she could show, without turning the visit into a test of his right to live.

The physical recovery was real. No new treatment was required to demonstrate every morning that the body would continue functioning.

“This doesn't explain everything that happened,” the doctor said.

“I know.”

“And I don't intend to invent an explanation so I can let you go.”

Alexander nodded. He did not hand her an ancient identity to reward her precision, nor ask her to erase what she had seen during the crisis.

The two inert Quiet Glass fragments remained in custody separate from the toxic residue. They were not presented as another chance to repeat the return.

When Mirel left, Teren entered with an update on access. The baron had not become a guest of the house again; proceedings continued outside the duke's personal guard. The head of security did not bring a judgment that did not exist.

“The restrictions on his contacts here remain,” Alexander said. “I don't want his son having to ask forgiveness to cross an entrance.”

Teren recorded the distinction and asked who could confirm a visit if he was unavailable. Security needed dull answers even after a miracle.

They agreed on the contact. No new guard was invented to cover every shift fear might have desired.

He remained alone for a while. The sun had moved far enough to leave the desk's edge in shadow. Edran looked at the place where he had met Ravelle, then lowered his eyes to his hands.

A glow sprang from his palm, warm in color and contained within the intensity he had given it. Nearby paper became readable without a flame consuming a wick. It was not something created with Cradle. It was light that he was emitting, the exercise of an ability he knew so well that he did not need to confuse it with the origin of every object that light happened to illuminate.

He moved his hand slightly. The inkwell's shadow shifted. The room revealed no secret merely by becoming brighter; wood remained wood, letters continued to say only what had been written.

Edran felt the joy that had surprised him before the cup of water. A body could contain immense power without being obliged to give it the scale of catastrophe every time. This small emission was obeying the very same authority that, in his memories, had operated on an immensely greater scale.

Once that light had reached faces asking whether it was finally possible to stand. Some had covered their eyes; others had wanted to keep looking. Edran could not remember them all at the same instant. The glow on his palm held steady while a face lost a name and a name returned without a face.

He wanted to call someone. Not to be worshipped. To hear a person laugh at his bringing so much power to a sheet he could not read properly by the window.

Moved by memory, he increased the light. The inkwell's shadow sharpened all the way to the table's edge. Edran followed it and lowered the intensity again. Warmth reached the paper in the measure he had chosen; the sheet remained a sheet. He could separate every exercise, recognize what he asked of Halo and what he would ask of Cradle, find in destruction the same precision with which he closed his fingers. None of those authorities had become merely an image to remember.

The First Testament returned precise knowledge of the Effigies, authorities he would know how to exercise and oppose. He lacked the days the world had lived after his death. A formula could explain a power; it could not tell him who had taken a loved person's place, which temple had been demolished, which name had survived by changing meaning. Through the duke's eyes he had found a calendar. To reach the others he would have to find a history. The light slipped across the one sheet offering him nothing of the kind: the morning's final piece of business.

He wondered who would still recognize the light.

The question left a cold deeper than the room. The duke's memories contained prayers, public names, people to whom the household would entrust an oath. Edran's place did not appear where he would have sought his past. He would have to seek it in the world, and the world might notice him searching.

Footsteps came from the corridor. He ended the emission before the handle moved. The sun reclaimed the sheet with its weaker light. Edran stayed a moment with his palm open: for the first time, the desire to be recognized also seemed something from which to protect the girl who might enter.

Later Celine found him tidying the table. She had received Etienne's reply and did not come to hand it over.

“He wrote,” she said simply.

“Good.”

The girl paused before her father's chair, which the young duke had continued using.

“Does being here still hurt?”

Edran looked at its back. Alexander's memories held Lucien's hand resting there, an explanation interrupted by a visitor, the desire to be considered old enough at last.

The pain was available with a precision that did not grant him the right to call it entirely his own.

“Sometimes.”

It was not the whole truth. It was not a performance invented so she could recognize the right answer.

“You can move it,” Celine said.

“I don't want to erase your father from the room.”

“Neither do I. But a chair isn't him.”

Alexander looked at her. He had spent lifetimes watching men confuse the symbol of a power with the person seated in it. The girl was not building a philosophy to help him: she wanted the house to stop making them ask the dead for permission for every change.

He moved the chair beside the bookcase and took one better suited to the table. No object was unmade.

Celine tested the distance between her own seat and the papers she had brought.

“I can see you better like that,” she said.

He sat. For a few minutes they worked without speaking.

Edran thought of the child who had learned to desire the end of every hand laid upon his life. Of the strength acquired afterward, the names time had consumed, the right he could have claimed to consider this house merely shelter.

The boy's grief had not killed him. Another man had made a choice. What remained did not now need to be preserved as a mistake committed by the victim.

Celine read a page and returned it to its pile. Edran looked at Lucien's chair beside the bookcase. It had not been banished behind furniture; he had not changed its wood to prevent its resemblance to the past.

“Do you want to leave it there?” she asked.

“For now, yes.”

“If we sit and read, it's nearer the books.”

She spoke with the practical attention that had made him move his own work in previous days. Grief did not vanish because they found a new use for a chair. But it might stop ordering every movement as though returning to the same position were fidelity.

Edran remembered his childhood hunger and how a sheltered place could become a desire so great it left room for nothing else. Afterwards he had obtained power and worship. Neither had automatically taught him to inhabit a house without making it depend on his wound.

She moved a foot beneath the table and struck his chair leg. She apologized quickly, surprised by the new distance. He said they could move it again if needed. Celine tried another position and returned to the sheet.

That small impact restored him to his present body more than all his reflection. He could stay close enough to someone to be accidentally disturbed. It was no threat to what he had been. He wanted to remain seated until she finished, and discovered he did not want to invent an engagement to excuse it.

“Will you have breakfast here tomorrow?” Celine asked.

Alexander looked up from the papers.

“If you want.”

“I'm asking you.”

He smiled.

“Then yes.”

Celine returned to arranging her sheet. She did not make him repeat the time to solemnise the promise. Edran stayed seated until she finished the line.

It was a promise he could keep without commanding anyone to believe it.
