The carter had brought his son instead of his brother.

Mael saw it from the landing before the overseer pointed out the difference. The boy held his cap in both hands and watched the load with the concentration that preceded a first question. The crates had to pass from one entrance to another without being overturned. It was not difficult work until someone tried doing it alone to prove he deserved a grown man's pay.

“How old?” Mael asked.

The father turned. He had a thin beard and a neck reddened by the strap. “He can carry.”

“That isn't what I asked.”

The man gave an age. The boy opened his mouth; his father laid a hand on his shoulder, and he closed it again.

It was June sixth. Low morning light entered through the gate, cut by the bars of its leaf. Mael descended two steps and indicated the load.

“That stays on the cart. He waits outside.”

“My brother has a fever. I have to finish the round.”

“Then you shouldn't have promised two men.”

The boy raised his cap as though to put it on and could not find the courage to finish. Mael recognized the movement and looked at the father. A man with someone to protect had a reason to respond better to the second proposal.

“You can use a warehouse porter,” he said. “His time comes off your round.”

The father looked at the overseer, then the cart. He accepted with a nod he tried to turn into thanks. Mael let him. He went upstairs while the boy was sent to wait on the side of the gate where he would not obstruct the work.

Sybille had entered the room without sitting. She had opened the window a little, and the air shifted the edge of the plan on the table. Ilian's bill beneath a paperweight did not move.

“You gave him expensive help,” she said.

Mael closed the door. “I gave him a man.”

“Taken out of his round.”

“The warehouse shouldn't pay because his brother is ill.”

Sybille watched him long enough to make clear she had heard the unspoken part too. The father would finish unloading; the difference between finding help and accepting its price would emerge at delivery. The boy outside would wait without knowing how much of his father's time would be charged against him.

Mael removed his gloves and put them beside the returned sample. The object remained in its fine cloth. He disliked looking at it in the morning: it held the weight of an answer others had formulated calmly. He covered it before sitting.

“Uren took the question.”

“Has he come back?”

“He left a note.”

Sybille approached. The note said the collar comparison could be presented as a separate assignment, and a technical answer had to come from whoever would do the work. It contained no offer from Ruy, appointment, or promise from Veyne. Mael remembered well how little space a refusal needed. This was not one yet.

“He spoke to the copyist,” Sybille said.

“Yes.”

“And brought both drafts.”

It was an observation, but Mael heard the judgment in her emphasis. He had not forbidden the draftsman to keep his papers. Forcing him to destroy them would turn a man returning for a balance into a man who wanted a witness. That difference interested him more than the satisfaction of seeing him obey.

Mael pushed the plan toward her. “Let's look at the entrance.”

The room chosen for the inspection had a wide door to the courtyard and a narrower side entrance. There was a bench, sufficient light, and room to set the collar without asking the technician to bend over a crate. The description need not be false. A place where the work could actually be done was more likely to receive a competent man than one built only to lure him.

Sybille followed the side passage with her nail.

“Does this door open?”

“Yes.”

“For whom?”

Mael took the key from the drawer and left it on the plan.

She did not touch it. “When a surveyor arrives, I don't want two porters between him and the street.”

“Why two?”

“Because three are noticed sooner.”

Mael's smile drew no answer. He had learned to distinguish her warnings from her challenges; sometimes Sybille granted him the courtesy of disguising one as the other. This morning she seemed unwilling.

“We aren't fixing a visit nobody has accepted,” he said. “I want to know how to describe the access.”

“Describe it as it will remain. A door, a street, the collar. If the question asks only for dimensions, the man must be able to give only dimensions.”

Mael rested his thumb where the plan showed the bench. He was interested in a Bearer's gesture at work, what he needed to approach, the kind of reference he chose. The other request had sought the final result too soon. An ordinary measurement could show him the person at the moment he stopped talking about his abilities and began using them.

Ilian did not need to know that.

Mael rose and opened the low cupboard. The collar they had commissioned was wrapped in coarse canvas, unlike the sample's fine cloth. He carried it to the table. Its halves opened around the pin; the inner edge had a dull stretch where the finishing had not been completed.

Sybille rested a finger on the edge and withdrew it. “That scratches.”

“It needs measuring, not stroking.”

“A man notices when he picks it up.”

Mael ran his thumb over the indicated stretch. The roughness was not enough to prevent the gesture, but left a pale mark on his skin. He had asked for an ordinary object, not a neglected one. He took the chalk and marked the point.

Then he closed the collar around the wooden cylinder used to support it. The halves would not meet without leaving a gap. Sybille put a hand beneath the component before it could slide off the edge.

“That's why we're seeking a comparison,” Mael said.

“That's why the man needs to open it without asking you for a key every time.”

He left the catch loose. She withdrew the cylinder and put the collar back on the canvas, open. It seemed smaller like that, without the closed form that made it resemble something ready for an arm.

Mael noticed where her gaze had stopped. He did not ask what she had imagined. Instead he indicated the chalk mark and told her to have the point finished before showing the object to anyone who needed to work with it. Sybille nodded slowly and wrapped the metal, leaving the catch accessible.

“Another surveyor,” he said. “If Ruy doesn't come, we find another surveyor.”

Sybille looked up. “For the collar?”

“For the collar.”

He added nothing. Leaving a question open was less tiring than preventing someone from formulating the answer they wanted. Sybille looked at the covered sample, then the key. Her hand paused at the plan's edge, close enough to take either object, and took neither.

A sharp knock rose from below. Something had struck a threshold, followed by a muffled curse. The overseer called a name. Not the boy's.

Mael opened the assignment ledger. Among deliveries of objects, some lines concerned people: an accompanied journey, two attendances for an evening, renewal of an availability. Each entry had hours, a place, and an amount. The descriptions made faces unnecessary until something happened that required one person to be distinguished from another.

Sybille moved to read the page without bending over him.

“Is this still open?”

She indicated an attendance recorded for three days. Mael knew whom she meant. A woman hired to accompany someone to a station had waited beyond the agreed time for the guarantor's return. It was distant enough not to bring him a new problem that morning; near enough to leave someone available to ask for news.

“The return confirmation is missing.”

“Did she return?”

“That isn't the question recorded.”

Sybille removed her finger from the line. It was a small movement, but Mael saw and kept it. He disliked her trying to rename things before an open book. She had done it in the past, then collected her share anyway. The memory of that choice should make judging his less comfortable.

“Someone else can provide confirmation,” she said.

“You can seek it, if the time is yours.”

She did not immediately answer. That stretch of silence was a cost, and Mael waited to see whether she would pay it. Sybille looked at the line, then Ilian's bill protruding beneath the paperweight.

“The inspection first,” she said.

Mael closed the ledger. He had not obtained complete obedience; the sequence would do.

The carter knocked shortly afterward. He held his cap like his son. A streak of blood on his thumb had been badly wiped against his trousers.

“The load is inside.”

Mael followed him to the stairs. Two crates had been placed beyond the mark on the floor. The overseer showed the point and waited. The father began saying the space had been occupied, saw Mael, and changed his sentence.

“We'll move them.”

“You will,” Mael confirmed.

It was brief, unpleasant work. The load had already found a balance and did not want to lose it; the father called the porter by name instead of ordering him. Mael saw the second man hesitate, then set his shoulder. When they finished, the passage was clear and nobody could maintain that the request had been unreasonable. The dust on the man's coat told another part of the operation.

The boy still waited outside. Seeing his father, he took a step toward him. The man showed his palm to stop him, not strike him. Then he turned to the overseer and asked how much of the round had been charged.

Mael left them to discuss the time. He returned upstairs quietly wanting a warm cup.

Sybille had taken the key.

“I'm going to look at the bench,” she said.

“Take someone capable of fixing it. If the collar is to be measured, it mustn't rock.”

“I know.”

She folded the plan without covering Ilian's note. Before leaving, she looked at it once more.

“He's owed the balance for the drawing he's already done.”

Mael did not rise. “And he'll keep asking for it.”

Sybille stayed in the doorway one breath too long. Then she descended. Her footsteps sounded ordinary and clear on the wood; the warehouse did not need to become silent for her to cross it.

Mael drew the new note toward him and marked the work to offer another surveyor if necessary. External dimensions, alignment, ordinary collar. The price would be discussed after he had seen the face of whoever could do it.

Below the window, the cart moved off. The father had not put his son on the load; the boy walked beside him, his cap finally on his head. The gate stayed open long enough to let them both out, then the overseer brought it against its stop.

Mael took Ilian's bill and put it back on top of the workshop's answer, where it would be impossible to miss at the next visit.
