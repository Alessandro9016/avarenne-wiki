Yselle’s letter did not ask for money.

Alexander turned it over, checking for a second sheet. Standing before the study, Maurice followed the movement without offering an explanation.

The invitation was for the fifth of June, in Lethorne. His aunt wanted to show him the work and ask what he thought of it. She had written after the technicians’ visit the previous day; the delivery had arrived that morning.

A water stain marked the final line without dissolving the name.

In the duke’s memories, Yselle often wrote with something running or open beside her elbow. Alexander remembered a letter his mother had insisted he answer with the same care he would give a formal invitation. It had cost him an afternoon; his aunt had then replied asking whether something had happened to his hand.

He had been twelve. He had been deeply offended.

Edran smiled at the sheet before realizing he had done so.

“Shall I confirm?” Maurice asked.

“The fifth. Yes.”

The butler looked at the calendar open on the table.

“You have two appointments already arranged.”

Alexander followed his finger. One could be moved to the previous afternoon. For the other he would have to offer a different date. He thought how easy it would be to get up and leave someone else the work of making an absence reasonable.

“Don’t cancel that one. Ask whether they can come the day before. For the second, suggest a date after my return.”

“When do you expect to return?”

The question stopped him. He had decided to travel for a visit without yet telling his aunt what he meant to visit.

He reread the invitation. Pumps, drawings, components to examine. No mention of the length of a stay. Yselle had not asked him to spend the rest of his life in the workshop in order to answer a question.

“Have the accommodation and length of the visit confirmed. For now, don’t promise a return on the same day.”

Maurice made a note and left the study. Edran remained with his pen poised above the reply.

He could have reached many things without a carriage. Not everything he remembered knowing how to do answered him in his present body, and he did not intend to mistake a wish for an exercise he possessed. Twenty-three kilometers were short enough to be a journey and real enough to take a morning.

Teren arrived before he had finished the first sentence.

“Maurice told me Lethorne.”

“Unless he added a farther destination between the corridor and the stairs.”

The head of security looked at the sheet. He did not smile, but the duke’s eyes remembered the face well enough to notice the brief movement at the corners of his mouth.

“The ordinary road?”

Alexander said yes.

Teren asked whether he wanted to stop elsewhere, who would receive news of the journey and whether the visit involved a place open to a large public. There was no answer to the last question. Edran picked up the letter and realized Yselle had described her work as she would to a nephew, not to a man who traveled carrying other people’s eyes with him.

“I’ll ask. I’d rather see the workshop while it works.”

“Then I need to know where we can wait without standing between people and machines.”

Teren described the needs of the carriage and the two guards he would assign to the journey. He would come too. The other shifts would not fill themselves because the duke was leaving the residence.

Edran asked only that they not send anyone running ahead to order the city to stop moving.

This time Teren really smiled.

“That isn’t how one arrives at a workshop.”

“I’m pleased to hear it.”

When the officer left, Alexander had a slightly longer reply that was finally useful to send. He accepted the fifth, asked where he could conveniently stay and explained who would come with him. The question about the project remained short.

He wanted to see it before talking about help.

He had not sealed the letter when Celine knocked twice in quick succession.

He recognized the sound from Alexander’s memories: a question Celine preferred asking before she risked changing her mind.

“Aunt Yselle wrote?”

Edran showed her the envelope.

Celine came close enough to read the addressee’s name, then looked back at him.

“For me too?”

“She wants me to see the pumps.”

“That isn’t what I asked.”

Alexander lowered the sheet. She held a book against her chest, a bent finger pressed between its pages as though a bookmark were not enough to guarantee the place.

“This one is for me. I don’t know whether another arrived.”

“I asked Maurice. He was still looking.”

Celine looked at the paper on the desk without reaching for it. He offered it so she could read the invitation; she took only the less stained edge.

“On the fifth I’ve already told Sabine I want to go to the library.”

“It isn’t an order to come with me.”

“I know. I wanted to know whether you’d already decided to tell me I could choose.”

Edran accepted the small defeat with a nod. She handed back the letter, pleased to have found the point at which his kindness became predictable.

“I’m sorry I still haven’t come with you.”

“It isn’t the same outing I asked for before. The books change.”

He looked at the volume against her chest.

“Do you like that one?”

Celine hesitated, her expression once more that of someone with a second question.

She opened the page she had kept with her finger. A drawing showed a lever, two weights and an explanation taking up almost the whole margin. One word had been underlined.

“Aunt explained it. I remember the result, not how.”

Alexander recognized the subject. The duke’s memories gave him a day spent looking out of a window while Martin made a similar drawing. Edran, however, could see the relationship between force and distance clearly. What he did not remember was which part the girl had already been taught.

“Show me where you stopped.”

Celine pointed to the underlined word. The sentence asked her to change a point on the figure, but she had moved the weight along with the support. That made the arrows in the next step seem to contradict one another.

“You don’t move that as well,” he said.

“But if the point goes here…”

She came to the desk and showed him the diagram with a fingertip. Alexander took a free sheet and copied only the line of the lever.

“What we move is the support. The weight stays where it is.”

Celine stared at it.

He took the paper knife, rested it on the pencil and set a securely closed inkwell at one end. The metal tilted. He held it still with two fingers before the ink could become an altogether different demonstration.

“Here I can press without coming closer to the weight.”

“If you break the paper knife, Maurice will say you shouldn’t have rested like this.”

“Then let’s try to finish before he arrives.”

She put down the open book. Alexander replaced the inkwell with a small paperweight and had her press the free end. Celine felt the weight lift. Then he moved the pencil without moving the paperweight.

“Now try.”

She pressed again. The metal went down less, and the weight tried to slide toward the middle. Edran stopped it with a finger.

“That wasn’t in the drawing,” Celine said.

“In the drawing no one has to chase it across the floor. But this is the change you need to look at.”

He had her repeat the pressure before and after. She stopped staring at the diagram and looked at the pencil. When she returned to the page, she followed the arrow with her finger to the new support point.

“I moved two things.”

“Yes.”

“Martin would have said that before the paper knife.”

“Martin knows how to teach. I’ve managed to make you dirty my desk.”

Celine looked at the little graphite mark the pencil had left. She took the cloth from the edge of the desk and began wiping it, then stopped.

“What if I put it even closer?”

Edran let her try. This time the paperweight really slid; they both caught it, one hand on the other. She burst out laughing before setting it still again.

He realized he had remembered the way Celine laughed, not the actual weight of her hand. He left her the paper knife and returned to the book. The next line concerned exactly what they had just seen.

When Maurice returned with the second envelope, he found them bent over the same page.

Celine looked up at once.

It was the reply she had been waiting for. She recognized the paper, not the name. She took it and moved away from the desk to read; Edran remained with his half of the reasoning and a book his sister seemed to have forgotten she wanted back.

“I’ll bring it later,” she said, already at the door.

“The book or the solution?”

“Whichever comes first.”

Maurice waited until her steps had faded before asking for the duke’s reply. Alexander signed it, checked the address and handed it over.

Later, Albin brought the matters needing a decision before his departure. They laid them out without burying Celine’s drawing. One issue could be dealt with that morning; another needed clarification that had not arrived.

“They won’t wait for your return to seek it,” the administrator said.

“Good. I couldn’t invent it from a carriage window.”

Albin made no comment. He indicated the person who would receive the answer and left the duke only the part of the decision that remained his.

Edran signed the last instructions and handed them to Albin. The administrator gathered up the papers; the book and the pencil mark remained on the table. He liked how little the departure resembled an escape.

When he was alone, he picked up his sister’s volume again.

Beneath the lever was the name of a man who had written three generations before Alexander’s birth. The note called him an ancient author. Edran ran his thumb beside the letters and went still.

Ancient.

In the duke’s life, that word had a precise place: books, coins, stories given a particular tone of voice. In his own there were people who had already forgotten more generations than that name had passed through. He tried setting the two times beside each other and found no reference that truly belonged to both.

Yselle was alive. He could hear it in the impatient handwriting, the stain at the edge, the choice to invite a nephew without first telling him how moved he was supposed to be.

The people he wanted to seek in the world had not sent him a letter.

He closed the book. A silence so long could not yield a conclusion: perhaps they did not know where to find him, perhaps they no longer existed, perhaps their silence had a reason he would hate discovering.

The thought left a cold, lucid anger, at last without a household object on which to spend it.

He rose and went to the window. In the courtyard a man carried a bundle of boards, followed by someone showing him where to put them. The carriage was not yet ready for him, and none of the people below had made him the center of their afternoon.

He would go to Lethorne on the fifth. He would see his aunt’s work, speak with Martin and return Celine’s book with an answer he could defend.

Then he would find something to read that did not call a man born only yesterday ancient.
