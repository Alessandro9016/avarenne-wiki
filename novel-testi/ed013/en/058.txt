Martin had burned a slice of bread and was scraping it over the sink when Alexander came into the kitchen.

“Don't tell my wife.”

“I'm here,” Yselle said from the table.

“Then don't tell the duke.”

Alexander looked at the black rectangle and the knife that was now removing nothing but crumbs. “I think I arrived too late.”

Martin inspected the result, turned it over, and decided to serve the other side. The kitchen window stood open onto the yard; outside, someone was trying to bring in a cat that seemed to have other intentions. The call came three times, growing less polite. Yselle listened to the last threat and shook her head without stopping her work with the plates.

“That one only understands the cupboard closing.”

Martin went to close the cupboard. The cat appeared on the windowsill.

Alexander laughed. He had left his jacket in the guest room; when he bent his arms to wash, the clean cuff had seemed an unjustified novelty after the Mutual's water. Now he sat where his aunt indicated and removed one of Martin's notebooks from the next chair.

“Don't open it,” the teacher said. “Terrible things in there.”

“Bad calculations?”

“Handwriting.”

Yselle took the notebook and set it on an already tall pile. “Is that how you say you've forgotten to mark them?”

“No. It's how I ask for another evening.”

Dinner began before Martin received an answer. A bowl passed from hand to hand; Alexander broke his bread on the unburned side. It was easy to remember how the duke had sat in this house as a boy, trying to remain composed enough not to be teased and casual enough to seem not to care. The memory offered him a precise place. It did not dictate what he should do with it.

“Has Teren eaten?” he asked.

“Before you,” Yselle replied. “He asked to know when you were going out tomorrow morning.”

“For the test.”

“I told him. He didn't ask whether I could guarantee we'd stay dry this time.”

Martin lowered his spoon. “A polite man.”

Alexander had watched the security chief check the passage, count those coming out, pick up a broom. Now imagining him at dinner, free for a little while at least from the need to watch the door, made him want not to summon him for a question that could wait.

Yselle served herself last. She ate two mouthfuls, then reached toward the table by the window, where a folder lay.

Martin pushed the breadbasket into her path.

She looked at him. “I wasn't reaching for that.”

“I know.”

“I need to remember something.”

“Write it on the back of my terrible handwriting and keep eating.”

Yselle obeyed with an ostentatious sigh. She wrote a few words on the sheet her husband passed her, then returned to her plate. Alexander had known families where such a gesture would have opened a quarrel about authority. Here Martin had merely saved dinner from the workshop's return.

“Earlier you asked whether the pipe was repaired,” she told her husband. “Not whether he knew how to repair it.”

“Because the first question had a useful answer.”

“And the second?”

Martin looked at Alexander. “I saw three machines yesterday. Today I watched you come back with a repaired pipe and a story Noemi wouldn't have believed if she'd heard it from me. The second question is getting larger.”

Alexander put down his bread. “Which part would you like to ask?”

The teacher considered. “The part about lunch tomorrow. Will I still have to make it?”

Yselle laughed, then nudged him with her elbow. The question had left Alexander room not to answer everything. He recognized that and felt affection for the man with an ease that did not come only from inherited memories.

“I'm afraid so,” he said.

“Then we're safe. I still have an exclusive skill.”

“Burning bread,” Yselle said.

Martin broke his own slice with great dignity. Alexander found himself watching his hands: ordinary hands, without secret inscriptions to assign to someone who knew how to teach a machine. The marriage before him needed no hidden force to hold together. It was enough for one to remind the other to eat, and for her, after protesting, actually to eat.

Hooves sounded in the yard. The open window carried the voices of two men arguing over a delivery. One insisted he would not return until he received the payment owed. The other told him that in that case he would have to wait outside the office, and he replied that he would wait sitting down.

There were no chains in that quarrel. Nobody claimed the other's body because he had dared refuse.

Edran went still with a piece of bread between his fingers.

He had wished for that world before he knew what shape a free street might take. He had known the order that made a person property and the terror that taught people to ask permission even to breathe. He did not need to invent a face from the past to recognize what was missing in the yard. A threat was missing, one that had once been so normal it needed no words.

The men kept arguing over payment. They did not become a monument to anyone's victory. That was precisely why Alexander felt his throat tighten.

Yselle touched his forearm. “You don't have to eat that part if it's hard.”

He looked down at the bread. “It isn't the bread.”

She waited. Martin let go of a joke he had been about to make.

“I was listening to the men outside,” Alexander said. “One of them can refuse to return.”

Yselle glanced toward the window. “He can. If they want him back, they'll have to pay him.”

“Yes.”

He could not offer her the weight of that word without opening everything he had kept hidden. Neither did he want to reduce it to a convalescent's whim. He drank some water and found a truth small enough to fit in that kitchen.

“I'm glad he can say it.”

Martin nodded. “Even when he says it about a delivery we're waiting for.”

Yselle shot him a look. “Was it ours?”

“I don't think so. But we ought to remain consistent if it was.”

His aunt threw back her head and laughed. The pressure in Alexander's throat eased. Outside, the cart moved; the man who wanted to wait sitting down had probably found a chair. Nobody in the house knew that a remote part of his life had just found a result still at work.

After dinner they carried the plates to the sink together. Martin handled the water and Yselle dried without letting him choose the order. Alexander began gathering the cutlery; his aunt tried to take it from him, then saw his expression and left him the task.

“You made more noise as a child,” she observed.

“With the cutlery?”

“About the injustice of having to carry it.”

He remembered. The adolescent duke had believed he could be both guest and innocent. “You hadn't argued my case well enough.”

“Your mother argued it. She decided against you.”

The unspoken name occupied a place on the edge of the sink. Alexander set the cutlery down gently. Yselle noticed, but did not withdraw the memory. Instead she told him how his mother had insisted on washing one cup and then broken a much prettier one while trying not to stain a sleeve.

Only half that scene was in the young man's memories: he had already gone out, offended, before the noise. Now the rest arrived. Yselle described Martin, just home, asking whether the cup had fallen because of a problem with noble rank. Alexander's mother had thrown the cloth at him.

He laughed and missed someone without having to distinguish how much of that loss had begun then and how much had grown within him.

When the sink was empty, Yselle asked whether he would write to Celine. She had put paper and ink on the little table in the guest room.

“I promised her news.”

“Tell her I'll write again before you leave too. She asked questions that deserve more than an answer in a margin.”

“About the workshop?”

“And what I'd put in a garden if I were forced to design one.”

“A pump.”

“An entrance wide enough to bring it in.”

Alexander carried the laughter back to his room. Before sitting, he checked the calendar: June sixth. He would leave Lethorne on the eighth, after Yselle had time to face the deadline with the new state of things. Twenty-three kilometers were not a distance to steal from the road; they would still take the carriage, Teren, the men, and several hours. He wrote Celine the day of his return.

He did not turn the failure into news without danger. He said an old section had given way during the test and been repaired; nobody had been hurt. He stopped, crossed out the last sentence, and rewrote it: Noemi had suffered a cut, now healed. Celine would recognize all too well a reassurance meant to end questions.

He told her about the cat, the bread Martin had rescued, and a machine that in the morning should send water where it had previously arrived with difficulty. He promised to bring her aunt's letter. He did not promise a workshop visit without discussing it with her and Yselle.

The pen hovered above his signature. Alexander thought of Elias, the way he had held the caliper after the creation, the need not always to be in the wrong moment. Around him were people capable of telling him what a pipe lacked. He wanted to know someone who would ask him for something to learn and offer him something he did not already possess.

Not a devotee waiting for a miracle. Someone in conversation who could surprise him.

Downstairs a chair scraped in the kitchen. Martin asked whether the folder could at least leave the dry half of the table free. Yselle answered that the dry half was precisely why she had put it there. The discussion resumed, low and domestic, accompanied by the rustle of pages.

A little later Martin knocked with a candle and an envelope. The door was open, but he waited for Alexander to tell him to come in anyway.

“For the morning post. Yselle said otherwise you'll use one of her large envelopes and Celine will think she's received a design.”

Alexander showed him the single sheet. “Not this time.”

Martin set down the envelope. A line of ink remained on his index finger that washing dishes had not removed.

“Do you still have notebooks?”

“Enough to hope someone invents new handwriting tomorrow. I've seen this sort already.”

“May I see one of the exercises?”

Martin hesitated, surprised by the question. Then he returned to his own room and came back with a sheet whose name he had covered. Alexander followed the description of a lever and pointed to an answer that seemed to contradict the diagram. The teacher laughed: the answer was right; it was the drawing that had an unclear point. He shifted the candle and showed him the line concealed in the fold.

“Tomorrow I'll have to correct my question before I correct them.”

Alexander returned the sheet. That small defeat of a teacher had been offered without shame and without asking anything in return. It kept him better company than the thought of all the answers he could have supplied alone.

Alexander signed the letter. He left the window ajar and listened a little longer to what stayed lit in the house.
