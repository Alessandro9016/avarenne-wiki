Livia had written a clear explanation, and Edith had circled the word at which she stopped understanding anything.

It was not even a difficult word. Reference. Livia used it in documents, notes, and sentences explaining why a comparison was useful. In the notebook she had written: compare against the reference. Edith had drawn a circle and, beneath it, a question: Which one?

The irritating thing was that the explanation was hers, not some distracted clerk's work she could complain about. The previous evening she had promised Edith a page about the most common abbreviations in cross-references. She wanted her to read three lines of their work without having to ask her every time. Now three of her lines produced a new question.

She had finished the morning's first copies, and the work address was still quiet enough for her to open the notebook. Outside, two men argued about who had left a parcel near the step. Neither thought it necessary to lower his voice out of respect for the clarity of her prose.

Edith arrived with the original page, a small parcel, and a book beneath her arm. She put down only the book. “I haven't crossed anything out.”

“I saw. You chose a color you can see from a street away.”

“The other one ran out.”

Livia took the pencil and wrote the document's name beside the circle. Edith stopped her with a finger.

“You know it this time. What if I get a different sheet?”

Livia searched her own note for the answer, as though the circle might have concealed a line already there. Then she turned the page. She had constructed an explanation that worked only for the example she had been thinking about. Beneath the heading Abbreviations she added Where to find the title in full.

Edith sat without asking whether it was a bad question. She showed her a cross-reference in the book she had brought: two letters and a number. Livia immediately said the second letter indicated a volume. When they opened the page referred to, they discovered it meant a section.

“Don't write that down,” Livia said.

“I haven't got the pencil yet.”

“Worse. You'll remember.”

She laughed despite herself and took the book. She could have preserved the shape of those letters exactly, but that would not oblige the author to use them as she did. The clarification was in a note at the beginning, far from the point where they had stopped. They read it and changed the example. Livia wrote a line admitting two possible meanings instead of choosing the more usual one.

Edith offered no money for the correction. Beside Livia's page she placed a sheet containing the words that had tripped her up and a rewritten sentence for each. In exchange she asked to keep the corrected copy of the abbreviations. Livia accepted. An Effigy had not written the bad page in her place, and it would not prevent an ordinary reader from improving it.

“Shall we let Elias read this too?” Edith asked.

“Especially him. He might think a reference is a place he can reach with his hands.”

Edith smiled, then at last pushed the parcel between them. Inside were two shapes of dark fabric, cut to cover the upper face, with ribbons not yet attached. Livia lifted them by a corner.

“Are they for a play?”

“They were for a play. These are copies of the pattern. I wanted to find out how uncomfortable they were before making something serious of it.”

They were cloth, thread, and a proposal. Edith held one before her eyes without tying it. The edge touched her lashes; she had to lower it, leaving her nose half covered.

“Very,” Livia concluded.

“What if I cut more here?”

She placed the fabric in Livia's hand. Livia tried it. The world shrank to two openings that did not quite match the distance between her eyes. She could see Edith or the edge of the table, but passing from one to the other required moving her head. She did, and the loose ribbon slipped across her cheek.

Edith opened the book before her and asked her to read a line. Livia began in her own voice, stopped at a word hidden by the edge, and turned her head to find its ending. The fabric pressed against her cheek. She might learn to hold the book higher, but during a conversation someone could set a page in a different place.

“The margin won't fit,” she said.

“The margin fits. You're looking through the wrong hole.”

“If I enlarge one, you can see half my face.”

Edith held one of the shapes to the light and turned it. The pattern had been copied for an actor looking toward an audience, not for people sitting around a table. Livia indicated the seam point that could pull toward the eye. They marked it with chalk without pretending they had found protection. The face might become less readable; the voice that had just complained about the margin was the same one Livia had used to collect payment for a copy the day before. Edith recognized it by imitating her sentence, and Livia surprised herself by laughing while she still held the cloth over her nose.

Someone knocked on the doorframe from the corridor. Livia removed the shape too quickly and shoved it beneath the notebook. It was only someone coming to collect the finished copies. They checked the number of sheets, asked the price already agreed, and went away without noticing the small drama they had missed.

Edith watched the fabric protruding from the cover. “Very discreet.”

“I didn't say it was a good idea.”

“I didn't say it had worked.”

Livia pulled it out. Her heart was beating harder over a mask to be sewn than over a letter sent to a duke. She waited for it to settle. She laid the cloth flat without pretending nobody could recognize her hand as she arranged it.

“He'll have our names,” she said.

“That doesn't mean everyone has to.”

“No. But it means we can't present ourselves as three people he's never seen and expect him not to ask questions.”

Edith nodded. She had already marked the point to enlarge without cutting it. If they used coverings in a future conversation, they needed to be able to look at one another and read a page, not merely make an impression entering. They had not even agreed on a place yet. Livia preferred not to spend the morning dressing a meeting nobody had accepted.

“Let's wait for the answer before sewing three,” she said.

“That's why there are two.”

The count silenced her. Edith had not asked her to guess her problem: she had set it on the table, where Livia could see it without immediately managing to give it the right name.

Elias arrived late in the morning, his folder emptier than when he had passed the previous day. Livia asked whether he had lost a job. He answered that he had handed one over and set his hand on the table as though to show he brought no parts to repair.

“The Mutual received the sheets. They're keeping my final copy at the workshop.”

“And our letter?”

“I don't have an answer yet. I'll stop at Yselle's when she's finished with the handover copies.”

The dark shape caught his eye. “What's that?”

“A proposal that doesn't see very well yet,” Edith said.

Elias lifted it. He held it too high, then too low. Livia realized he was trying to solve the problem of the cloth because it was easier than the problem the cloth was supposed to solve. She took the ribbon from his fingers before he could try knotting it behind his head.

“If we choose coverings, we choose them for a precise reason. Not to pretend the letter wasn't signed.”

“We can ask when he replies,” he said. “Tell him we'd rather avoid other names in front of outsiders.”

“We've already given him ours.”

“We can't take those back. The others aren't in the letter.”

Livia lowered her eyes to her notebook. Footnote did not appear on the cover; she had left it inside, beside notes about the exchange. Northbound belonged to Elias. Neither could become a second face capable of erasing the rest of a life. They were names chosen for a use, and they had already begun to seem firmer than they were.

Edith smoothed the second shape. “Don't choose one for me while you discuss the other two.”

“We weren't doing that,” Elias said.

Livia touched his arm before he added an explanation. Edith had not accused anyone of something already done. She had said where she did not want to end up.

“You don't have one yet,” Livia said. “It can stay that way.”

“I'd like it to remain something I choose. Not the empty place to fill so the count comes out right.”

Elias put down the shape. “All right.”

He did not ask for a date by which she should think about it. Livia laid the two coverings together and returned them to the parcel; for now there was no third thing to add. Edith's name was already beneath the letter, beside theirs. For the duke, nobody had signed from a more secret position than anyone else.

Something less conspicuous remained to prepare. Livia reopened the notebook and asked what questions they would want to address if a yes arrived. Elias began with the limits of creation, then stopped to look at Edith. She did not lend him a question to complete the round.

“I said mine yesterday. But I don't want him answering before he knows what I'm trying to learn.”

She showed him the book with the mistaken cross-reference and the page corrected with Livia. Elias read the explanation and found a sentence clear that Edith had circled.

“You already knew the document,” she observed.

He went back, searched for the title, and found the added note. Livia saw comprehension change the way he held the sheet. She did not need to make them argue to prove the corrected note had value. She let them finish comparing and stood to close the window against the noise of a wagon.

When she returned, Elias had added an arrow at the useful point and Edith was copying the line. They passed the pencil without counting the favor. Livia thought she would rather show the duke that table at that precise moment than a row of successful masks. It was a preference, not a reason to give him an address they had not decided to share.

“Tonight we'll meet at Room Six,” she said. “After work. If the answer arrives, we'll read it together.”

“And if it doesn't?” Elias asked.

Edith pointed to the notebook. “We'll make you read the whole reference.”

Livia laughed. They agreed to use the ordinary entrance and wait for no one who had not been invited. Three people that evening, whatever the answer. The future meeting with Alexander, if it happened, would have a different venue and terms to discuss. Nobody was holding open a door for him across the city.

Elias picked up his folder. Edith collected the book but left Livia her corrections. For a moment Livia tried to return the sheet out of habit, then remembered the exchange: that page was hers now, and the copy of the abbreviations belonged to the other woman.

Left alone, she checked the afternoon's copies and moved the parcel of shapes into a drawer. The notebook did not promise that nobody would discover who she was. It contained a circled mistake, a correction, and two working names that did not exhaust the people. She left the next line free without writing the third.
