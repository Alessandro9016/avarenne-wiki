Chapter 45 — Six Crowns to Earn

Marcel read the dimension a second time and turned the drawing to see whether the figure appeared on the other side too.

Elias pointed to the section. The line ended in the right place, before the gasket seat. He had gone to sleep afraid he had confused that stretch with the usable opening; now he seemed to see his mistake before the master even spoke.

“It's right,” Marcel said.

“I checked again.”

“I noticed. You've written it three times.”

Elias took his finger off the sheet. It was June fourth; the dimensions had to leave that morning. On the drawing, the enlarged detail of the catch occupied the space he had originally reserved for his signature. He would have to sign farther down. He did not mind.

Marcel put the instruction copy beside the drawing and slid a ruler along the edge to hold the fold open. The small picture of the notch had survived copying better than the words around it.

“Did Martin ask for this?”

“He tried pulling without seeing it.”

“How hard?”

“Hard enough to make us change the sheet.”

The master smiled. Then he separated the documents for delivery from those that would remain at the workshop. Elias saw the record of his assignment appear and straightened involuntarily.

“Six additional crowns,” Marcel said. “For your contribution and the assistance we're agreeing on. At the end of the assignment.”

The figure remained between them for a moment. Elias had thought about payment several times, and never found a way to ask without seeming to have moved on before finishing the work.

“For tomorrow too?”

“Tomorrow too. And the work expected afterward. If that changes, we'll discuss it.”

“If it takes more time?”

Marcel looked up. “Before you give it away.”

Elias studied the record. His name was not followed by a new qualification. There was the task, the days still to be agreed, and the figure listed separately from his wages. Six crowns did not sound like coins in his palm, but for the first time they let him imagine the end of this work without filling it entirely with gratitude.

“All right,” he said.

Marcel passed him the pen. Elias confirmed the part concerning him and let the ink dry. Then they wrapped the drawing in a rigid cover. The reinforced fold must not crush the corner of the instructions. The master checked the address; Elias checked the list of sheets.

Before the delivery, Marcel had him check the drawing in its wrapping. One edge rubbed against the rigid fastening. Elias slipped a scrap beneath the corner and pulled gently: the paper slid without creasing. He loosened the tie, then retied it firmly enough to keep the package closed. The master lifted the parcel and handed it to him. Now it could be carried without keeping two fingers against the fragile point.

The person who came to collect them did not need to hear how much work had gone into putting that notch in the right place.

Once the documents had gone, the bench seemed too large.

Elias still had to clean the caliper and check the points. He opened his toolbox. One empty slot immediately made him afraid he had left a tool at Yselle’s workshop; instead he found it beneath the cloth, wrapped to keep it from marking the wood. He had done that himself. He returned it to where he was used to looking.

“The duke will be there tomorrow,” he said.

“I know.”

“Yselle wants us there too.”

“So we'll go.”

Marcel continued counting the parts of a small regulator. Elias wanted a longer sentence, perhaps a story about an important customer in whose presence the master had spilled oil. Instead Marcel found a spring caught in the fabric and showed him the solution to the problem that interested him.

“Should I call him Your Grace?”

The master raised his head. “When you address him, yes. If he asks for a figure, give him the figure.”

“And if I don't know?”

“You call me. That's why I'm coming.”

The answer was too practical to allow Elias to continue being elegantly afraid. He finished cleaning the caliper.

Livia arrived during the break, a parcel beneath her arm and a printed sheet poking from her pocket. There was an ink stain between her thumb and forefinger; Elias recognized the kind that resisted the first washing and worsened at the second.

“Edith says this has to stop drinking,” she announced, setting the parcel on the clear bench.

Inside was the nurse's notebook, carefully protected enough to survive the journey. One corner of the cover bore an old damp mark. Livia opened it only far enough to show the binding, keeping the written pages closed.

“The wrapping slips. When she tries to pull it out with one hand, the wrapping comes away and the notebook stays behind.”

“A nurse ought to have at least four hands.”

“One of them for hitting whoever suggests that.”

Elias found a scrap of fabric among the instrument covers. He spread it beside the notebook. It was not large enough, but it could help test the fold. Livia already had two samples tucked into the parcel. One was thin and flexible; the other stiffer, with an edge that tended to scrape the back of the hand.

She tried folding the first. “This withstands water better than it withstands Edith closing it in a hurry.”

Elias took a piece of cardboard of the right size and passed it inside the fold. “It mustn't grip here. If it catches the cover's edge, it takes the whole thing away with it.”

Livia lifted the notebook by the wrong point. The wrapping stayed on the bench, and she showed him the result with satisfaction.

“You've built a different way to fail.”

“It's a prototype.”

“Oh. Then we can blame the materials.”

He laughed and shortened the flap. They tested it without opening the pages, using the cardboard for movements that might crease them. By the third fold, the spine could slide while the edge stayed caught. Livia tested it with her left hand, then her right. She chose softer reinforcement where Edith would insert her thumb and marked it with chalk.

“She can keep this even while I'm asleep,” she said.

Elias looked at the stain on her hand. “Are you tired?”

“I still have copies to finish.”

She did not say it as an invitation to investigate. She put the notebook away and kept the folded cardboard, which she would show Edith before having the good cover cut. Elias told her about the test with Martin, the liner finally coming out, and the visit planned for the next day. He left out Yselle's financial papers: he remembered the list, but had no story to hand around with lunch.

At the duke's name, Livia stopped tying the knot.

“Alexander Valcienne?”

“Do you know many other dukes who are her nephews?”

She hit his arm with the cardboard. “You start being funny when you're frightened.”

“Then tomorrow they'll want to keep me until evening.”

Livia tightened the knot and placed the parcel away from the cups. “Is he the one who asked you to do the work?”

“No. Marcel was commissioned. Yselle wants to show him the prototype.”

“So he has something to look at.”

Elias thought of the machine beneath the windows. As long as he had imagined it in front of a duke, he had forgotten that the prototype would not change shape when the carriage arrived. The liner had to come out the same way; the distances would not bow.

Livia took out the printed sheet. It was a new installment of the novel she had begun. The edge was badly trimmed and the title crossed a printing stain, but the corridor illustration occupied almost half the page.

“Don't look at the wall,” she said.

Elias looked at it.

“I haven't opened my mouth.”

“You made the face.”

In the picture, a man vanished into a passage between two rooms. The caption promised an impossible return. Elias held the sheet by its margin and searched his memory for the previous installment's drawing. “You said the room faced the street.”

“The room does face the street.”

“Then behind that wall is the street.”

Livia took the page away. “And he'll cross it very discreetly.”

At the nearby bench, Marcel made a sound that might have been a cough. Elias looked at him. The master was bent over the regulator with unconvincing concentration.

“It could be an alcove,” Elias tried.

“For a man two fingers wide?”

“We don't know how wide he is yet.”

Livia gave in to laughter. To avoid upsetting her cup she had to put the sheet on her knees. Elias felt a knot he had carried since morning loosen; the duke remained where he was, a day away, while Elias tried defending a passage that did not belong to him.

They read the first exchange in low voices. Livia refused to give him the role of the character who explained everything at the end; Elias chose the one presumed dead, because he would not have to speak for two paragraphs. They stopped before the reversal, when she checked the clock and put the installment back in her pocket. She would not tell him how it ended even when he asked whether the wall would be acquitted.

After she left, Elias prepared the tools for the visit. He left the things needed for ordinary work at the workshop and packed the caliper, suitable points, his notebook, and a spare pencil. He tested the clasp twice. It made the same small, reliable sound.

At home that evening, Lise had occupied the center of the table. She was working on a page of her own and raised a hand when he brought his toolbox near the edge.

“The chair.”

Elias put it on the chair. Adrien was reassembling something beneath the lamp; a small tray kept the screws apart. The place where Elias usually dropped his gloves was already full.

“Marcel has agreed on six extra crowns for this assignment,” he told his father.

Adrien looked up. “When?”

“At the end.”

“And what's left?”

“The tests. The assistance. It depends how tomorrow goes.”

His father nodded and asked about the liner. Elias described the catch hidden in the drawing and imitated Martin accusing Yselle of distributing extra catches as prizes. Lise laughed without leaving her work. He wanted to ask about her interview, but nothing on the table announced its outcome, and instead she began asking what the duke would see.

He described the pump and the Mutual's tank. His sister listened most closely to the maintenance. When he said you only had to pull out the liner, she looked over her page.

“Only?”

Elias sighed. “There's a new sheet explaining how much only.”

After dinner he cleaned his jacket with a cloth. Lise showed him a dry stain on the cuff that he would not have noticed until greeting someone. The glue came away in flakes; beneath it the fabric was a little paler. He did not own a jacket capable of looking new overnight.

In his room he reopened the caliper case and cleaned the groove where dust had gathered beneath the catch. He closed it and returned it to the toolbox prepared for morning. He brought out his jacket, hanging it so the cuff could finish drying.

From the doorway, Lise asked whether a duke knew how to recognize good work.

“I'll tell you tomorrow.”

“Tell him stains don't count.”

Elias turned toward the cuff. His sister had already gone. He arranged the cloth on the chair back beside his jacket and realized that, for the first time, he wanted tomorrow to arrive at least as much as he feared it.
